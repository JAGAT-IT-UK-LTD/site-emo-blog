1. What is a UGC creator, and do I need followers?

UGC stands for user generated content. Brands want ads that look like a real person talking about a product on their phone, not a polished studio advert. A UGC creator makes those videos.

The key difference from influencing is who posts the video. An influencer posts to their own audience. A UGC creator usually hands the file to the brand, and the brand runs it as an ad or on its own page.

That is why followers matter less. Billo, a UGC marketplace, says plainly that "no professional experience or social media presence is required". It accepts creators living in the United States, Canada or the United Kingdom, aged 18 or over.

TikTok's own marketplace is different. TikTok One, home of its Creator Marketplace, needs you to be 18 or over and says brand projects need "at least 1,000+ followers". So the TikTok route suits people who already post.

Billo says every task guarantees at least one video's payout plus the product itself, if there is one. UK creators are paid through Stripe. Once a video is approved, the money reaches your Stripe balance within 3 days, and Stripe sends it to your bank every Monday.

TikTok One works differently. Its creator programme terms say payouts go through Stripe. Base pay is due when your content is approved, and any revenue share when it runs as an ad. The terms do not publish rates or a TikTok fee. They do say payouts include all taxes, including VAT.

One line in those terms deserves attention. You give TikTok a licence to your project content that lasts 2 years, and TikTok can pass it on to the brand. It is non exclusive, so you can license the same video to others.

3. How should I price a video myself?

No regulator or primary source publishes a typical UK rate for a UGC video. Any figure you see quoted as "the going rate" is someone's estimate. The honest way to price is to start from what an hour of your time is worth.

Count every step, not just filming. Here is our own assumption for one 30 second video: 15 minutes reading the brief, 20 minutes scripting, 30 minutes filming, 45 minutes editing, 20 minutes on a round of changes and 10 minutes of admin. That is 2 hours 20 minutes.

Now compare. On those hours, Billo's regular 30 second rate of about £29.86 works out at about £12.80 an hour, and the premium rate of about £59.72 at about £25.60. Those are before tax and before your kit.

Three rules make your own pricing hold up:

  • Price the usage, not just the video. A brand running your face in paid ads for two years is getting more than a file. Say in writing how long and where the video can be used.
  • Price the revisions. Include one round of changes and charge for more.
  • Set aside tax. If your salary already uses your Personal Allowance, 20% of your profit goes to Income Tax. A £20 hourly target is £16 after that.

4. Worked example: Megan in Cardiff

Megan, 26, a retail supervisor in Cardiff

Megan has a full time job and makes Billo videos at weekends. She has about 400 followers and has never posted an ad. She takes six 30 second videos a month at the regular rate.

Six videos at $40 is $240 a month, about £179.16. Over a year that is about £2,149.90 in cash.

Each task also comes with the product. We assume each is worth £15, which is our own figure. That is £1,080 of products in a year.

HMRC counts both. In the 2026 to 2027 tax year her gross trading income is about £3,229.90, well over £1,000, so she must register for Self Assessment. It is even over the £3,000 allowance that starts in April 2027.

She takes the £1,000 trading allowance instead of claiming expenses. Her salary already uses her Personal Allowance, so she pays 20% on about £2,229.90. That is about £445.98.

Without the products, the tax would be about £229.98. So £216 of her tax bill is on things she was sent, not money she was paid.

Her time is six videos at 2 hours 20 minutes, which is 14 hours a month. Her cash rate is about £12.80 an hour before tax. Every figure in this box is our own arithmetic.

Reaching Billo's premium tier would double her rate per video without adding hours.

5. What do the ad rules say?

Two sets of rules apply, and both bite the creator as well as the brand.

The advertising code. Rule 2.1 of the CAP Code says ads must be obviously identifiable as ads. The Advertising Standards Authority says content becomes an ad if you got a payment or any other incentive, and a free product counts.

If you post a brand's video on your own account, the ASA wants a clear label such as "Ad" at the start. A label buried in hashtags is not enough. "Gifted", "sponsored", "thanks to" and "#BrandAmbassador" are unlikely to be clear enough. The ASA says brands and creators are both responsible.

The fake reviews ban. The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 bans writing or commissioning a fake consumer review. A fake review is one that claims to be based on someone's genuine experience, but is not. Hiding the fact that a review was paid for is also banned.

The CMA says these rules apply to practices from 6 April 2025. This matters to UGC because the style is built to look like a real customer. If a brand's script says "I have used this every day for months" and you opened it on Tuesday, do not say it.

6. How is it taxed, free products included?

UGC income is trading income. Nobody takes tax off before you are paid, and TikTok's terms say you are solely responsible for any tax.

Free products count. HMRC's guidance on online platform income says income from creating online content includes gifts and services you receive from promoting products. For things you get instead of money, you use what they would have cost you.

The £1,000 trading allowance. GOV.UK says it covers up to £1,000 a year of gross income. HMRC's side hustle guide says it is a single allowance for all your side hustles together. Above £1,000, register for Self Assessment by 5 October after the tax year ends.

Once registered, you choose the allowance or your real expenses, not both. If you have bought a ring light, a microphone and editing software, compare the two.

From 6 April 2027 the numbers change. HMRC confirmed on 28 October 2026 that the trading allowance rises to £3,000, and the Self Assessment reporting threshold rises to £3,000 with it.

National Insurance and VAT. Class 4 National Insurance at 6% starts only when your self employed profits pass £12,570. VAT registration only applies once your taxable turnover passes £90,000 in 12 months, which few UGC creators reach.

HMRC also says you must keep full records of income and expenses. Keep a list of every product, what it would have cost, and the date. Our side hustle tax guide covers the trading allowance in full, and how to register for Self Assessment covers the paperwork.

7. The honest verdict

UGC is one of the few creator side hustles that does not need an audience, and Billo's published table proves people are paid for it. But at Billo's regular rate of $40 for a 30 second video, our own sums put the work at about £12.80 an hour. That is wage level money, not a windfall.

It is worth it if you are quick, enjoy filming and can reach a higher tier or your own clients. Price from your hourly target, charge for usage and revisions, and say no to work below it.

It is not worth it if you are chasing free products. They are taxed as income, and they do not pay the bill. Label your own posts "Ad", never fake a review, and register with HMRC once your side income passes the trading allowance.

Frequently asked

Do you need followers to be a UGC creator? Not for most UGC work, because the brand posts the video. Billo says no social media presence is required, and accepts UK creators aged 18 or over. TikTok One is the exception: it says brand projects need at least 1,000 followers.

How much do UGC creators get paid per video in the UK? Billo, one of the few platforms that publishes rates, pays $30 to $75 per video for regular creators and $70 to $120 for premium creators, depending on length. That is roughly £22 to £90 at HMRC's November 2026 rate. Most other rates are private.

Do I pay tax on free products from brands? Yes, if you received them for promoting the product. HMRC says gifts and services from promoting products count as income, valued at what they would have cost you. They count towards the trading allowance, £1,000 now and £3,000 from 6 April 2027, along with any cash.

Do UGC videos need an ad label? If you post the video on your own account after being paid or gifted the product, the ASA expects a clear "Ad" label at the start. Brands and creators share responsibility. Words such as "gifted" or "sponsored" are unlikely to be clear enough.
