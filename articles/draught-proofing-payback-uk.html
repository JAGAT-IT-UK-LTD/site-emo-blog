Most of the articles in this series end with a payback measured in decades and a verdict that says buy it for comfort. This one is different. The Energy Saving Trust's own figures put whole-house draught proofing at about £250 professionally and £55 a year saved, and the DIY version costs a fraction of that for most of the saving. It is the cheapest heat you will ever keep, and the only trap is sealing the gaps that are there on purpose.

1. The figures, and why they are more reliable than most

The Energy Saving Trust's draught proofing page, updated 20 May 2026 and read again on 4 September 2026, gives two numbers for Great Britain. Professional draught proofing of windows and doors for a whole house could cost around £250. Draught proofing around windows, floors and doors could save around £55 a year. Its fuel prices are from July 2026, which are slightly below the Ofgem cap for October to December 2026, so if anything the saving this winter is a little higher.

Divide one by the other: £250 divided by £55 is 4.5 years. That is the professional payback, and it is the shortest of any measure the Energy Saving Trust prices. Loft insulation from nothing is about four years in a semi, but most homes already have some, and topping up runs to decades. Double glazing is 56 years. A new boiler is ten to eighteen. Draught proofing sits on its own. If the house needs more than gaps sealed, the insulation grants that close at the end of December 2026 are the cheaper route while they are still open.

There is a second figure on the same page: an open chimney in a fireplace you do not use, draught proofed with a cap or an excluder, saves around £45 a year. That is on top of the £55, and it applies to a lot of older terraces and semis where the fireplace is decorative.

Two cautions. The £55 is a whole-house figure for a home that is draughty to begin with; a modern house with sealed uPVC windows and a composite front door has little to gain. And the saving depends on your heating bill: it is calculated for a gas-heated home at typical use, and an all-electric home with the same draughts saves about three times as much because its heat costs three times as much per unit.

3. Doing it yourself, and what the materials cost

Find them first. On a windy evening, walk the house with the back of your hand. Doors, windows, the loft hatch, the letterbox, the keyhole, where pipes go through outside walls, the gap under the skirting, the fireplace. A moving curtain is a draught; so is a whistle.

Windows that open. Self-adhesive foam strip is the cheapest and lasts a winter or two; brush or rubber-wiper strip on a metal or plastic carrier costs a little more and lasts years. Match the strip to the gap: too thick and the window will not close, too thin and it does nothing. For a window that never opens, a bead of silicone around the frame.

Doors. Four gaps: the keyhole (a cover with a drop disc), the letterbox (a brush or a flap), the bottom (a brush strip or a hinged flap on the door, not a sausage on the floor, which moves), and the edges (the same strips as windows). Do not draught proof internal doors; the gap under them is how air moves round the house.

The chimney. An inflatable chimney balloon or a wool-type excluder that sits in the flue, with a tag hanging down so nobody lights a fire under it. They are designed to leave a little ventilation, which the chimney needs. Take it out before any fire.

Floors and skirting. A flexible filler or decorator's caulk between the skirting and the boards, and between boards where you can see daylight into the void. It has to flex, because floors move; a hard filler cracks out in a season.

The loft hatch. Foam strip around the frame, exactly as for a door, and a piece of insulation on the back of the hatch itself. Ten minutes.

A three-bed semi with an unused chimney, two external doors, nine opening windows and a bit of skirting comes in at roughly £60 to £120 in materials. Against most of the £55 plus the £45 chimney saving, that is a payback of about a year.

4. The gaps that are there on purpose

The Energy Saving Trust is unusually firm on this and so are we. A house needs to breathe, and the following are ventilation, not draughts:

Extractor fans in kitchens, bathrooms and utility rooms. Airbricks and underfloor grilles, which keep timber floors dry. Wall vents. Trickle vents in window frames, which should normally be left open. If you have none of these, your home may already be too airtight, and more draught proofing risks condensation and mould rather than saving money.

Rooms with an open fire or an open-flued appliance, and any room with a flueless gas heater or a gas hob, need their fixed ventilation left alone; the gas regulations require it. And kitchens and bathrooms, where the moisture is made, should be the last rooms you seal, not the first.

The rule of thumb: seal gaps that let air in by accident. Leave anything that was put there to let air in on purpose.

5. Worked example: Fiona in Dundee

Two-bed 1930s terrace, gas central heating, original timber sash windows at the front, an open fireplace in the living room she has never used

Fiona's living room is cold in the evening even with the radiator on, and the curtains move. She has £100 to spend and a free Saturday.

The chimney. An inflatable excluder, about £25. Energy Saving Trust saving £45 a year. Payback about seven months.

The front door. Brush strip on the bottom, letterbox brush, keyhole cover, foam around the frame, about £25. Our estimate of the saving: £15 to £20 a year. Payback under two years.

The sash windows. Foam strip does not work on sashes, so she fits brush strip along the meeting rail and the bottom rail of the two front windows, about £30, and leaves the back uPVC windows alone because they are already sealed. Saving perhaps £10 a year; payback three years; a professional sash job later would do better.

What she leaves. The trickle vents on the uPVC windows, the bathroom extractor, and the airbrick under the front bay.

Total: about £80, saving about £70 to £75 a year, payback just over one winter. The room also stops feeling draughty, and she turns the living room radiator valve down a notch, which is where the Energy Saving Trust's £120 a year for one degree starts to appear. She does not count that in the sum, but she notices it.

6. The honest verdict

Do it. Draught proofing is the one home energy measure where the arithmetic is not marginal: £250 professionally for £55 a year is a four and a half year payback on the Energy Saving Trust's own figures, and £60 to £120 of DIY materials for most of the same saving pays back in a year or two. If you have an unused open chimney, the excluder alone pays back inside a winter.

The only way to get it wrong is to seal the ventilation the house depends on. Leave the extractors, airbricks, wall vents and trickle vents alone, keep the ventilation in any room with a fire or a gas appliance, and start with the chimney and the front door.

Frequently asked

How much does draught proofing save per year in the UK? The Energy Saving Trust puts draught proofing around windows, floors and doors at about £55 a year in Great Britain, and draught proofing an unused open chimney at a further £45, both at July 2026 fuel prices. An all-electric home saves roughly three times as much because its heat costs more per unit.

Is professional draught proofing worth it? At around £250 for a whole house against £55 a year, it pays back in about 4.5 years, which is shorter than any other insulation measure. It is most worthwhile for older houses and sash windows, where DIY strips do not fit well. The measures it beats on payback are the expensive fabric ones: what external wall insulation actually costs runs to five figures on a typical semi, which is why sealing gaps comes first.

Can I draught proof a rented home? Yes. Self-adhesive foam and brush strips, letterbox brushes, keyhole covers and a chimney balloon need no permission and can all be removed. Filling skirting gaps and fitting a chimney cap are the two jobs to ask about first. If you rent, the question is which of it you can take with you when you move, and a renter energy saving kit answers exactly that.

What should I not draught proof? Extractor fans, airbricks and underfloor grilles, wall vents and trickle vents, and any fixed ventilation in a room with an open fire, an open-flued appliance, a flueless gas heater or a gas hob. Internal doors should keep their gap underneath.
