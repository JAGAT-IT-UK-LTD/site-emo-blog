How to Spot Fake Amazon Reviews Now Every Checking Tool Has Shut Down

Published 22 August 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026 · 12 min read · Figures checked November 2026

How to spot fake Amazon reviews in 2026: read the three-star reviews first, check whether complaints cluster on one fault, and trust the "frequently returned item" badge over the star average. Every automatic checker, Fakespot included, has shut down, so the check is now yours to do. It takes about ninety seconds a product once you know where to look.

It matters more than it used to. Since 6 April 2025 fake reviews are banned outright in the UK, and the CMA can fine a business up to 10% of its global turnover. The law is on your side, but it does not read the listing for you.

Is there still a fake Amazon reviews checker?

No. If an older guide told you to paste the product link into Fakespot or ReviewMeta, skip that step. All three of the well-known checkers are dead.

  • Fakespot: bought by Mozilla, which shut the Review Checker inside Firefox on 10 June 2025 and the Fakespot website, apps and extensions on 1 July 2025.
  • ReviewMeta: dead. The site returns HTTP 403.
  • TheReviewIndex: dead. Its own site says it is permanently down because of Amazon's policy changes.

Checked again 20 November 2026.

The common cause matters more than each closure. All three relied on scraped review data. Amazon's API and policy changes cut that supply off.

That wall has not moved. So the new tools calling themselves Fakespot alternatives sit on the same sand. Assume any tool promising an automatic authenticity score is either guessing or about to close.

The squeeze runs the other way too. Google spent August 2026 demoting thin review and deals pages published under borrowed authority. What the site reputation policy changed for affiliate and review sites explains why the round-up pages that used to rank above the product are thinning out. That leaves manual reading as the method, not a stopgap.

If you read reviews this closely because you write about products, the earning side of Amazon splits along the same line. The Influencer Programme pays the same commission rates as Associates, so the choice turns on where your traffic comes from. If you are thinking about selling instead, start from what FBA actually costs a UK seller per unit once fees and storage are in.

Manual checking is what is left. The rest of this guide shows how to do it properly. It pays least where reviews cannot settle the question at all. Think whether a mattress needs replacing or just a topper, or whether an ink subscription beats buying cartridges. Both turn on your own use, not anyone's stars.

It pays most on cheap electronics. That is why it is the method behind choosing budget headphones without trusting the star rating.

How many Amazon reviews are fake?

Honestly, nobody can say, and you should know that before you believe a scary number anywhere.

The large percentages that circulate online mostly trace back to Fakespot era estimates. The method died with the tool, so we do not repeat those figures here.

What is documented comes from Amazon itself. It says it blocked more than 275 million suspected fake reviews in 2024. In July 2025 it won a court ruling against more than 75 fake review websites, with ownership of the related domains moved to Amazon.

Both things are true at once. The platform is fighting hard. The problem is large enough to need that much fighting.

What words give a fake review away?

Reading single reviews, these are the patterns worth noticing:

  • Uniform praise with no downsides at all. Real reviews name trade-offs, because real products have them.
  • The product name used instead of "it". "The X arrived quickly. The X was easy to assemble." That is a paid per mention habit, or an AI one.
  • "I was sceptical, but..." A built-in credibility template. It turns up far more in paid reviews than in life.
  • Grammar that is too clean. No typos, no fragments, no regional words at all.
  • Copy that mirrors the marketing bullets rather than real use. If the review could have been written from the listing alone, it probably was.
  • Objections handled before anyone raised them. "I worried about the durability, but..." means the writer read the negative reviews first.

AI writing makes the language test weaker every year. Clean, specific sounding text is now cheap. So lean on the structural signs below, which are much harder to fake at scale.

What patterns give a fake listing away?

These are more reliable than wording, because they cost real money to fake:

  • Burst clusters. Fifteen or more five-star reviews inside about a day and a half, then silence. Genuine reviews arrive in lumps, but not lumps like that.
  • Reviewers who only ever review one brand. Click through on two or three of the keenest reviewers.
  • Studio photos in "customer" images. White background, professional lighting. Reverse image search it. It is often the press kit.
  • Review hijacking. Amazon and the CMA call it catalogue abuse. A seller merges a new product into an old listing and inherits its reviews. The tell is reviews describing a different product, or far too many reviews for the listing's age.
  • "Verified Purchase" is weak evidence. Brokers reimburse buyers for real purchases, which produces genuinely verified fake reviews. Treat it as a floor, not a signal.

What is the single best habit?

If you take one thing from this guide: read the three-star reviews first.

They are the least worth faking in either direction. Nobody is paid to write a lukewarm review. No rival bothers to sabotage a product with a middling one. Three-star reviews are where people name the real trade-offs.

Then read the one-stars, and ask one question. Do the complaints cluster on one specific defect, or scatter across unrelated issues?

Clustering means a real quality problem. Scatter is normal for any product with thousands of buyers.

Is the discount real?

Unlike the review checkers, price trackers survive. CamelCamelCamel is free. Keepa has a free tier plus a paid tier, and adds sales rank history.

  1. Is the deal real? Compare today's price with the twelve-month average line, never the struck-through list price. If today sits near or above the yearly average, there is no deal.
  2. Is this the year's low? A "40% off" that lands above the ninety-day low is worse than an ordinary Tuesday three months ago.
  3. Was the price raised just before the sale? A step up a few weeks before Prime Day or Black Friday, then a "discount" back to the old price, is the classic pattern. It is plain on the chart.
  4. Set an alert and walk away. For anything not urgent, this turns price psychology from a weakness into an advantage.
  5. Use the sales rank graph as an authenticity check. A rank that was flat and obscure, then spiked overnight with a burst of reviews, is a launch manipulation signature.

There is peer-reviewed evidence that some discounts are manufactured. A study published in Marketing Science in 2022 tracked more than 1,700 vacuum cleaners on Amazon, with nearly half a million price observations from 2016 and 2017.

It found sellers adding a list price at the same moment as a price rise, so the rise looked like a discount. More than a quarter of the vacuum cleaners had been "discounted" this way at some point. Shoppers paid about 23% more on average.

The trick showed up elsewhere too, in more than 13% of cases for blenders, digital cameras and drones. Vacuums are a fair test case, because the discount is rarely the real question. Whether a robot or a cordless suits the house decides more than any star rating. For the November sales in particular, our Black Friday fake discount checks go through the same chart step by step.

Know the terms too. The struck-through figure may be a list price, often a fictional manufacturer RRP. Or it may be a "was" price, Amazon's own recent price calculation.

Neither reliably shows what anyone actually paid recently. The percentage off is worked out from a number the seller largely controls.

Worked example: Aisha's vacuum in Leicester

Aisha and her partner in Leicester want a cordless vacuum before Christmas. The figures below are an illustration of the method, not a real listing.

The listing shows 4.6 stars, "£160, now £120, 25% off". That sum checks out: 25% of £160 is £40, and £160 less £40 is £120.

Step one, price history. The tracker's twelve-month average is £115. So the "deal" costs her £5 more than the typical price, and the £160 was never what people paid.

Step two, the three-stars. Five of the eight three-star reviews say the battery fades after about six months. The one-stars say the same.

Step three, the pattern. Twenty five-star reviews arrived over two days last spring, all with studio photos. That is a burst cluster.

Aisha sets a price alert and looks at a rival model instead. If she buys anything over £100, she pays by credit card. On a £120 vacuum that puts her card provider on the hook alongside the seller under Section 75.

Total time spent: about two minutes. Money at risk if she had bought on the stars alone: the full £120, on a battery the buyers themselves were warning about.

What are your UK rights?

Two things work in your favour that Amazon's own pages do not advertise.

"Fulfilled by Amazon" is not "sold by Amazon". FBA only means Amazon stores and ships it. The seller can still be anyone. Only "Ships from and sold by Amazon" means Amazon is the seller.

That is exactly where warranty and authenticity risk lives, and most shoppers mix the two up. For FBA stock sent in from 31 March 2026, Amazon ended commingling, so you now get the unit from the seller you chose. That closes the "good seller, fake unit" hole. A seller who stocks fakes still ships you fakes.

Pay by credit card for anything expensive. Under Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act, for a single item priced over £100 and up to £30,000, your card provider is jointly liable with the seller. Section 75 against chargeback explains which to use when.

That remedy is faster and stronger than Amazon's goodwill, and it runs separately from it. UK sellers also owe you goods that match the description and are of satisfactory quality, whoever they bought from. The 30 day refund rule is the part most people forget they have.

One caution. Amazon warns customers whose overall return rate is high, and it does not publish the threshold. Return what is faulty, but do not use returns as a free trial.

Are fake reviews illegal in the UK?

Yes. This is the part most review guides have not caught up with. The consumer rules in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 took effect on 6 April 2025. Fake reviews, and incentivised reviews that hide the incentive, became banned practices in their own right.

The CMA can now fine a business directly, up to 10% of its global turnover. It published its detailed fake reviews guidance on 4 April 2025. The key shift for shoppers is that the duty does not stop at the person who wrote the review.

Banned practiceWho it catches
Submitting a fake review, or one written for an undisclosed incentiveThe reviewer
Commissioning such reviews, including by offering any incentiveThe seller or brand
Publishing reviews misleadingly, such as hiding or cherry-picking genuine ones, or hiding that a review was incentivisedThe platform
Publishing misleading review information, such as star ratings or review countsThe platform
Offering to get banned reviews, or services that help others do itReview brokers and agencies
Failing to take reasonable and proportionate steps to prevent and remove banned reviewsThe platform

What "reasonable and proportionate steps" means. The CMA guidance says a platform hosting reviews should publish an easy to find policy banning fake reviews. It should run risk assessments based on evidence and keep them updated.

It should control who can post, run manual and automatic checks, and give others a way to report. It should investigate suspicious reviews promptly. It should remove confirmed fakes and correct the ratings, counts and rankings they affected. Then it should check regularly that all of this works.

The steps must match the level of risk. The guidance is explicit that a publisher cannot excuse itself on the grounds of limited resources.

What Amazon itself has promised. On 6 June 2025 the CMA announced formal undertakings from Amazon, after an investigation it opened in 2021. They cover the reviews, review counts and star ratings shoppers see on Amazon's UK store.

Amazon committed to detect and remove fake reviews and catalogue abuse quickly. Businesses that boost ratings that way can be banned from selling. Reviewers who post fakes can be barred, and their past reviews deleted. And reporting a suspect review is meant to be clear and easy.

The honest part. None of this makes the problem vanish, and it was never a promise that every fake gets caught. The standard is risk-based and proportionate, not perfect.

Enforcement runs through the CMA, not your own complaint. And the products most worth faking are the cheap unbranded ones, where the seller can vanish and relist under another name next week. Treat the law as the reason platforms must act on what you report, not as a reason to stop reading sceptically. The CMA has already shown it will act on hidden costs, as its drip pricing work shows.

How do you report fake Amazon reviews?

Start on the listing. Each review has a "Report" link beneath it. Use it for any review that looks bought, copied or about a different product.

If the whole listing looks rigged, report the seller to Trading Standards through the Citizens Advice consumer service. That is the route that feeds CMA enforcement.

Under its 2025 undertakings, Amazon has promised the CMA that reporting will be clear and easy. If it is not, that is worth saying when you contact Citizens Advice.

Frequently asked

Is there a website to spot fake Amazon reviews? Not a reliable one any more. Fakespot closed on 1 July 2025, and ReviewMeta and TheReviewIndex are down too. Any new checker needs review data it almost certainly cannot get. The honest answer is a two minute manual check: three-star reviews first, then clustered complaints, then the price history chart.

Are fake reviews illegal in the UK? Yes, since 6 April 2025 under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024. Writing, commissioning or selling fake reviews is banned, and so is hiding paid reviews. Platforms must take reasonable steps to stop them. The CMA can fine a business up to 10% of its global turnover for breaking the rules.

Does Verified Purchase mean a review is real? No. It only shows the reviewer bought the item on Amazon. Review brokers often refund buyers after a five-star review, so the purchase is real and the review is not. Treat Verified Purchase as a minimum, then look for burst clusters, one-brand reviewers and complaints that repeat the same fault.

How do I report a fake review on Amazon? Click "Report" under the review on the product page. For a seller you think is buying reviews, contact the Citizens Advice consumer service, which passes reports to Trading Standards and feeds CMA enforcement. Amazon promised the CMA in June 2025 that reporting fake reviews would be clear and easy to use.

Should I ignore the star rating completely? Not completely, but it is the least useful number on the page. Read the three-star and one-star text and check whether complaints cluster. A few detailed, mixed reviews on a new product tell you more than four hundred glowing ones posted in a single week.

The verdict

The tools are gone and they are not coming back, so the skill is now yours. Read the three-stars, look for clustered complaints, and treat "frequently returned" as the one badge that means something.

Check the price against the twelve-month line, and pay by credit card if it is over £100. The law now backs you, so report what looks rigged. That is most of the protection available, and it costs about two minutes.
