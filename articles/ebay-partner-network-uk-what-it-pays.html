eBay runs its own affiliate programme, the eBay Partner Network, and unlike some marketplaces it publishes the rate card. The rates are low, the cookie is short, and the second-hand nature of the stock makes it a better fit for some niches than others. Here is the card, the rules that decide whether you get paid, and what the money looks like on a small UK site.

1. The rate card, category by category

The eBay Partner Network, EPN for short, is eBay's own affiliate programme. You join at partnernetwork.ebay.com, build links to listings, searches or categories, and earn a percentage of the price of anything a visitor buys within the tracking window. The current global rate card, which eBay dates to 24 August 2020 and still publishes as the operating card, sets the percentage by the category the item sits in:

The percentage applies to what eBay calls GMB, the sale price of the item, and eBay says plainly that items on which it earns little or no revenue, such as charity listings and certain promotions, pay little or no commission. Partners who drive a sale through a coupon can receive a reduced rate. The card is in dollars because it is global; a UK account earns in pounds on the same percentages.

Compare that with Amazon UK, where we set out the full commission table: eBay's 4% fashion rate sits close to Amazon's clothing rate, its 1.5% on computers is below Amazon's, and its 3% on home and garden is in the same territory. The difference is not mainly the rate. It is the stock, which on eBay is largely second-hand and auctioned, and the cookie.

2. The rules that decide whether you get paid

The cookie lasts 24 hours. When someone clicks your link, eBay drops a cookie that credits you with anything they buy on the site in the next 24 hours, not just the item you linked to. After that the credit is gone. eBay uses last click, so if the same person clicks another partner's link inside your 24 hours, that partner gets the sale, not you. Amazon works the same way with its own 24 hour window; Awin's retail programmes typically give 30 days, which is why the cookie is the single biggest structural difference between networks for a small site.

Auctions get a special rule. If the visitor places a bid within 24 hours of your click and then wins the auction within 10 days, that is a qualifying transaction. Bids placed after the 24 hours, or auctions won after the 10 days, do not count. In practice this suits sites whose readers are hunting for a specific used item and bid the day they find it, and does not suit long browsing.

You are paid once per qualifying transaction, and the caps above apply per transaction. There is a Network Agreement with the usual restrictions: no bidding on eBay trademarks in paid search, no cookie stuffing, no self-purchases through your own links. The programme is open to sites, apps and social channels, and UK partners join the same network as everyone else, choosing ebay.co.uk as their programme.

3. When and how the money arrives

Three numbers govern the cash. The minimum payment threshold is 10 units of your account currency, so £10 for a UK account, and anything below it carries forward to the next month. Commission is locked for 30 days after the end of the month in which it was earned, which is eBay's window for returns and cancellations. Payment is then sent around the 10th business day of the following month. eBay's own example: January's earnings are paid around 12 March. Payment goes to a bank account by direct deposit or to PayPal; you need to complete the withdrawal settings and a tax form before the first payment goes out.

So a sale on 3 January pays on or around 12 March. That is slower than Awin's fortnightly runs once a merchant has validated, and roughly in line with Amazon's 60 days after month end. For a site earning a few pounds a month, the £10 threshold is reached quickly; the wait is the lock, not the threshold.

4. A worked example: a vintage watch blog in Norwich

Tom runs a small blog in Norwich about mechanical watches from the 1960s and 1970s. His readers are buyers as well as readers, and eBay is where most of that stock changes hands. He joins EPN and links each article to a saved eBay search for the model discussed.

Watches sit in the fashion category at 4%. Suppose in a month 1,200 readers click through, and 2% of them, 24 people, buy something within 24 hours. The average sale is £60, because his readers buy straps, crystals and service parts as often as whole watches. Twenty-four sales at £60 is £1,440 of sales, and 4% of that is £57.60 in commission for the month on our arithmetic. Over a year, if traffic holds, that is £691.20.

Now the same traffic on a laptop review site. Computers pay 1.5%, so the same £1,440 of sales pays £21.60 a month, or £259.20 a year. The traffic and the conversion rate were identical; the category cut the income by nearly two thirds. That is the first lesson of the rate card: EPN rewards niches that live in the 3% and 4% rows.

Tom's month includes one big one: a reader clicks through and wins a £1,800 auction for a whole watch eight days later, having bid within the first 24 hours. That is a qualifying transaction, and 4% of £1,800 is £72, well under the USD 550 cap. A single auction win doubled his month. It also shows the flip side: had the reader bid on day two rather than day one, Tom would have earned nothing.

5. Where EPN fits, and where Amazon still wins

EPN is the right programme when your readers already buy on eBay: collectables, vintage clothing, used vehicle parts, retro games, records, tools, and the long tail of things that are not sold new any more. Those niches sit in the 3% and 4% rows, the stock is on eBay and nowhere else, and readers who have found the exact item they want tend to buy or bid the same day, which is what a 24 hour cookie needs. We covered the retro gaming market from the seller's side; the buyer's side is exactly this kind of traffic.

Amazon wins for new products with a review site behind them, for anything where readers compare and come back, and for the cross-sell effect of a basket that fills with unrelated items. Amazon's 24 hour cookie is the same length, but its buyers convert at a higher rate on new goods, and the programme's rules and reporting are more familiar to most UK sites. The two are not exclusive: a site can run both, and the honest approach is to link each product to the place your reader would actually buy it.

Two cautions. First, EPN's rate card has not been updated since August 2020, and eBay reserves the right to change it; a site built on the 4% row should be able to survive a cut. Second, second-hand prices vary wildly and the commission tracks the price actually paid, so income is lumpier than an Amazon programme with steady new-product prices. Budget on the quiet months, not the auction month.

6. The honest verdict

EPN pays 1% to 4% of the sale price, by category, with a 24 hour cookie and a 10 day auction rule. A £60 fashion or collectables sale earns £2.40; the same sale in computers earns 90p.

Join if your readers already buy second-hand, vintage, parts or collectables on eBay. Those niches sit in the 3% and 4% rows and their buyers act within the day, which is what the cookie needs.

Do not build a site around it. The rates are low, the card is six years old and can change, and the income is lumpy. Use it alongside Amazon or a network programme, and link to wherever your reader would genuinely buy.

Frequently asked

How long is the eBay affiliate cookie? 24 hours from the click for Buy It Now purchases. For auctions, the bid must be placed within 24 hours and the auction won within 10 days. eBay uses last click attribution.

What is the minimum payout on the eBay Partner Network? 10 units of your account currency, so £10 for a UK account. Balances below that carry forward to the next month.

When does eBay pay affiliates? Commission locks 30 days after the end of the month it was earned, and payment is sent around the 10th business day of the month after that. January earnings arrive around 12 March, on eBay's own example.

Do I earn commission if the buyer purchases something different from what I linked? Yes. Anything the visitor buys within the 24 hour window counts, at the rate of the category the purchased item sits in, not the item you linked.
