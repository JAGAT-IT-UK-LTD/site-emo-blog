Broadband Price Rises in April 2027: What Your Contract Really Costs

Broadband price rises in April 2027 are already fixed for most customers. BT, EE, Plusnet, Virgin Media and TalkTalk add £4 a month, and Vodafone £3.50. On a typical £27 deal that flat rise costs more than the inflation rule Ofcom banned, and the headline price understates your real monthly cost by about 17%. Here are the sums, and the two moves that save far more.

  • Since 17 January 2025 providers cannot write inflation linked price rises into new contracts. They must state any rise in pounds and pence, before you sign. That is an Ofcom rule, announced 19 July 2024.
  • The April 2027 rises are already set. BT, EE and Plusnet add £4 a month on 31 March 2027. Virgin Media and TalkTalk add £4 in April. Vodafone adds £3.50 on 1 April. Sky has not published a 2027 figure.

1. What did the pounds and pence rule actually change?

For years most large UK providers wrote a yearly increase into your contract. It was linked to a future inflation figure, usually CPI plus 3.9%, and applied every March or April.

Ofcom estimated that as of April 2024, around six in ten broadband and mobile customers were on contracts of that kind.

Ofcom's own research found people did not understand them. More than half of broadband customers, 55%, could not say what inflation measures like CPI and RPI actually measure.

Among customers of providers using inflation linked rises, only 16% were both aware there would be a rise and able to say it was inflation linked with an extra percentage on top.

So Ofcom banned the practice. From 17 January 2025, providers cannot put inflation linked or percentage based rise terms into new contracts. Any rise must be set out in pounds and pence, before you sign, with the dates it applies.

That is a genuine improvement, and it should be said clearly. The future cost of your contract is now knowable on the day you sign it.

Ofcom's own illustration shows exactly this. A price of £30.00 until a stated date, then £31.50, then £33.00, all printed next to the headline.

What it did not do is make the rises smaller. In April 2026 most big providers added £3 to £4 a month to broadband for customers who signed after the ban. The same amounts are written in again for April 2027.

A flat increase is the same cash for every customer. That makes it a far larger percentage for the cheapest deals, which are bought by the households with the least room to absorb it.

3. Is a flat rise cheaper than the old inflation rise?

Every guide to this subject lists the £3 and £4 figures. Almost none asks the obvious question. At what monthly price would the flat rise equal the formula it replaced?

The latest figure is CPI of 3.6% for the twelve months to October 2026, published by the ONS on 18 November 2026. Add the customary 3.9% and the old formula would produce 7.5%.

Our arithmetic, and it is only division. A flat rise equals a 7.5% rise where the flat amount is 7.5% of the monthly price.

£3.00 ÷ 0.075 = £40.00 a month
£3.50 ÷ 0.075 = £46.67 a month
£4.00 ÷ 0.075 = £53.33 a month

Below those prices, the flat pounds and pence rise takes more money off you than the banned term would at today's inflation.

For comparison, at January 2026 inflation of 3.0% the formula gave 6.9%, and the £4 break even was £57.97. Higher inflation since then has narrowed the gap, but not closed it.

Very few households pay £53 a month for broadband alone. BT's own Full Fibre 150 deal is advertised at £34.99, and Vodafone's entry plans start in the low to mid twenties.

So for the ordinary customer, the rule brought in to protect them still produces a larger cash rise than the practice it outlawed.

This is not a claim that Ofcom was wrong. Its stated aim was certainty and easy comparison, not a lower bill, and on certainty it delivered. But the trade was real, and it deserves a number rather than a feeling.

4. Worked example: the Rahman household in St Albans

The Rahmans sign a 24 month full fibre contract on 1 December 2026 at £27 a month. The contract discloses, as it must, a £4 rise on 31 March 2027 and another £4 on 31 March 2028.

What they actually pay.

December 2026 to March 2027, 4 months at £27 = £108.00
April 2027 to March 2028, 12 months at £31 = £372.00
April 2028 to November 2028, 8 months at £35 = £280.00

Total over 24 months: £760.00. True average: £31.67 a month.

The advertised price was £27. The real average is £4.67 a month higher, an understatement of 17.3%.

The same contract under the banned formula, at 7.5%. £27.00, then £29.03, then £31.21.

4 × £27.00 + 12 × £29.03 + 8 × £31.21 = £706.04.

The pounds and pence rule costs the Rahmans £53.96 over the contract.

Two honest caveats. This assumes inflation stays near its October 2026 level, which nobody can promise. That uncertainty is exactly what Ofcom objected to.

If CPI spiked to 8%, the old formula would be far worse. The point is not that flat rises are always dearer. It is that at the inflation rate we actually have, they are, and the gap has a number.

Signing in December also hurts. The first rise lands after only four months, so the Rahmans carry the higher prices for 20 of their 24 months.

5. Should you pick the deal with the smaller rise?

Once both rises are printed on the contract, it is tempting to shop on the size of the rise. That is mostly a mistake, and the arithmetic says so plainly.

Deal A: £25 a month, two £4 rises. 24 months from December = £712.00, an average of £29.67.

Deal B: £27 a month, two £3 rises. 24 months from December = £732.00, an average of £30.50.

Deal A wins by £20, despite having the bigger rises.

The reason is structural. On a term signed in December, a £1 difference in the yearly rise is worth about £28 in total. The first rise applies for 20 months and the second for eight.

A £2 difference in the headline price is worth £48, because it applies to all 24 months. The headline price usually still wins.

What genuinely changes the answer is when in the year you sign. Sign in February and the first rise lands after a single month. Sign in April and you get almost a year at the price you agreed.

If your current deal ends in winter, ask whether a short extension into April is cheaper over the next two years. Do that sum with your own two figures, not ours.

Some providers sidestep the question entirely. Zen Internet promises the price you agree will not rise during the contract. Uswitch also lists YouFibre, Fibrus, G Network, Utility Warehouse and others as fixing prices for the full term.

Most are regional or full fibre only networks, so check availability at your postcode first. Uswitch also shows Hyperoptic and Community Fibre now add a fixed amount each year, so read the order page.

6. Where is the real money in a broadband bill?

Everything above concerns £20 to £54 over two years. Worth knowing, not worth losing an evening over. Two other numbers are much larger.

Being out of contract. Ofcom's February 2026 research finds in contract customers spend £7 to £9 a month less than out of contract customers. That is £84 to £108 a year.

Ofcom's latest data says 28% of broadband customers are out of contract. If that is you, one call to your provider's retentions team is worth more than every price rise decision in this article.

You do not even have to switch. Re-signing with the provider you already have usually brings the price down. If you do move, One Touch Switch makes changing provider simple, and you only deal with the new company.

Social tariffs. If anyone in the household receives Universal Credit, Pension Credit, Jobseeker's Allowance, Income Support or Employment and Support Allowance, you may qualify for a social tariff.

On Ofcom's list, Hyperoptic Fair Fibre Fast is £12 a month for 50Mbit/s and EE Basics is £12. Virgin Media Essential Broadband is £12.50. BT Home Essentials starts at £16.50 for Universal Credit claimants with no income.

Ofcom counted 532,000 customers on a social tariff in June 2025, up 26,000 on the year. It also says 70% of eligible households do not know these tariffs exist.

Eligibility lists vary by provider, so check the provider's own page. We have set out who qualifies in our guide to broadband social tariffs.

The honest verdict

The pounds and pence rule did what it said. You can now read your contract and know the total. Use that.

Add up all 24 months and compare totals, not headline prices. On a deal signed this winter, the headline understates the truth by about 17%.

Do not shop on the size of the rise. A £1 difference in the yearly increase is worth about £28 over two years, while £2 off the monthly price is worth £48. The headline still wins most of the time.

And be clear about scale. If you are out of contract, that is £84 to £108 a year, and a fifteen minute call fixes it. If someone in the house is on a qualifying benefit, a social tariff from £12 a month may halve the bill. Everything else here is worth knowing, but it is not where your money is.

Frequently asked

Can I leave my broadband contract if the price goes up? Usually not. Ofcom's rules give you 30 days' notice and 30 days to leave without exit fees only when a rise was not set out in your contract. A pounds and pence rise printed when you signed is part of the deal. Sky works differently and lets customers leave within 30 days of its notice.

How much will broadband go up in April 2027? For most recent contracts, £3.50 to £4 a month. BT, EE and Plusnet add £4 on 31 March 2027. Virgin Media and TalkTalk add £4 in April, or less on older deals. Vodafone adds £3.50 on 1 April. Sky had not published its 2027 figure by late November 2026.

Are broadband prices actually rising, then? Across the market, no. Ofcom reported in February 2026 that broadband prices fell in real terms, by 6% on average across speeds. What rises is the bill of an existing in contract customer. New customers get cheaper deals than existing ones, which is exactly why re-signing at the end of your term matters.

Does the rule cover mobile and pay TV too? Yes. The ban applies to phone, broadband and pay TV contracts signed from 17 January 2025. Mobile rises are usually smaller. Vodafone's pay monthly airtime plans, for example, rise by £2.50 a month, and BT's TV plans by £2. Older contracts can still carry an inflation linked rise.

What if my contract started before 17 January 2025? It can still carry the old inflation linked term, because the ban covers new contracts only. Virgin Media customers from before 9 January 2025 still get RPI plus 3.9%. Check your own terms, and if you are near the end of the term, re-sign onto a fixed rise deal.
