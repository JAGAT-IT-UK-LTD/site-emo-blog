Nobody Tells You An Email List Bills You For The People Who Never Open It

"Build an email list" is the most repeated advice in affiliate marketing, and it is usually given as though the list were free. It is not. You are billed monthly on how many addresses you hold, not how many people read anything, and in the UK there is a rule in regulation 22 of PECR that decides who you are even allowed to email.

The short version

  • You are charged per subscriber, not per email sent. A subscriber who has never opened a single message costs exactly the same as your best buyer.
  • Kit's free plan runs to 10,000 subscribers, which is more generous than the "you must pay from day one" framing suggests. Its Creator plan is $33 a month at 1,000 subscribers, and Pro is $66, checked 3 September 2026.
  • The bills are in US dollars. The sterling cost moves with the exchange rate and your card may add a non-sterling transaction fee. Nobody includes that in the "cost per subscriber" maths.
  • If a quarter of your list opens anything, your real cost per engaged reader is four times the headline rate. That is arithmetic, not pessimism.
  • UK law, regulation 22 of PECR: you must not send marketing email to individuals without specific consent. The "soft opt-in" exception applies only to your own previous customers, never to bought or scraped lists.
  • Sole traders and some partnerships count as individuals under PECR, so business-to-business emailing is not the free-for-all it is often described as.

1. The pricing model nobody explains up front

Almost every email platform aimed at creators charges on the same basis: a monthly fee that steps up as the number of addresses on your list grows. Not the number of emails you send. Not the number opened. The count of records held.

That single design decision produces most of the surprise. Sending is effectively free at the margin, so the platform has no reason to charge for it. Storing and remaining ready to send to a name has a fixed cost, so that is what gets priced. The result is that your bill is driven by the size of your database and is completely indifferent to whether anyone in it is paying attention.

For a UK affiliate site this cuts in an unhelpful direction. The usual advice is to grow the list as fast as possible, typically with a free download offered to anyone who will hand over an address. That works, in the sense that the number goes up. What it also does is fill your paid database with people who wanted the free thing and have no interest in you, and you will pay for them every month for as long as you keep them.

The fix is not complicated. It is pruning, which almost nobody does, because deleting subscribers feels like going backwards.

2. The actual numbers, and the free tier that undercuts the pitch

Kit, formerly ConvertKit, is the default choice in the affiliate and creator world, so its published pricing is a fair benchmark. Checked on 3 September 2026, from Kit's own pricing page:

The first row deserves more attention than it usually gets, because it contradicts the standard sales narrative. The free plan runs to 10,000 subscribers and includes unlimited broadcasts, unlimited landing pages and forms, tagging and segmentation, and the ability to sell digital products. For a UK affiliate site building an audience, that is a genuinely usable product at no cost, and a very large number of people paying monthly fees do not need to be.

What the paid tiers buy is automation. Unlimited visual automations, unlimited sequences, subject line testing, removal of platform branding. Those are real features and they matter once you are running a sequence that converts. They are not what a site with 300 subscribers and no funnel needs.

Prices shown are Kit's list prices at the 1,000 subscriber tier and they rise as the list grows. We are not going to publish a full tier ladder here, because platform pricing changes several times a year and a stale table is worse than no table. Check the provider's own page on the day you decide.

3. The currency problem

Every figure in that table is in US dollars, and that is true of most of the platforms UK creators are pointed towards. Two consequences follow, and neither appears in the cost-per-subscriber maths people do in public.

Your cost in sterling is not fixed. It moves with the exchange rate, every month, in a direction you do not control. A subscription that felt affordable when you signed up can drift by a meaningful percentage over a year without the provider changing its prices at all.

Your card may add a fee. Many UK debit and credit cards apply a non-sterling transaction fee to dollar-denominated payments. It is typically a small percentage, but it is charged every month on a recurring bill, and it is invisible unless you go looking for it on your statement. We are not going to quote a rate, because it varies by card issuer. Check your own card's terms, and if you pay for several dollar-priced tools, check whether a card without the fee is worth having.

None of this makes a US-priced platform a bad choice. It makes the true cost slightly higher and slightly less predictable than the advertised one, which is exactly the sort of thing this site exists to point out.

4. Who you are legally allowed to email in the UK

This is the part that gets skipped, and it is not optional. The rules sit in regulation 22 of the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations, enforced by the Information Commissioner's Office.

The ICO's own summary is blunt: you must not send marketing emails or texts to individuals without specific consent. There is one narrow exception, usually called the soft opt-in, and it applies only where all of the following are true. The person is an existing customer who bought, or negotiated to buy, a similar product or service from you. You gave them a simple way to opt out when you first collected their details. And you give them a way to opt out in every message since.

Three points follow that catch UK creators out.

The soft opt-in does not cover prospective customers. Someone who downloaded your free guide has not bought anything from you, so they are not an existing customer. You need their actual consent, which is why the sign-up form has to be a genuine opt-in and not a pre-ticked box buried under a download button.

It does not cover bought-in lists at all. The ICO says so explicitly. Buying a list of UK addresses and emailing it is not a grey area.

Sole traders and some partnerships are treated as individuals. The received wisdom that "business-to-business email is fine" only holds for corporate bodies, which means companies, LLPs, Scottish partnerships and government bodies. A large share of the small UK businesses a creator might want to reach are sole traders, and they are covered by the individual rule.

You must also not disguise or conceal your identity, and you must give a valid contact address. One live caveat worth knowing: the ICO has flagged that this guidance is under review because of changes made by the Data (Use and Access) Act, so the detail may move. The consent principle is not the part likely to change.

5. Worked example: the cost of not pruning

Worked example: Marek in Sheffield

Marek runs a UK home-tech review site. Over two years he has built a list of 5,000 subscribers, almost all acquired through a free buying checklist. He is proud of the number and mentions it when people ask how the site is going.

Roughly 25% of the list opens anything he sends. That is not a bad rate. It is broadly what a general-interest list does once the people who wanted the freebie have drifted off.

So he is paying to hold 5,000 records, and reaching about 1,250 people. His cost per subscriber, the number he quotes to himself, is a quarter of his cost per reader. Whatever the headline monthly figure is, the real cost of reaching someone who actually reads him is four times that.

What pruning does. If he removes the 2,000 addresses that have not opened anything in twelve months, his list drops to 3,000, his monthly bill drops a tier or two, and the number of people reading him does not change at all, because those 2,000 were not reading. His open rate rises mechanically, which also helps deliverability, because inbox providers treat engagement as a quality signal.

The number goes down and everything else gets better. That is the trade almost nobody makes, because 5,000 sounds better than 3,000 and the bill is on a different screen from the vanity metric.

6. The honest verdict

An email list is a genuinely good asset for a UK affiliate site, for the reason usually given: it is the only audience you own, and it does not disappear when a search algorithm changes. That part of the advice is sound.

What is missing from the advice is that it is a recurring liability priced on a number most creators are actively trying to inflate. You pay for names, not readers. Growth for its own sake buys you a bigger bill and a worse open rate.

If you are starting, the free tier at 10,000 subscribers means the honest answer is that this costs you nothing for a long time and you should not be paying yet. If you are already paying, the highest-value hour you will spend this month is deleting everyone who has not opened anything in a year. And before you send anything at all, check that the people on your list gave you consent under regulation 22, because that is the part with a regulator attached.

Frequently asked

Is the free plan actually usable or is it crippled? On Kit's published feature list the free plan includes unlimited broadcasts, unlimited landing pages and forms, tagging and segmentation, and selling digital products. What it lacks is automation: sequences, visual automations and subject line testing sit on the paid tiers. For a site that emails a newsletter and links to its own content, the free plan does the job.

Does GDPR or PECR apply to a hobby blog? PECR applies to electronic marketing regardless of the size of the sender. The ICO's guidance is not limited to large organisations. Non-commercial promotions such as charity fundraising are treated differently, but a blog with affiliate links is commercial.

Can I email people who commented on my site? Not for marketing, unless they separately consented to marketing email. Leaving a comment is not consent to be added to a mailing list, and it does not make someone an existing customer for the soft opt-in.

How often should I prune? Once or twice a year is enough for most lists. The usual test is no opens in twelve months, applied after one final re-engagement email so people who want to stay can say so. Removing them costs you nothing you were actually getting.
