A third of households were not affected by the cap rise, and most of them do not know it

Ruhul Amin · Published 19 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Around 11 million UK households are on fixed energy tariffs. The October price cap rise did not touch a single one of them. If that is you, most of the energy advice you read this month was written for somebody else.

The short version

  • Around 35% of households, roughly 11 million, are on fixed tariffs and were not affected by the October 2026 cap rise.
  • The cap only protects standard variable tariffs. Ofgem says so plainly on its own explainer page.
  • Four groups are outside it: anyone on a fixed tariff, anyone on a business energy contract, anyone on a heat network, and anyone using heating oil.
  • Your bill will tell you in one line. Look for the tariff name and an end date. A fix has an end date. A variable tariff does not.
  • Being on a fix is not automatically good or bad. It only means the cap is not the number that governs your price.

1. The split nobody puts in the headline

When Ofgem announced that the price cap would rise 4% from October 2026, taking the typical dual fuel direct debit figure to £1,723 a year, that was reported everywhere as an energy bill rise.

Buried in Ofgem's own release is the qualification that changes who the story is about. Around 35% of households, roughly 11 million, are on fixed tariffs and were not affected by this rise.

Eleven million households is not a rounding error. It is more than the entire population of Greater London and Greater Manchester combined. For every three households reading a "your bill is going up £60" headline, one of them is reading about a price change that does not apply to their account.

2. The four groups the cap does not cover

Ofgem is unusually direct about this. Its price cap explainer states that the cap protects people on tariffs where the unit rate can go up or down with the market, which are standard variable tariffs. It then states that you are not protected by the price cap if you:

  • agreed a fixed tariff with your supplier, for as long as that fix runs
  • have a business energy contract, which includes some people living in properties supplied under one
  • get your heat from a heat network, which covers many newer flats and some estates
  • use heating oil, which is common in rural properties off the gas grid

The last two are worth dwelling on, because those households are not only outside the cap, they are outside most of the public conversation about energy prices entirely. A heat network customer has no cap, often no ability to switch supplier, and no standard variable tariff to fall back on.

3. How to check which group you are in, in two minutes

You do not need to phone anybody. Open your most recent bill or your supplier's app and look for two things.

First, the tariff name. Suppliers name fixed tariffs in a way that usually gives it away, typically with a word like Fixed and a duration or an end year.

Second, and more reliably, look for an end date. This is the single clearest test. A fixed tariff has a date on which it ends. A standard variable tariff does not, because there is nothing to end. If your bill shows a tariff end date, you are in the 35%.

A third clue is the exit fee. If your bill or tariff information mentions an early exit fee, you are on a fix, because there is nothing to exit early from on a variable tariff.

Note what is not a reliable test: whether you pay by direct debit, whether you have a smart meter, and whether your payments recently changed. Direct debit amounts get reviewed for reasons that have nothing to do with the unit rate, such as a build up or run down of credit on your account.

4. What it means if you are in the fixed third

It means three things, and only three.

Your price did not change on 1 October. Whatever you agreed is what you pay until your fix ends.

The next cap announcement on 25 November does not change your price either. It is worth reading, because it tells you what you would be paying if you were not fixed, which is the comparison that matters when your fix ends.

The date your fix ends is the date that matters to you. Put it in a calendar. Suppliers are required to tell you before a fix ends, but the notice arrives in an email among many others, and the default when a fix ends is usually to roll onto a variable tariff, which is where the cap does start applying to you.

Being on a fix is not inherently clever or foolish. If you fixed at a good moment you are ahead. If you fixed at a bad one you are behind, and there may be nothing useful to do about it until the end date. Either way, the useful action is knowing the date, not re-reading cap coverage.

5. A worked example: two neighbours, one announcement

Callum rents a terrace in Stockport on his supplier's standard variable tariff. He has never switched. On 1 October the cap change applied to him directly: the annualised typical figure moved from £1,663 to £1,723, a rise of £60 a year, about £5 a month, with gas carrying nearly all of it because gas costs rose around 8% while electricity stayed broadly flat.

Next door, Imani fixed for two years last spring. Her bill shows a tariff end date and an exit fee. On 1 October, nothing happened to her unit rate at all. She spent a week worried about a £60 increase that was never going to arrive, and she considered switching in response to it, which would have triggered an exit fee for no benefit.

Imani's useful action was not switching. It was writing her fix end date in her calendar with a reminder six weeks before, so that when the fix ends she chooses her next tariff deliberately rather than rolling onto a variable rate by default.

That is the honest conclusion of this article, and it is a small one. For roughly a third of households, the correct response to a price cap announcement is to note it, understand it does not apply to them yet, and go and do something else. There is no saving to chase here and no action to take. Knowing that is worth more than another article telling you to switch.

Frequently asked

How many UK households are on fixed tariffs? Around 35%, which Ofgem puts at roughly 11 million households. They were not affected by the October 2026 cap rise.

How do I tell if I am on a fixed tariff? Look for a tariff end date on your bill. A fixed tariff has one, a standard variable tariff does not. An early exit fee is another reliable sign of a fix.

Does the price cap protect me if I live in a flat on a heat network? No. Ofgem states the cap does not protect you if you get your heat from a heat network, nor if you use heating oil or are on a business energy contract.

My fix is ending soon. What happens? Unless you choose a new tariff, you will normally move onto your supplier's standard variable tariff, at which point the price cap does start to apply to you.
