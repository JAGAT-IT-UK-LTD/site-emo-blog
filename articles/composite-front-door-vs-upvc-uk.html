The short version

1. The heat loss sum, which takes about a minute

A standard external door opening, including the frame, is roughly 1.9 square metres. A three bedroom semi has something like 200 square metres of external walls, roof, floor and glazing. So the front door is under one per cent of the surface through which your house loses heat. That fact alone should end most of the energy conversation, but let us do the numbers anyway.

What you probably have. A uPVC door from the 1990s or 2000s with a glazed panel performs at roughly 3.0 W/m²K, and an older one with single glazed panels can be worse.

What you are allowed to fit. Approved Document L sets a limiting U-value for a replacement door of 1.4 W/m²K. Anything sold to you as a replacement should meet it, and good composite doors come in below.

The improvement is therefore 1.9 × (3.0 minus 1.4) = 3.04 watts per degree.

Now put that against a heating season. Inside 20 degrees, outside an average of 5, so a difference of 15. Heating on 8 hours a day for 210 days is 1,680 hours.

3.04 × 15 = 45.6 watts, over 1,680 hours = 76.6 kWh of heat a year. A gas boiler at about 90 per cent efficiency burns 85.1 kWh of gas to deliver it, and at the Ofgem price cap rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 7.97p per kWh, that is £6.78 a year.

Six pounds and seventy eight pence. That is what a new front door does for your heating bill.

If your door does not close properly, if you can see daylight round it or feel wind on your ankles, the heat you are losing is air, not conduction, and air leakage does not appear in any U-value. That can be worth ten times the figures above, and it is also fixable for about £15 with new brush strips and a weather bar, which is what our page on draught proofing is about. For comparison, every other fabric measure on this site beats a door by an enormous margin, because they cover an enormous area. Loft insulation covers 50 square metres. Cavity wall insulation covers 80. A door covers 1.9. Area is destiny in heat loss.

3. The security case, and the standard behind it

This is the argument that actually holds, so it is worth understanding properly rather than accepting a salesperson's version.

PAS 24 is the UK specification for enhanced security performance of doorsets and windows. It is not a description of the material; it is a set of physical tests applied to a complete doorset, simulating what a real opportunist does: levering, kicking, and attacking the lock with the tools somebody would actually bring. A door passes as an assembly, which is why a PAS 24 door slab in a cheap frame is not a PAS 24 doorset.

Where it is compulsory: Approved Document Q of the Building Regulations makes PAS 24 a minimum requirement for new build homes. For an existing home, Part Q is advisory, so nobody is obliged to fit one when replacing a door, though Part L's energy requirement does apply.

Composite doors have largely displaced uPVC for external doors in UK housing, and the reasons given are consistent: the appearance of timber, better thermal performance, lower maintenance and stronger security ratings than older uPVC doors. The first, third and fourth of those are real and worth money. The second, as section one showed, is worth £6.78 a year.

But here is the part the sales pitch tends to skip, and it changes the decision entirely.

4. The thirty pound version of a twelve hundred pound fix

The most common way an older uPVC door gets forced is not through the panel and not through the glass. It is the lock cylinder. A standard euro cylinder can be gripped and snapped, and on an unprotected door that is the work of well under a minute with tools that fit in a pocket.

The fix for that specific vulnerability is a TS007 3 star anti-snap cylinder, or a 1 star cylinder combined with a 2 star security handle. These are engineered to break at a sacrificial point, leaving the locking mechanism intact. They cost roughly £25 to £40, and swapping one on an existing multipoint uPVC door takes about ten minutes with a screwdriver and a tape measure.

So the comparison you should actually be making is not composite against uPVC. It is:

  • £1,200 for a new composite doorset, which gets you PAS 24 level security across the whole assembly, a door that closes properly, no more maintenance and something that looks better
  • £30 for a 3 star cylinder, which addresses the single commonest method of forced entry on the door you already own, and takes an evening

If your existing door is structurally fine and shuts properly, do the second one first. It is forty times cheaper and it closes the specific gap that most break-ins go through. Then, if you still want a new door in two years, you will want it for the right reasons and you will have lost nothing.

One thing worth checking while you are at it: many home insurers ask what locks you have and some require a British Standard lock on final exit doors. It is worth reading your own policy wording, because the answer occasionally changes what you are covered for, and that is a bigger number than anything else on this page.

5. Worked example: the Dhillons in Stockport

The Dhillons have a 2004 uPVC front door with a glazed top panel. It shuts, it locks, and it looks dated. They are quoted £1,380 for a composite replacement, and the salesman leads with energy efficiency.

They do the sum. Their existing door is a reasonable 3.0, so the improvement is 3.04 watts per degree, worth £6.78 a year. £1,380 divided by £6.78 is 203 years.

Then they check the actual complaints. The door is not draughty. It is not insecure in any way they can point at, except that they have never checked the cylinder. It is simply ugly, and the letterplate rattles.

So they spend £34 on a TS007 3 star cylinder and £12 on a new letterplate, and fit both on a Sunday. Total £46.

Eighteen months later they do replace the door, because they are having the porch redone and it makes sense to do both together. That is a perfectly good reason. What they did not do was spend £1,380 in a hurry on the strength of a £6.78 argument, and the eighteen months of waiting cost them approximately ten pounds of gas.

6. The honest verdict

Do not replace a front door to save energy. It saves under seven pounds a year and pays back in 177 years. A door is under one per cent of your external envelope and always will be.

If your door is draughty, fix the draught for about £15. Air leakage is a real cost and it is a different problem with a much cheaper answer.

If your concern is security, spend £30 on a TS007 3 star cylinder first. It addresses the commonest forced entry method on the door you already have, and you can do it this weekend.

And replace the door when it stops working or you want a different one. Both are legitimate. A door that shuts properly, keeps the weather out and looks like somewhere you want to live is worth having. Just buy it for that.

If you are buying

For a new doorset, ask whether the whole assembly is certified to PAS 24 rather than just the slab, and ask for the U-value in writing so you can check it against the 1.4 limit. If you are staying with the door you have, the cylinder is the thing to buy, and the only measurement you need is the distance from the centre of the cylinder to each face of the door. The same quoting habits show up on every other opening in the house, so it is worth knowing what bifold doors cost once they are fitted and what a garage door replacement runs to before one salesman prices all three together.

Frequently asked

Do composite doors really last longer than uPVC? Generally yes, and this is a better argument than the thermal one. A composite door has a solid timber or foam core with a GRP skin, so it does not flex and warp in the way a hollow uPVC panel can, and the colour is through the skin rather than a foil that can peel. Twenty five to thirty five years is commonly quoted against fifteen to twenty five for uPVC. That difference, spread across the price gap, is a far more honest case than six pounds of gas.

Does a new door need Building Regulations approval? Replacing a door is controlled work for energy purposes, so it either goes through building control or is fitted by an installer registered with a competent person scheme such as FENSA or Certass, who self-certifies it. Make sure your quote says which, because the certificate is what a conveyancer will ask for when you sell.

Will a better door cut my home insurance? Rarely as a direct discount, but policies frequently ask about lock standards and some require specific ones on final exit doors. The value is in not having a claim disputed rather than in a lower premium, and the way to find out is to read your own schedule rather than to ask the door salesman.
