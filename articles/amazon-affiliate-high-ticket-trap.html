Why Expensive Products Pay You Less: The Affiliate Maths Nobody Runs

22 August 2026 · 8 min read · Checked August 2026
The short version
  • Your scarce resource is clicks, not products. So the only number that matters is what one click to Amazon is worth.
  • Earnings per click equals click-to-order rate, times basket value, times category rate. Price sits inside only one of those three.
  • A £350 skincare set can pay more than a £2,500 television, because Amazon rates televisions at 2% in the US and pins them to the 3% catch-all here.
  • Five structural reasons expensive electronics lose, and the 24 hour cookie is only the first of them.
  • The sweet spot in 2026 is roughly £50 to £180, inside a 5% category, with low return rates.

The only equation that matters

Almost every new affiliate makes the same decision in the same order. They look for the most expensive thing they can plausibly write about, reason that one sale of a £3,000 item is worth a hundred sales of a £30 item, and build the site around it.

The arithmetic does not support it. Here is the equation the decision should actually be made on:

Earnings per click = click-to-order rate × basket value × category rate

Price appears in exactly one of those three terms, and the other two both move against you as the price goes up. Expensive items convert worse, because people research them for weeks. And Amazon rates expensive categories lowest, on purpose, because it believes you add no marginal value to a purchase the buyer was going to make anyway.

About that $500 laptop row. It is the highest number in the table and the most misleading one in this article. A 1% conversion rate on a $2,000 laptop assumes the buyer clicks your link and purchases within 24 hours. Real laptop consideration cycles run two to six weeks. In practice that 1% is closer to 0.2%. Returns on electronics are the highest in retail and reverse your commission months later. And the buyer has three other tabs open comparing prices. High-ticket electronics look best on a spreadsheet and perform worst in an account.

Five reasons expensive electronics lose

  1. The 24 hour cookie against a six week decision. Items added to basket inside the window stay attributed for up to 89 days, but the add-to-basket has to happen on day one. Nobody adds a £3,000 camera to their basket the first time they read about it.
  2. Amazon rates high-ticket categories lowest on purpose. Televisions 2%, consoles 1%, computers 2.5% on the US card. These are price-shopped commodities where Amazon has thin margin and believes you contributed nothing.
  3. Price shopping. Even when the reader buys, they frequently buy somewhere else. Expensive electronics are the most comparison-shopped category in retail.
  4. Returns reverse commissions, and they are deducted from future earnings. A single high-ticket return can wipe out a good month. The April 2026 rule requiring the item to be shipped and paid for within 180 days of the click makes backordered goods worse still.
  5. No basket effect. Nobody adds a second laptop. A kitchen, pet, hobby or car click routinely produces a multi-item basket, and Amazon pays on the whole qualifying cart.

The basket effect, and how it shrank in 2026

The basket effect is the real engine of Amazon affiliate income, and it is why a "how to set up a home gym" guide outperforms a review of one expensive treadmill. Amazon pays you on qualifying items the reader buys within the session, not only the item you linked.

It did shrink in April 2026. Halo commissions on non-linked basket items were cut to a fixed 1 to 2% in categories that previously paid 3 to 4% halo. Industry estimates put the revenue hit to high-volume review sites at 10 to 20%. The effect is still real and still the reason build-guide content beats single-product reviews. It is simply no longer free money.

Where the money actually is

Putting all of it together, the profile of a product worth building content around in 2026 looks like this:

  • Roughly £50 to £180. Expensive enough that the commission is not trivial, cheap enough that people buy the same day they research.
  • In a 5% UK category: kitchen, automotive, tools, home, furniture, books.
  • With strong brand search, because branded queries are the only search intent measurably gaining click-through under AI Overviews.
  • With low return rates. Kitchen and pet beat clothing and electronics on this by a wide margin.
  • That belongs in a set. If the natural next question is "what else do I need", you have a build guide rather than a review, and the basket rule works for you.

Keep a small number of high-ticket items in the mix, from cameras, coffee equipment or car parts. Those are what produce the occasional large payment. Just do not build the site on them.

Questions people ask

So expensive products are never worth writing about? They are worth writing about, they are just a bad foundation. The realistic shape is a site built on mid-priced repeat categories with a handful of high-ticket pages for the upside.

Does the 89 day rule save the high-ticket case? Only if the reader adds the item to their basket in the first 24 hours. That is the whole trick, and it is why good high-ticket content ends with a reason to add to basket now rather than a reason to keep researching.

What about the extended cookie some other programmes offer? That is a real advantage of non-Amazon programmes and one of the better reasons to diversify. Amazon's 24 hours is among the shortest in affiliate marketing.

Are these conversion rates real? They are modelled estimates, and this article says so on purpose. Anyone quoting exact conversion rates for products they do not sell is guessing. What holds up is the ordering, not the decimals.

The verdict

Stop choosing products by price. Choose them by price times rate times conversion, and the answer is almost always a mid-priced item in a category Amazon actually pays for, inside content that fills a basket. The expensive flagship review is the most satisfying page to write and one of the least profitable ones to own.
