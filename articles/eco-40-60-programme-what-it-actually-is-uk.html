Your washing machine's label describes one programme, and it is not the one you press

Ruhul Amin · Published 9 November 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked November 2026

There is a button on almost every modern washing machine that the entire energy label is built on, and it is not the cotton wash, the daily wash or the quick wash. It is a legally defined programme called eco 40-60, and once you know what the law actually requires it to be, the gap between the label and your bill stops being mysterious.

The short version

  • Eco 40-60 is defined in law as the programme able to clean normally soiled cotton laundry washable at 40C or 60C, together in the same washing cycle.
  • It is the only programme the label relates to. Regulation 2019/2014 says so in terms, and every other cycle on the dial is unmeasured.
  • The figure is a weighted average of three load sizes, at rated capacity, half and a quarter, so it does not describe any single wash you will ever do.
  • At 26.32p per kWh, a label figure of 50 kWh per 100 cycles is 13.16p a wash. A hotter or faster programme can be several times that.
  • Pressing eco costs nothing and the payback is immediate, on our illustrative figures about £52 a year against 200 washes. What it costs is time, and the label prints that too.

1. What eco 40-60 is, in law

Annex I of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/2014, read again on legislation.gov.uk on 9 November 2026, defines it at paragraph 11. Eco 40-60 means the name of the programme declared by the supplier as able to clean normally soiled cotton laundry declared to be washable at 40 degrees or 60 degrees, together in the same washing cycle, and to which the information on the energy label and in the product information sheet relates.

Read that last clause again, because it is the whole article. The information on the energy label relates to this programme. Not to the cotton 60 cycle, not to the synthetics cycle, not to the 30 minute quick wash. One programme.

The name is also not a temperature. It is a claim about what the programme can handle: a mixed load of things labelled 40 and things labelled 60, washed together, to a defined standard of cleanliness. The machine gets there however its designers chose, and in practice that usually means a lower temperature held for much longer, because heating water is where the energy goes.

Annex IV confirms the scope. The eco 40-60 programme is used for the measurement and calculation of energy consumption, the energy efficiency index, maximum temperature, water consumption, remaining moisture content, programme duration, washing efficiency, rinsing effectiveness, spin drying efficiency and airborne acoustical noise emissions in the spinning phase. All of it, measured concurrently, on that one cycle.

2. Why the number is an average of three washes

Here is the second thing that separates the label from your bill, and almost nobody knows it.

Annex I paragraph 13 defines weighted energy consumption for the washing cycle as the weighted average of the energy consumption for the eco 40-60 programme at rated washing capacity, and at half and at a quarter of the rated washing capacity, expressed in kilowatt hours per cycle. Annex IV adds that when measuring these parameters, the highest spin speed option for eco 40-60 shall be used at rated capacity, at half of the rated capacity and, where relevant, at a quarter.

So the number on the label is not the cost of a full load and not the cost of a half load. It is a blend of a full, a half and a quarter load. That is a sensible way to describe a machine, because nobody runs full loads every time, and it is a terrible way to predict what a particular wash costs.

The practical takeaway is the one the averaging is trying to nudge you towards anyway: a full load on eco is the cheapest wash the machine can do per kilogram of laundry, and the label's figure is already worse than that because a quarter load is baked into it. Washing more, less often, beats almost any other change you can make to how you use it.

For completeness, machines with a rated capacity of 3 kg or less are measured differently under the same annex, which is a detail that matters to almost nobody but is there in the law.

One further consequence of the averaging is worth spelling out, because it changes how you should compare two machines rather than how you use one. A large drum and a small drum are each measured against their own rated capacity, not against a common load. So a 10 kg machine and a 6 kg machine are both averaged over a full, a half and a quarter of their own capacity, and the 10 kg machine's quarter load is larger than the 6 kg machine's half. Comparing their label figures directly tells you which is more efficient at handling its own design load, not which is cheaper for the laundry you personally generate. If your washing never fills a 6 kg drum, the bigger machine's better looking number is describing washes you will never do.

3. What pressing eco is worth

The rate is 26.32p per kWh, the Ofgem cap electricity unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, and Ofgem notes there is no VAT on electricity between 1 October 2026 and 31 March 2027. When we checked on 9 November 2026, Ofgem had not yet announced the cap that starts on 1 January 2027, and the Commons Library reports forecasts of a rise of around 15 per cent in electricity unit prices, so treat every pence figure below as a floor rather than a promise. The water rate where it is relevant is £4.0945 per cubic metre on Severn Trent's 2026/27 metered rates: £2.5503 for water supply plus used water at £1.6255, which its scheme of charges bills on 95 per cent of the water supplied. The assumption is 200 washes a year.

The payback line is the honest one and it is the same shape as every other calculation in this series: cost divided by annual saving. Here the cost of the change is £0, because it is a button that already exists, and the saving on these illustrative figures is £52.64 a year, so the payback is immediate.

What eco costs is time, and the law makes the manufacturer tell you how much. Annex III requires the label to show the duration of the eco 40-60 programme at rated capacity, in hours and minutes, rounded to the nearest minute. It is printed next to the energy figure. We are not going to quote a typical duration here, because it varies by machine and we have not measured any, but you can read yours off the sticker before you buy.

4. A worked example

Hamid, Luton. Family of four, machine bought last year, roughly four washes a week, which is a little over 200 a year. He uses the 60 degree cotton programme for everything because it is the first one on the dial and it finishes in about an hour.

He checks the label still stuck inside the door. It gives a figure per 100 cycles for eco 40-60 and a duration considerably longer than an hour, which is why he has never used it.

On the illustrative figures above, 208 washes at 1.5 kWh is 312 kWh, or £82.12 a year at 26.32p. The same washes on eco at 0.5 kWh are 104 kWh, or £27.37. Moving his everyday washing to eco is worth around £55 a year, and it costs nothing, so the payback is immediate. But the duration is the real obstacle, and pretending otherwise would not help him. What makes it workable is when the machine runs rather than how long it takes: he starts it after breakfast and it is done by mid afternoon, and nobody is waiting for it.

The bigger win turns out to be the load size. He has been running four small washes a week because that is the rhythm the house fell into. Consolidating into three fuller ones takes him from 208 washes a year to about 156. On the eco figure that is 78 kWh, or £20.53 a year, against the £82.12 he started at. The programme change is worth about £55. The programme change plus the load change is worth about £62.

He keeps the quick wash for genuinely light loads, which is what it is for, and stops using it as a default. The label never described that cycle and never claimed to.

The verdict

Use eco 40-60 for normal laundry, because it is the only programme your machine's energy label describes and it costs nothing to select. On our illustrative figures that is worth about £52 a year against a 60 degree cotton cycle, with an immediate payback.

When you are buying, compare machines on the eco 40-60 figure and read the duration printed beside it, because that is the trade you are actually making. And remember the label number is already a blend of full, half and quarter loads, so running full loads beats the label rather than merely matching it.

Frequently asked

Is eco 40-60 a 40 degree wash or a 60 degree wash? Neither, as a temperature. The regulation defines it as the programme able to clean normally soiled cotton laundry declared washable at 40C or 60C together in the same cycle. How the machine achieves that is up to the manufacturer, and most do it with cooler water held for longer.

Why does eco take so much longer? Heating water is where most of the energy in a wash goes, so a lower temperature held for longer generally uses less energy than a higher one held briefly. The label has to print the duration at rated capacity so you can see the trade before you buy.

Does the energy label cover the quick wash? No. Regulation 2019/2014 says the information on the energy label and in the product information sheet relates to the eco 40-60 programme. Every other cycle on the dial is outside what the label measures, so a quick wash can use more or less energy than the label suggests and you have no official figure for it.

Is a full load really better than two half loads? On energy per kilogram of laundry, yes, and the regulation's own method hints at it: the label figure is a weighted average including a quarter load, so a consistently full load performs better than the printed number. Fewer, fuller washes also cut water use on a meter.
