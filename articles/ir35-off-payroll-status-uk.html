The Client Decides Your Status. You Only Get to Decide the Rate

The short version

  • Inside IR35 you pay the employer’s National Insurance out of your own rate. That is the whole cost, and it is 15% of pay above £417 a month in 2026/27.
  • On a £300 a day, 200 day contract worth £60,000, outside IR35 through your own company nets roughly £44,600. Inside IR35 through an umbrella nets roughly £40,500.
  • That gap is about £4,200 a year, or 7% of the contract value, and it buys you no extra employment rights worth having.
  • You do not decide. For medium and large clients the client makes the determination and must give you a Status Determination Statement with reasons. Only with a small client does your own company decide.
  • The number to negotiate is the assignment rate, not the status. If a role moves inside IR35, the honest ask is roughly 10% more on the rate to stand still.

1. Who actually decides, and what you are owed

The off-payroll working rules ask one question: if the contract between you and the end client were direct, ignoring your limited company, would it look like employment? If yes, the engagement is inside IR35 and is taxed broadly like employment.

Where the end client is a medium or large organisation in the private sector, or any public sector body, the client makes that determination. It must take reasonable care, and it must give you a Status Determination Statement setting out the conclusion and the reasons for it. If you disagree, there is a client-led disagreement process and the client must respond within 45 days.

Where the end client qualifies as a small company under the Companies Act test, the old rules apply and your own company decides its status, and carries the risk of getting it wrong. The thresholds for that test have moved in recent years, so check the current ones rather than assuming.

Two practical consequences. First, a blanket determination applied to every contractor without looking at individual contracts is not reasonable care, and it is worth saying so. Second, you are entitled to the reasons, in writing. Ask for them.

2. What it costs, on the same day rate

Here is a single contract priced three ways. Day rate £300, 200 billable days, so the contract is worth £60,000 to whoever is paying. All figures use the 2026/27 rates: personal allowance £12,570, basic rate 20% to £50,270, higher rate 40%; employee National Insurance 8% then 2% above £967 a week; employer National Insurance 15%; corporation tax small profits rate 19%; dividend rates 10.75% basic and 35.75% higher with a £500 allowance.

Read the three rows as a pair of comparisons, because each one tells you something different.

Row one against row two is the cost of IR35: £4,185 a year on this contract, or just under 7% of its value. That is what the status determination is worth in cash.

Row two against row three is the part that annoys contractors, and fairly. The take-home is identical. The difference is who funds the employer’s National Insurance. A permanent employee has it paid on top of their salary. An inside-IR35 contractor has it taken out of the rate they negotiated. Same tax treatment, none of the employment rights, and you are paying the employer’s share.

Inside IR35 you are taxed like an employee and paid like a contractor who has just been charged the employer’s bill. The rate is the only lever you have.

3. Where those numbers come from

Nobody should trust a take-home figure they cannot follow, so here is the arithmetic.

Outside IR35. £60,000 revenue less £2,000 of allowable expenses leaves £58,000. A director’s salary of £12,570 sits just inside the personal allowance and just below the employee National Insurance threshold, so it attracts no income tax and no employee NI. Employer NI is 15% on the part above £5,004 a year, which is £1,135. Company profit is then £44,295, under the £50,000 small profits threshold, so corporation tax is 19%, or £8,416. That leaves £35,879 of dividends. The first £500 is covered by the dividend allowance and the rest falls in the basic rate band at 10.75%, giving £3,803 of dividend tax. Net £44,646.

Inside IR35, umbrella. The £60,000 assignment rate has to cover the employer’s costs before any salary exists. Take off the umbrella’s margin of £1,200, then solve for the gross salary that leaves employer NI at 15% above £5,004 and the 0.5% Apprenticeship Levy. That gives a gross of £51,559, with £6,983 of employer NI and £258 of levy. Income tax on £51,559 is £8,056 and employee NI is £3,042. Net £40,461.

Two caveats. Umbrella margins vary and some are much higher than £1,200 a year, which comes straight off your net. And the outside-IR35 figure assumes you can genuinely take dividends, which means the company is trading properly, not a wrapper.

4. The only lever that works

You cannot argue your way out of a determination by saying you would rather be outside. What you can do is price it.

If £300 a day outside nets £44,646, the inside rate that nets the same is roughly £330 a day, because you have to cover the employer’s NI, the levy and the margin out of the uplift. As a rule of thumb, ask for about 10% more to move from outside to inside and stand still.

That is a concrete, defensible number to put to a client or agency, and it reframes the conversation from a legal argument you will lose into a commercial one you might win.

Worked example: Dev in Warrington

Dev has contracted at £300 a day outside IR35 for two years. At renewal the client, now a large group, reassesses the role as inside IR35 and issues a Status Determination Statement citing control over how the work is done and no genuine right of substitution.

What he loses by accepting at the same rate. £44,646 becomes £40,461, a fall of £4,185 a year.

What he asks for. £330 a day, a 10% uplift, restoring him to roughly where he was. Over 200 days that is £66,000 rather than £60,000.

What he checks first. The Status Determination Statement itself. If the reasoning is generic or applied across every contractor without looking at individual terms, that is not reasonable care, and he raises it through the disagreement process, to which the client must respond within 45 days.

What he does not do. Argue that he has always been outside, so he must still be outside. Status follows the working arrangements as they are now. If the client has tightened control, the determination may well be right, and the conversation that pays is the one about the rate.

5. Frequently asked

Can I just stay outside and take the risk? Where the client is medium or large, the decision is not yours, and the liability for getting it wrong sits with the fee-payer, not you. Operating against a determination is not an option that is open to you.

Does a substitution clause fix it? A genuine, unfettered right of substitution that you could actually use is a strong indicator of self-employment. A clause that exists on paper but the client would never accept is not.

Should I close my company if I go inside? Not necessarily. Many contractors take inside-IR35 work through an umbrella while keeping their company for outside-IR35 engagements. Keeping it dormant costs little.

Is an umbrella the only option inside IR35? No. Some clients operate agency PAYE directly. The take-home is broadly similar but there is no umbrella margin, so it is usually slightly better. Ask which is on offer.

6. The honest verdict

On a £60,000 contract, a move from outside to inside IR35 costs about £4,200 a year, or 7%. That is the whole story in one number, and it comes almost entirely from the employer’s National Insurance being charged to your rate instead of to the client.

So do two things. Read the Status Determination Statement and challenge it if the reasoning is generic, because you are entitled to reasons and to a response within 45 days. Then negotiate the rate, asking for around 10% to stand still.

What is not worth doing is treating the status as an identity. It follows the working arrangements, and those change. The rate is the part you can actually move.
