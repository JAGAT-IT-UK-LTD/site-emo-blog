A recorded course and a live class are different things to HMRC

Most advice on selling a course covers filming and pricing. The thing that actually catches UK sellers is a VAT rule that turns on whether a human being is involved in delivering it.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Automated learning is a digital service, per HMRC. Pre-recorded, no human involvement, it counts.
  • A live class is not. HMRC: education delivered by a person over the internet, such as a webinar, is not an electronically supplied service.
  • That distinction decides your VAT. Sell a digital service to an EU consumer and you must register for Non-Union VAT MOSS, or in each EU country you sell into.
  • The UK VAT threshold is £90,000 of taxable turnover in the last 12 months, per GOV.UK.
  • Card fees still come off the top: Stripe's UK standard rate, read 12 September 2026, is 1.5% plus 20p on standard UK cards.

1. The rule almost nobody mentions

HMRC's guidance on VAT for digital services sold to consumers draws a line that is not the one people expect. Not price, not subject, not platform. It is about whether a person is involved in delivering it. HMRC's own words:

  • Services given by a person. "Education, training, or a similar service delivered by a person over the internet or an electronic network (such as a webinar), is not considered to be an electronically supplied service because an actual person is involved in the delivery." Those supplies are outside the digital services rules entirely.
  • Services given through automated learning. "Automated learning does not have human involvement and is therefore a digital service."

The definition underneath is that "electronically supplied" covers services automatically delivered over the internet where there is minimal or no human intervention.

HMRC applies the same test to marking. An exam that a computer scores automatically is a digital service. The same exam marked by an assessor is not.

So a pre-recorded course with automatic access on payment is a digital service. The same content taught live is not. Two products that look identical in a basket sit on opposite sides of a VAT rule.

2. Why that matters the moment you sell abroad

For UK sellers the consequence lands on cross-border sales. HMRC: if you make supplies of digital services to consumers in the EU, the place of supply is where the consumer is located, and you must either register for the Non-Union VAT MOSS scheme in an EU member state, or register for VAT in each EU member state where you supply.

Read that again, because the word that is missing is "threshold". The UK's £90,000 VAT registration threshold is a UK rule. It does not shield you from the EU obligation on digital services.

For UK consumers the familiar rules apply: supplies of digital services to UK consumers are liable to UK VAT, and whether you must register turns on the UK threshold, which per GOV.UK is total taxable turnover over £90,000 in the last 12 months, or an expectation of exceeding it in the next 30 days.

Two practical consequences most course sellers never consider:

  • Teaching live can be simpler than recording. If a live cohort keeps you outside the digital services rules, that is a real saving, and the opposite of the usual advice about scaling.
  • Selling through a platform can change who the supplier is. HMRC covers digital portals, platforms and marketplaces separately. If a marketplace is treated as supplying the customer, the VAT obligation can sit with them. Check what your platform says.

3. The costs before any of that

Tomasz, Ipswich. A pre-recorded course at £120, sold to UK buyers, no platform cut because he hosts it himself.

  • Card fee at Stripe's UK standard rate, 1.5 per cent plus 20p: £1.80 plus 20p = £2.00, or 1.67 per cent. Note how much kinder the fixed 20p is at £120 than at £5.
  • Net per sale: £118, before hosting and before tax.
  • To reach the £90,000 UK VAT threshold takes 750 sales in twelve months. A long way off for most people, which is why the UK threshold is the wrong thing to watch.
  • To trigger the EU obligation takes one sale to a consumer in an EU country, if the course is automated.

That gap, 750 against 1, is the most useful number here. The obligation people plan for is the furthest away. The one that arrives first is the one nobody mentions.

Who this does not help

If a course is a few hundred pounds a year of income, the EU obligation is not worth taking on. Say Tomasz sells 100 copies at £120 in a year and five of those buyers are in the EU. That is £600 of EU sales, and the price of keeping them is a Non-Union MOSS registration in a member state plus a quarterly return for as long as he trades, or a VAT registration in every country he sells into. Blocking EU checkout costs him £600 of turnover and removes the obligation entirely. For a seller at that scale that is usually the right trade, and it is the opposite of what a course about selling courses will tell you.

It also does not help anyone whose plan is to film once and sell everywhere. The two attractive things about a pre-recorded course, that it needs no human and that it sells across borders, are exactly the two facts that put it inside the digital services rules. A live cohort, which scales worse, stays outside them. Pick which problem you would rather have before you film, not after.

For a small seller the honest answer is one of three: keep sales to UK buyers and block EU checkout; teach live so it is not a digital service; or sell through a platform that takes on the supply. Choosing none of them and hoping is not an option.

The verdict

Before you film anything, decide which side of HMRC's line your course sits on, because the answer changes what you owe and where. Automated and pre-recorded is a digital service. Delivered live by a person is not.

Then decide who you will sell to. A UK only, pre-recorded course is straightforward and stays under the UK threshold for a long time. The moment an EU consumer can check out, an automated course brings an EU VAT obligation with no threshold to hide behind.

None of this makes courses a bad business. It makes the first hour of admin worth more than the first hour of filming, which is not what anyone selling a course about selling courses will tell you.

Frequently asked

Is a live Zoom course a digital service? Per HMRC, no. Education, training or a similar service delivered by a person over the internet, such as a webinar, is not an electronically supplied service, because an actual person is involved in the delivery.

Do I need to register for UK VAT to sell a course? Only if you meet the UK rules. GOV.UK says you must register if taxable turnover for the last 12 months goes over £90,000, or you expect to exceed it in the next 30 days.

What if I sell one course to someone in Ireland? If it is an automated digital service, HMRC says the place of supply is where the consumer is, and you must register for Non-Union VAT MOSS in an EU member state or for VAT in each member state you supply. No EU threshold protects a single sale.
