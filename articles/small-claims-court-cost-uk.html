Chasing £280 Costs £35. Leaving One Box Empty on the Form Costs £10,000

Ruhul Amin · Published 13 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The fee to start a money claim is published, banded, and much smaller than most people expect at the bottom end. It is also not the only fee, and there is one line in the official guidance that turns a small claim into the most expensive mistake in the civil courts.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The issue fee is banded by claim value. £35 up to £300, rising to £455 for claims between £5,000.01 and £10,000.
  • Above £10,000 it goes percentage. From £10,000.01 to £200,000 the fee is 5% of the claim. Over £200,000 it is a flat £10,000.
  • The fee is charged on the amount plus interest, not on the bare debt. Adding interest can push you into the next band.
  • Leaving the amount claimed blank on the paper form costs £10,000. That is stated on the GOV.UK fees page in one sentence.
  • The issue fee is not the last fee. GOV.UK says plainly you may have to pay more later, for a hearing and for enforcement.
  • Help with Fees exists for low income or certain benefits, via form EX160, and you apply before you make the claim.

1. The full fee ladder

You pay a court fee when you make the claim. It is based on the amount you are claiming, plus any interest. These are the published bands:

Up to £300: £35

£300.01 to £500: £50

£500.01 to £1,000: £70

£1,000.01 to £1,500: £80

£1,500.01 to £3,000: £115

£3,000.01 to £5,000: £205

£5,000.01 to £10,000: £455

£10,000.01 to £200,000: 5% of the claim

More than £200,000: £10,000

Two features of that table are worth pausing on.

The bottom of it is cheap and almost nobody knows it. £35 to put a £280 debt in front of a court is less than most people assume, and it is often less than the cost of continuing to chase someone by phone. If a tradesman has kept a £250 deposit, the fee to make that a formal claim is £35.

The jump at £5,000 is brutal. Going from £5,000 to £5,000.01 takes the fee from £205 to £455. That is a £250 increase for one penny of extra claim. If your claim is genuinely worth around £5,000, the arithmetic of whether to include a marginal disputed item changes a lot at that line. The same shape appears at every band edge, just smaller.

And remember the fee is calculated on the claim plus interest. A £2,950 debt with £120 of interest is a £3,070 claim, which crosses from the £115 band into the £205 band. Work out the interest before you decide which band you are in, not after.

For claims over £10,000, 5% is calculated by multiplying the claim by 0.05 and rounding down to the nearest penny. If you claim online, the fee is calculated for you.

2. The blank box that costs £10,000

Here is the sentence, and it is easy to read past on the GOV.UK page: "If you leave the 'amount claimed' blank, the fee is £10,000."

This only applies to the paper claim form, because you cannot make an online claim without an amount. If you do not know the exact figure you owe yourself the effort of estimating it. GOV.UK's own instruction is to estimate the amount and pay the fee for that estimate. Its example: if you estimate between £3,000.01 and £5,000, you pay £205.

The logic behind the £10,000 default is that an unlimited claim is treated as the largest possible claim. The effect on a person filling in a form at a kitchen table is that a blank box turns a £1,400 dispute into the maximum court fee in the system. Before paying any of it, check whether the fee is payable at all, because Help with Fees has published income and capital limits that write the fee off in full for a lot of households.

The rule to take away: never send an N1 with that field empty. An estimate you can justify is always better than nothing, and the fee follows the estimate.

3. The fees that arrive later

GOV.UK says it in one line and then moves on: "You may have to pay more fees later on, for example, if there's a court hearing or you need to get a judgment enforced."

That sentence contains the two most commonly overlooked costs in the whole process.

A hearing fee. If the claim is defended and goes to a hearing rather than settling or being decided on paper, there is a further fee at that stage.

Enforcement. Winning is not the same as being paid. If the other side ignores the judgment you have to enforce it, and enforcement has its own fees. This is the step people do not plan for, and it is where a lot of successful small claims quietly end.

There is a step before any hearing that costs nothing and is worth taking seriously: mediation. The Small Claims Mediation Service is part of the process for defended money claims and it can end the matter without a hearing at all.

The one piece of good news: GOV.UK states that you may be able to claim the fees back if you win the case. May, not will, and it depends on the outcome and on the other side actually paying. Budget as though you will not get it back and treat recovery as a bonus.

4. Worked example: Bethan in Swansea chases £1,480

Bethan paid a contractor £1,480 for work that was never finished. Letters have gone unanswered for four months. She wants to know what going to court costs.

Step one, the claim value. £1,480 of principal. She is also entitled to claim interest, and if that comes to £70 the claim is £1,550.

Step two, the band. £1,480 alone sits in the £1,000.01 to £1,500 band at £80. With the £70 interest it becomes £1,550, which crosses into the £1,500.01 to £3,000 band at £115.

So the interest costs her £35 in extra fee to recover £70. Still worth doing, but only just, and that is the sort of calculation nobody tells you to make. If the interest had been £15 rather than £70, adding it would have cost her £35 to gain £15, and she should not have bothered.

Step three, the realistic total. £115 to issue. If it is defended and reaches a hearing there is a further fee. If she wins and he does not pay, enforcement costs more again. Her downside is not £115. It is £115 plus a hearing fee plus enforcement, against a debtor who may have nothing.

Step four, the question that actually decides it. Not "can I win" but "can I collect". A judgment against a dissolved company or someone with no assets is a piece of paper. That question is worth more thought than the fee table.

And before any of it, if she paid the £1,480 by credit card, Section 75 may give her a route that costs nothing at all. Check that first.

5. Help with Fees, and getting the fee back

You may be able to get help with fees if you have a low income or are on certain benefits.

Online: apply for help with fees before you make the court claim. You get a Help with Fees reference number, which you use when you make the claim.

By post: if you apply by post you must make the claim by post too, and do both at the same time. That means form EX160 for help with fees and form N1 for the claim, sent together to the Civil National Business Centre in Northampton.

The ordering matters. Applying for help after you have already paid is not the process described, so check eligibility before you issue.

On payment methods: online claims are paid by credit or debit card. On the paper form you can pay by card by sending a covering letter asking the court to ring you for the payment, or by postal order or cheque payable to HM Courts and Tribunals Service.

The honest verdict

For small sums the money claim system is much cheaper to use than its reputation suggests. Thirty five pounds for a claim up to £300, and £80 up to £1,500, is not a barrier for most people chasing money they are owed, and the online service does the fee arithmetic for you. If you have been letting a few hundred pounds go because you assumed court was for other people, the fee table is the argument against that assumption.

What the fee table hides is everything after issue. The published number is the entry price, not the cost of the process, and the sentence about hearing fees and enforcement is doing a great deal of quiet work. Assume you will not get the fee back, and ask yourself whether the person you are suing has anything to take, because a judgment you cannot enforce has cost you the fee and bought you nothing.

Two concrete things. Never leave the amount claimed blank on a paper form, because that single omission is priced at £10,000. And before you issue anything, check whether a card payment, a chargeback or a free mediation route gets you there without a fee at all.

Frequently asked

How much does it cost to claim £500? A claim of exactly £500 sits in the £300.01 to £500 band, so the fee is £50. A penny more and it is £70. Remember the fee is based on the claim plus interest.

Do I get the fee back if I win? GOV.UK says you may be able to claim the fees back if you win the case. That is not a guarantee, and it still depends on the other side paying.

Is there a fee to defend a claim? This guide covers the fees for making a claim. Responding to one is a different process with its own rules, and GOV.UK has a separate guide on responding to a court claim for money.

What if I cannot afford the fee? Apply for Help with Fees using form EX160 before you make the claim. If you apply by post, send EX160 and the N1 claim form together.
