1. £25 and £18: the figures and their one condition

The Energy Saving Trust's page on radiator reflector panels, updated 31 October 2025 and read again on 5 September 2026, is short and unusually direct. Reflector panels, also sold as radiator foil, are thin sheets of foil or reflective material, sometimes with a thin insulating layer, that sit between a radiator and the wall and bounce heat back into the room instead of letting it go into the wall. A roll costs around £25, and fitting panels on uninsulated external walls can save £18 a year in Great Britain and £20 in Northern Ireland, cutting about 60 kg of carbon.

Divide £25 by £18 and the payback is 1.4 years, about 17 months. That is the second shortest payback on this site after a chimney draught excluder, and it comes with the smallest annual saving. The two facts belong together: the foil is worth doing because it is nearly free, not because it is a big saving.

The condition is in the sentence itself, and the Trust repeats it three times on the page: uninsulated external walls. Its answer to the question of whether to put foil behind every radiator is no, because a radiator on an internal wall warms the wall between two rooms and that heat stays in the house. The rest of this article is about that condition, because it decides whether the £25 is the best-value purchase on this site or a waste of £25.

2. Which walls qualify: external, uninsulated, and how to tell

External is easy: the wall has the outside on the other side of it. In most houses that is the wall under the window, which is also where most radiators were put, because a radiator under a window counteracts the cold draught off the glass. So most radiators are on external walls, and the first test passes for most of them.

Uninsulated is the harder test. Houses built after about 1920 usually have cavity walls, and if the cavity has been filled the wall is insulated and the foil has little to reflect back that the wall would have lost. Houses built after about 1990 were built with wall insulation. Houses built before 1920 usually have solid brick or stone walls with no insulation at all, and those are where the £18 lives. The tests are the same ones in our cavity wall article: brick pattern, wall thickness at a door reveal, the EPC, a borescope hole, or a hand on the wall on a cold morning. A wall that is cold to the touch behind the radiator in January is a wall that is taking the heat.

The honest map of a typical house is therefore: a pre-1920 terrace or semi, foil behind every radiator on an outside wall; a 1930s to 1980s house with unfilled cavities, the same, and read the cavity article; a filled-cavity or post-1990 house, foil behind nothing, and spend the £25 on draught strip instead.

The point of the last column is that reflector foil is never the whole answer to a cold wall. On an unfilled cavity it is a £25 down payment on a £2,200 job that pays back in eleven years. On a solid wall it is the affordable substitute for a job that never pays back on bills and is worth doing for other reasons. On a wall that is already insulated it is unnecessary, and knowing that saves you the £25 and the afternoon.

4. What to buy and how to fit it

Buy the real thing. The Trust is blunt that aluminium kitchen foil will not work: it is hard to keep in place and has none of the insulating layer or the design of a purpose-made panel. The two products that do work are a roll of foil-faced insulation you cut to size and stick to the wall, and rigid or semi-rigid panels that clip onto the radiator brackets and hang behind it. Rolls are cheaper per radiator; clip-on panels are quicker, cleaner and come with you if you move.

Fitting a roll. Measure each radiator on an external wall, turn the heating off and let the radiator cool, cut the foil slightly smaller than the radiator so it does not show, and fix it to the wall with the adhesive backing or double-sided tape, sliding it down behind the radiator from the top. Some rolls come with fixing strips. Do not block the gap at the bottom of the radiator or the space behind it; the radiator needs air to move over its back face, and a panel pressed against the fins is worse than no panel.

Fitting clip-on panels. They hook over the brackets and hang in the gap, no adhesive, five minutes a radiator. This is the renter's version: the Trust says clip-on panels are easy to install and remove, and suggests checking with the landlord anyway.

If the radiators are off the wall for decorating, fit the panels then; it is the only time the job is easy on a double panel radiator.

5. Worked example: Gareth in Swansea

Three-bed 1900s solid-walled terrace, gas central heating, seven radiators: four on external walls under windows, three on internal walls

Gareth has seen radiator foil in the DIY shop and wants to know whether to do all seven.

The wall test. The house is pre-1920 with alternating brick headers showing on the front: solid walls, uninsulated. The four radiators under the windows qualify. The three on internal walls do not, on the Energy Saving Trust's own advice.

The sum. One £25 roll covers the four external-wall radiators with some left over. Saving on the Trust's figure: £18 a year. Payback 17 months. Over ten years, £180 for £25.

What he does not do. Buy a second roll for the internal walls, which would be £25 for nothing. Tape up kitchen foil, which would be down by February.

What he does next. Reads the solid wall article, learns that the proper fix is £12,000 and pays back in 80 years for a terrace, and files it under comfort and grants. Spends the £25 he saved on the internal walls on draught strip for the sash windows, which pays back faster than the foil did.

6. The honest verdict

If your radiators sit on solid walls or unfilled cavity walls, buy one roll of purpose-made reflector foil or a set of clip-on panels, fit them behind the radiators on outside walls only, and you have spent about £25 for £18 a year on the Energy Saving Trust's figures, paid back in 17 months and then free for as long as the radiator lasts. It is the cheapest insulation on this site and, on the right wall, the fastest to pay.

If your outside walls are insulated, or the radiator is on an internal wall, do not buy it. The Energy Saving Trust says it is not worth fitting there and the arithmetic agrees. And whatever the wall, do not use kitchen foil; it is the one version that costs less than £25 and returns less than nothing.

Frequently asked

Do radiator reflector panels actually work? Yes, on radiators fixed to uninsulated external walls, where the Energy Saving Trust says they can save £18 a year in Great Britain and £20 in Northern Ireland for about £25 a roll. They reflect heat that would otherwise go into a cold outside wall back into the room. On internal walls the Trust says they are not worth fitting, because the heat stays in the house anyway.

How much do radiator reflector panels save? £18 a year in Great Britain on Energy Saving Trust figures, page updated 31 October 2025, for a home with radiators on uninsulated external walls. At around £25 for a roll that is a payback of about 17 months. It is the smallest annual saving of any measure the Trust prices and one of the fastest to pay back.

Can I use kitchen foil behind a radiator? No. The Energy Saving Trust says aluminium kitchen foil will not work: it is difficult to keep in place and lacks the insulating layer and design of a purpose-made panel. Buy a roll of proper radiator reflector foil from a DIY store or clip-on panels that hang from the radiator brackets.

Should I put reflector foil behind every radiator? No. Fit it only behind radiators on external walls that are not insulated, which in practice means solid-walled pre-1920 houses and later houses with unfilled cavities. Behind radiators on internal walls, or on insulated external walls, it does nothing measurable. If the cavity is unfilled, the foil is the £25 version of a £2,200 job that pays back in about eleven years.
