A Liner Costs More Than the Stove. Work Out How Many Evenings You Actually Light It

Relining a chimney for a stove runs 1,200 to 3,000 pounds depending on grade and access. Before you spend it, count the evenings a year you genuinely light a fire, because for a lot of households the correct answer is to cap the flue and stop paying to maintain a hole in the roof.

The short version

  • 316 grade stainless liner, 1,200 to 2,000 pounds fitted on a straightforward two storey stack. 904 grade is 1,700 to 3,000.
  • 904 costs about 40 per cent more and lasts roughly twice as long in real use, which makes it the cheaper of the two per year on any chimney burning wood regularly.
  • Capping and ventilating a chimney you do not use costs 250 to 450 pounds and removes the maintenance, the draught and the water ingress in one go.
  • Approved Document J is the rule that decides what you need, and a solid fuel appliance installation also requires a carbon monoxide alarm in the same room.
  • Cost per year is the test. A 904 liner over 25 years is about 94 pounds a year. If you light the fire six evenings a winter, that is roughly 15 pounds an evening before you buy any wood.

1. When you actually need a liner

A chimney liner is a flexible stainless steel tube dropped down the masonry flue and connected to the appliance at the bottom and a cowl at the top. It does three jobs: it gives the flue gases a smooth, correctly sized path so the fire draws properly, it stops acidic condensates soaking into the brickwork, and it isolates the flue from any cracks in the old parging.

You need one in three situations. When you are fitting a stove to an old masonry chimney that has never been lined, which is most pre 1965 houses. When the existing liner has failed, which shows as smoke or smells appearing in an upstairs room, or as staining on the chimney breast. And when a smoke test fails, which is the check a HETAS installer will do before signing anything off.

You do not automatically need one for an open fire in a sound, swept, correctly sized flue. That is worth saying because liners are sometimes sold as a blanket requirement and they are not.

Approved Document J is the governing guidance in England and it is what the installer is working to. The same document is why fitting a solid fuel appliance brings a carbon monoxide alarm requirement in the room with the appliance. That alarm costs about 25 pounds and it is not the part to economise on.

2. 316 against 904, and why the cheaper one is usually dearer

Liners come in two common stainless grades and the numbers refer to the alloy, not the thickness.

316 grade is the standard product. It handles the conditions produced by well seasoned wood burned hot in a modern stove. It struggles with acidic condensate, which is what you get from damp wood, smokeless fuel, slow overnight burns and any chimney that runs cool. Real world life on a regularly used stove is commonly twelve to fifteen years.

904 grade contains substantially more nickel and chromium and is far more resistant to acid attack. It is the grade recommended for smokeless fuels and for anything burned in an appliance that is regularly damped down. Life of twenty five years or more is realistic, and most manufacturers offer a longer guarantee to match.

The price difference is roughly 40 per cent. The life difference is roughly double. That arithmetic means 904 is cheaper per year for anyone burning more than occasionally, and the exception is a household that lights the fire a handful of times a winter with dry hardwood, where 316 will outlive the owner's interest in the fireplace.

One thing that matters more than the grade: how the liner is installed. It must be continuous, correctly sized to the appliance outlet rather than to the old flue, and backfilled or insulated so it runs hot enough to avoid condensing. A 904 liner fitted badly will fail before a 316 fitted properly.

If you light the fire six evenings a winter, the liner route is costing roughly 30 pounds an evening in fixed costs. Some people will look at that and think it is worth every penny, which is a completely reasonable position. The point is to know the number rather than discover it.

4. Capping off, the option nobody quotes for

An unused chimney is not neutral. It is a permanently open hole from a room to the sky. It pulls heated air out of the house all winter, it lets rain into the stack, and in a house with a modern extractor fan it can even reverse and bring soot smells down.

Decommissioning properly means three things and all three matter. A cowl or cap at the top that keeps rain out but still allows a small amount of airflow. A vent at the bottom, usually a small grille in the blocked fireplace opening. And the two together, because capping without venting traps moisture inside the stack and is how damp on a chimney breast starts.

Done properly that is 250 to 450 pounds and the chimney never costs anything again. It is reversible too, in the sense that a future owner can take the cap off, sweep it and reline, though they will have to pay to do so.

The honest counterweight: a working fireplace is a selling feature in some markets and a period house without one can feel wrong. That is a real consideration and it is not measurable in pounds. What is measurable is the 179 pounds a year, and you should weigh the two against each other rather than pretending only one exists.

5. Worked example: the Gallagher cottage in Otley

Siobhan Gallagher has an 1870s stone cottage in Otley with two chimneys. The sitting room has a multifuel stove fitted by the previous owner in 2009. The second flue serves a bedroom fireplace that has not been lit in twenty years.

A sweep fails the sitting room flue on a smoke test. Quotes come in at 1,780 pounds for 316 grade and 2,540 pounds for 904, both including the cowl and a new register plate.

She burns smokeless because Otley sits in a smoke control area, and smokeless is exactly the fuel that eats 316 grade. On a thirteen year life the 316 is 137 pounds a year. On twenty five years the 904 is 102. She fits the 904, which costs 760 pounds more today and saves about 35 pounds a year for the next quarter century.

The bedroom flue is a different question. Relining it would be another 1,600 pounds to serve a fireplace nobody wants to use. She caps and vents it for 320 pounds. That stops the draught into a bedroom she had been closing the door on, and removes one of the two sweeps she was paying for each year, saving another 85 pounds annually.

Total spend 2,860 pounds. The decision that saved the most money was the one where she bought nothing at all.

Frequently asked

Can I fit a liner myself? Physically, with two people and a roof ladder, yes. Legally you then need building control to inspect and sign it off, because a solid fuel installation is notifiable work. Using a HETAS registered installer self certifies it and produces the certificate a buyer's solicitor will ask for. The saving from doing it yourself is smaller than it looks once you add the building control fee.

Does my house insurance care? Many policies require an annual sweep certificate for a solid fuel appliance, and a claim after a chimney fire can be refused without one. Check your wording. The sweep is not optional in the way people treat it.

Will a liner make the stove burn better? Usually yes, and noticeably. A correctly sized liner gives a much better draw than an oversized masonry flue, which is why stoves that smoke into the room often stop doing it once lined. That is a comfort gain rather than a money one.

What about a flue that serves a gas fire? Different rules and a different trade. Gas work is Gas Safe registered territory, not HETAS, and the liner specification is different. Do not let a solid fuel installer touch a gas appliance.

The honest verdict

If you use the fire regularly and you burn smokeless or damp down overnight, fit 904 grade. At about 94 pounds a year against 123 for 316, the expensive liner is the cheap one.

If you light it a few times a winter, cap and vent instead. Three hundred pounds once removes a draught, a sweep bill and a maintenance liability, and nobody who has done it has ever written to say they regret it.
