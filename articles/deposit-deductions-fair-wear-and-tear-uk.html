Deposit deductions and fair wear and tear: what a landlord can actually keep

Ruhul Amin · Published 9 November 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked November 2026

A landlord can make deposit deductions for damage, unpaid rent or cleaning, but not for fair wear and tear. If you disagree, the deposit scheme will decide for free, and an unprotected deposit can cost the landlord up to three times its value. The tenancy type changed on 1 May 2026, and here is what GOV.UK, the law and the schemes say today.

The short version

  • Assured periodic tenancies replaced assured shorthold tenancies on 1 May 2026, and the deposit protection duty applies to an APT.
  • The deposit is capped at 5 weeks' rent when the yearly rent is under £50,000, and 6 weeks' rent at £50,000 or more.
  • Fair wear and tear is not a deduction. The Deposit Protection Service says a claim should not be used to make upgrades at the tenant's cost.
  • 30 days to protect it, 10 days to return it once you and the landlord have agreed how much you get back.
  • The scheme runs a free dispute resolution service, both sides submit evidence, and GOV.UK states the decision is final.
  • If it was never protected, a court must order at least the deposit amount and up to three times it, plus repayment, within 14 days of the order.
  • The court fee is £387 on Form N208 and you can claim it back from the landlord if you win. On a £1,200 deposit the maximum award is £3,600.

1. What changed on 1 May 2026

GOV.UK's tenancy deposit protection guide, read again on 9 November 2026, opens with the change. Your landlord must put your deposit in a government approved tenancy deposit scheme if you rent your home on an assured periodic tenancy, and APTs replaced assured shorthold tenancies on 1 May 2026.

In England and Wales the deposit can be registered with one of three: the Deposit Protection Service, MyDeposits, including deposits that were held by Capita, or the Tenancy Deposit Scheme. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate schemes.

There is a detail in that opening paragraph worth pulling out, because it is the one exception. If you do not rent on an APT, GOV.UK says your landlord can accept valuable items, its examples being a car or a watch, as a deposit instead of money, and those items will not be protected by a scheme. If anyone suggests an arrangement like that, understand that you are outside the protection this whole article describes.

Two other things are protected that people assume are not. A holding deposit, the money you pay to hold a property before signing, does not have to be protected while it is a holding deposit, but GOV.UK is explicit that once you become a tenant it becomes a deposit and the landlord must protect it. And a deposit paid by someone else, such as a parent or a rent deposit scheme, must still go into a TDP scheme.

How much can they ask for? The cap comes from the Tenant Fees Act 2019 and it still applies after 1 May 2026. GOV.UK's guide for tenants on assured periodic tenancies says the deposit can be up to 5 weeks' rent if the rent for the year is less than £50,000, and up to 6 weeks' rent at £50,000 or more. On £1,000 a month, that is £12,000 a year, or about £230.77 a week, so the most a landlord can take is about £1,153.85.

The same page says a holding deposit can be up to one week's rent, and that from 1 May 2026 a landlord must not ask for or accept rent before you both sign the tenancy agreement. After signing, the usual maximum in advance on a monthly rent is one month's rent.

2. The two deadlines and the information you are owed

Thirty days to protect. Your landlord or letting agent must put the deposit in the scheme within 30 days of getting it.

Ten days to return. At the end of the tenancy, the landlord must return your deposit within 10 days of you both agreeing how much you will get back. If you are in dispute, the deposit stays protected in the scheme until the issue is sorted out, which is the single most useful fact in this article: a disputed deposit is not sitting in the landlord's account.

Within those same 30 days the landlord must also tell you a specific list of things, and it is worth knowing the list because a landlord who has not sent it has not complied. GOV.UK sets it out: the address of the rented property, how much deposit you have paid, how the deposit is protected, the name and contact details of the scheme and its dispute resolution service, the landlord's or agency's name and contact details, the name and contact details of any third party that paid the deposit, why they would keep some or all of the deposit, how to apply to get it back, what to do if you cannot get hold of the landlord at the end of the tenancy, and what to do if there is a dispute.

That seventh item does a great deal of work and nobody reads it at the time. The landlord has to state, at the start, the grounds on which they would keep any of your money. At the end of the tenancy, a deduction that does not correspond to anything in that document is a good deal harder to justify in front of an adjudicator.

3. What counts as fair wear and tear, and what a landlord can deduct

Fair wear and tear is the decline you would expect from a home being lived in normally. The Deposit Protection Service, one of the three schemes, uses the legal wording: reasonable use of the premises by the tenant and the ordinary operation of natural forces.

So a landlord can deduct for damage, for unpaid rent and for cleaning back to the standard at the start. A landlord cannot charge you for things simply getting older.

The DPS lists what its adjudicators weigh. Length of tenancy matters, because a longer stay brings more natural wear. Who lived there matters, because more wear is expected from a family with children. And the age and quality of the item matters, so a cheap or old carpet is not the tenant's fault for wearing out.

The key idea is betterment. The DPS says a claim should not be used as an opportunity to make upgrades at the tenant's cost. Its own example is claiming the full price of a new carpet after a tenant has lived there for several years.

Usually fair wear and tearUsually a fair deduction
Carpet flattened or faded after years of normal useA burn, a large stain or a pet tear in the carpet
Light scuffs on walls and doorsHoles, gouges or paint you added without permission
Paint fading in sunlightCleaning when the home is left dirtier than at the start
An old appliance wearing outRent you did not pay

The table is a guide, not a rule. The DPS says outcomes depend on the evidence. A check in report signed by both sides, or backed by date stamped photographs, carries weight. One that is neither is, in the DPS's words, unlikely to carry sufficient evidential weight.

4. The free service, and the penalty

Free dispute resolution. GOV.UK states that your TDP scheme offers a free dispute resolution service if you disagree with your landlord about how much deposit should be returned. You do not have to use it, but if you do, both you and the landlord have to agree to it, you will both be asked to provide evidence, and the decision made about your deposit will be final.

Final cuts both ways, which is why it deserves a moment's thought rather than an instant yes. But it is free, it is run by the body already holding the money, and the alternative is a county court. For most disputes about whether a carpet mark is damage or wear, it is plainly the right route.

There is a separate route if the landlord has vanished. GOV.UK says you can raise a dispute to get your deposit back if you cannot contact your landlord and the deposit is held by one of the approved schemes, and the scheme will refund it if the dispute resolution service agrees. It adds that there may be a limit on the time you have to raise a dispute, so contact the scheme as soon as possible rather than after a few months of unanswered emails.

Now the penalty. If the deposit was never protected when it should have been, you can apply to your local county court. GOV.UK gives the mechanics: apply using Form N208, the court fee is £387, and you can claim that back from the landlord if you win, with fee remission available if you claim certain benefits or have a low income.

The three times figure is GOV.UK's: the court may order the landlord to pay you up to three times the deposit within 14 days of making the order, and separately order them to repay it to you or pay it into a scheme's bank account within 14 days. The word is up to, and the court decides, so treat the table as a ceiling rather than an expectation.

There is a floor as well. Section 214(4) of the Housing Act 2004 says the sum must be not less than the amount of the deposit and not more than three times it. So on a £1,200 deposit the award runs from £1,200 to £3,600, on top of getting the deposit back. The Renters' Rights Act 2025 amended section 214 from 1 May 2026 so it refers to assured tenancies rather than shorthold ones, and legislation.gov.uk shows no change to that one to three times range.

Run it as a payback and it is the most lopsided calculation in this series: a cost of £387 against a ceiling of £3,600 on a £1,200 deposit is a return of more than nine to one, and the fee itself is reclaimable. GOV.UK also notes the court may decide you do not have to leave the property when the tenancy ends if your landlord did not use a scheme when they should have.

5. A worked example

Nadia, Doncaster. Two year tenancy ending, deposit of £1,150, and a proposed deduction of £640 for a stained living room carpet and a scuffed internal door.

She does three things in order. First she checks the deposit is protected, by contacting the scheme directly rather than asking the landlord. It is, and it was protected inside the 30 days.

Second she digs out the prescribed information sent at the start of the tenancy, the document with the list of ten items. The grounds it states for keeping money are rent arrears, damage beyond fair wear and tear, and cleaning to the standard at the start. That gives her the vocabulary the adjudicator will use, and it tells her the door scuff is arguable and the carpet stain is the real fight.

Third she uses the free dispute resolution service, which the landlord agrees to. Both submit evidence: her dated check in photographs against his end of tenancy report. The adjudicator allows a proportion for the carpet, reflecting that it was already four years old at the start, and disallows the door as fair wear and tear. The deduction comes down to £210 and the rest is returned.

What it cost her: £0, and an evening scanning photographs. What it recovered: £430. Had she simply accepted the first figure, she would have lost it. And had the deposit turned out not to be protected at all, the calculation would have been the other table: a £387 court fee against a ceiling of £3,450 plus the deposit back.

The habit worth taking from this is the check in photographs. They cost nothing, they take twenty minutes on the day you move in, and they are the single piece of evidence that decides most of these cases.

The verdict

If a landlord proposes a deduction you think is unfair, use your scheme's free dispute resolution service rather than accepting it, because it costs nothing, the money is already held by the scheme rather than by the landlord, and both sides simply submit evidence. Understand before you start that GOV.UK says the decision is final.

If the deposit was never protected at all, that is a different and much bigger claim: a county court can order repayment plus up to three times the deposit, and the £387 fee on Form N208 is reclaimable if you win. Get free advice from Citizens Advice or Shelter before you file.

Frequently asked

Can a landlord deduct for fair wear and tear? No. Deductions are for damage, unpaid rent or cleaning back to the standard at the start. The Deposit Protection Service says adjudicators weigh the length of the tenancy, the number of occupants and the age and quality of each item, and a claim should not leave the landlord with upgrades paid for by you.

How long does a landlord have to protect my deposit? Thirty days from getting it, according to GOV.UK, and within the same 30 days they must give you a specified list of information including the scheme's details and the grounds on which they would keep some of your money. If you are unsure, ask the scheme directly.

How long do they have to give it back? Ten days from you both agreeing how much you get back. If you are in dispute, the deposit stays protected in the scheme until the issue is resolved, so it is not sitting in the landlord's account. Agree the figure in writing so the 10 days has a clear start.

Does the free dispute service cost anything? No. GOV.UK states the scheme offers a free dispute resolution service. Both sides have to agree to use it, both provide evidence, and the decision about your deposit is final. Gather your check in report, dated photographs and receipts first, because the adjudicator decides on the evidence.

What if my landlord never protected the deposit? You can apply to your local county court on Form N208 for a fee of £387, reclaimable if you win. The court can order repayment, and must order the landlord to pay you between one and three times the deposit within 14 days. Get free advice first, though you do not need a solicitor to apply.
