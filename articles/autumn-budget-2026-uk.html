Budget 2026 Is Wednesday 28 October: What Is Already Decided, and the Five Figures With a Decision Attached

Between now and then the papers will fill with predictions, most of which will be wrong. This page does something narrower: it lists what is already law and cannot change on the day, the five household figures on this site that genuinely have a decision hanging over them, and the one sensible thing to do in the meantime.

The short version

  • The Budget is Wednesday 28 October 2026, confirmed by the Chancellor's letter to the Treasury Select Committee published on GOV.UK on 31 July 2026. The OBR forecast is published the same day.
  • Already law, so not a Budget decision: income tax thresholds frozen to 5 April 2031; the cash ISA limit falling to £12,000 for under-65s from 6 April 2027; unused pensions inside Inheritance Tax from 6 April 2027 (Finance Act 2026), a change whose effect on an ordinary estate rather than a wealthy one is worked through separately; dividend tax at 10.75%, 35.75% and 39.35% since 6 April 2026; eVED at 3p a mile for electric cars from April 2028.
  • Five figures with a live decision: the electricity VAT zero rate, currently ending 31 March 2027; the £1,000 trading allowance and the promised £3,000 reporting threshold; the £3,000 Capital Gains Tax exempt amount; the Plan 2 student loan threshold freeze from April 2027; and the National Living Wage for April 2027, which lands with the Budget.
  • Not confirmed, and not worth acting on: property tax reform, pension tax-free cash, further ISA changes, wealth taxes. Every one is a rumour until it is said at the despatch box.

  • Our verdict: plan around the list of what is decided, watch the five, and ignore the rest.

1. The date, the letter, and what arrives with it

The confirmation is a one-page letter from the Chancellor, John Healey, to the Treasury Select Committee, published on GOV.UK on 31 July 2026. It says the Budget will be held on 28 October 2026 and that the Office for Budget Responsibility will publish its economic and fiscal forecast alongside it. The Treasury's line for the event is "growth in every postcode". That is all that is official. Everything else you read before the day is analysis, lobbying or guesswork.

The Finance Bill clauses published after the speech, not the speech itself, say exactly who is affected and from when. We will update this page on 28 October with what was announced and each start date.

2. Already decided: the changes that cannot move on the day

A surprising amount of what people expect from a Budget has already happened, either in earlier Budgets or in the Finance Act 2026 that received Royal Assent on 18 March. These are law. The Chancellor could in theory reverse any of them, but reversals are rare and each would be its own headline. Student finance sits in the same settled column: tuition fees at £9,790 for 2026-27 and what that actually costs is fixed whatever is announced on the day.

Of these, the threshold freeze affects by far the most households and gets the least attention, because a freeze never appears as a tax rise on the day it is extended. It is worth roughly £130 a year in extra tax to a basic-rate worker for every £650 of pay rise that would otherwise have been covered by an uprated allowance. 3. Five figures that genuinely have a decision attached

These are not predictions. Each is a figure that appears in a guide on this site, that the government has either time-limited, promised to change, or left unset, and that a Budget is the natural place to settle. Watch these five and you can ignore most of the noise. One change already dated rather than debated is payroll reporting: from April 2027 a company car shows on the payslip itself, which moves when the tax is collected rather than how much it is.

1. The electricity VAT zero rate. VAT on domestic electricity falls from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026 and is currently confirmed only until 31 March 2027. Extending it, ending it or making it permanent is a Budget-sized decision, and Ofgem's announcement of the January 2027 price cap follows in late November, so the two will be read together. Our VAT cut guide has the unit-rate arithmetic.

2. The £1,000 trading allowance and the £3,000 reporting threshold. The government has said it will raise the point at which side income must be reported to HMRC from £1,000 to £3,000, without changing the £1,000 tax-free amount. That needs legislation and a start date, and neither exists yet. Until it does, the 5 October registration deadline runs on £1,000. Once side income clears that allowance by a wide margin the question becomes structural rather than seasonal, and whether a limited company beats sole trader for affiliate income turns on numbers most people guess at.

3. The Capital Gains Tax annual exempt amount. £3,000 since April 2024, with rates of 18% and 24% on most assets since 30 October 2024. It has been cut twice in three years and is the kind of allowance that moves quietly in a Budget red book table.

4. The Plan 2 student loan repayment threshold. Frozen at £29,385 for three years from April 2027, a decision that costs a graduate on £35,000 more than the interest rate change ever did. A Budget could extend, shorten or index it. Our student loan guide explains why the threshold matters more than the rate.

5. The National Living Wage from April 2027. This one is guaranteed to be announced on the day. The Low Pay Commission's remit gives a range of £13.02 to £13.34 with a central estimate of £13.18, against £12.71 today, and its recommendation is due by October so the Chancellor can confirm the figure in the speech. Until it changes, the current rate is the one to check a payslip against, and what the minimum wage should actually show on a payslip catches more underpayment than any rate rise does.

4. What is only speculation

Ahead of every Budget the same subjects circulate: property tax reform, the pension tax-free lump sum, pension tax relief, further ISA restrictions, a wealth tax, inheritance tax thresholds. They circulate because they raise revenue and think tanks publish costings, not because anything has been announced. The people who lose money in Budget season withdraw a lump sum or sell a property because of a headline, then find the change never arrives and the tax on the move was real. The same mistake is being made right now over car finance compensation, where a scheme people have already spent against has been put on hold rather than cancelled, and what the pause on motor finance redress actually means for a claim is a better guide to the timing than anything a Budget preview will tell you.

5. A worked example in Sunderland

Aisha, 34, lives in Sunderland, earns £38,000 as a team leader and makes about £2,400 a year selling handmade candles online. She has £9,000 in a cash ISA and is wondering whether to "do something" before the Budget.

Going through the lists above: the threshold freeze already applies to her and nothing on 28 October changes what she pays this year. The cash ISA cut does not bite until April 2027 and she is nowhere near £12,000 a year of cash saving, so it is irrelevant to her. The dividend change does not touch her. Of the five live figures, only the trading allowance matters: her £2,400 gross is over £1,000, so she must be registered for Self Assessment, and if the reporting threshold rises to £3,000 in future she might stop needing to file, but the £1,000 tax-free amount would not change and the tax on her roughly £1,400 profit, about £280 at 20%, would be the same either way. The sensible action list is short: register if she has not, keep paying into the ISA on current terms, and do nothing dramatic.

6. What to do before 28 October

Use the allowances that already exist, on their current terms, because they expire at the end of the tax year regardless. The £20,000 ISA allowance. Pension contributions, particularly if you are near £50,270 or £100,000 where they pull assessed income back under a cliff. Marriage Allowance if one partner is under the personal allowance. And check your tax code on your Personal Tax Account, because wrong codes are common and the money is yours whatever the Chancellor says. None of these depend on what happens on 28 October, which is precisely what makes them sensible rather than reactive. If you run a small payroll, the useful preparation is knowing what a small employer can reclaim on statutory payments before any rate moves.

7. The honest verdict

Most of what a household will feel in 2027 was decided before this Budget was scheduled: the threshold freeze, the cash ISA cut, pensions inside IHT and the dividend rise are all law. Five figures on this site genuinely have a decision attached, and one of them, the National Living Wage, will certainly be settled on the day. Household costs work the same way. The limit on branded school uniform items is already in force for this school year, not waiting on the Chancellor, and how the branded items cap works in practice is worth more to a family in October than any of the speculation above.

Use your existing allowances because they expire, check your tax code because it is often wrong, and ignore anything that asks you to make a permanent decision based on a prediction. We will update this page on 28 October with what was actually announced.

Frequently asked

When exactly is the Budget? Wednesday 28 October 2026, confirmed in the Chancellor's letter to the Treasury Select Committee published on 31 July 2026. The OBR forecast is published the same day.

Do Budget changes take effect immediately? Some do, from the day of the announcement. Most personal tax changes start on 6 April 2027. Some, like the salary sacrifice NIC change in 2029 and eVED in 2028, are years out. The Finance Bill clauses published after the speech give the exact dates.

Should I take money out of my pension before the Budget? Withdrawing pension money early has real tax consequences and usually cannot be undone. Nothing about the tax-free lump sum has been announced. This is exactly the situation for regulated advice rather than a news story.

Will the electricity VAT cut be extended? Nobody knows. It is confirmed to 31 March 2027 and the decision on what happens next is the kind of thing a Budget settles. Plan on the 5% rate returning in April 2027 and treat an extension as a bonus.
