You Get Six Months to Bring a Tribunal Claim Instead of Three. Almost Nobody With a Problem Today Will Benefit

The employment tribunal time limit doubles on 1 October 2026, which is a genuine improvement on a rule that has caught out a great many people. Then there is Regulation 10, which decides who it applies to, and it is the reason the change will help far fewer people this year than the coverage suggests.

The short version

  • The change: the time limit for bringing most employment tribunal claims goes from three months to six months.
  • The date is 1 October 2026, set by The Employment Tribunal (Extension of Time Limits) (Miscellaneous Amendments and Transitional Provisions) Regulations 2026. The regulations amend seven statutory instruments.
  • Scotland is different for one claim type. Breach of employment contract claims move to six months in England and Wales on 1 October 2026, but in Scotland on 9 November 2026.
  • Regulation 10 is the catch. The longer limit applies only where the act or failure to act happened on or after 1 October 2026.
  • For a series of acts, the extended limit applies only where the last of them falls on or after 1 October 2026.
  • It does not revive anything. A claim already out of time on 30 September stays out of time on 1 October.

1. What is changing on 1 October

The time limit within which you can bring a claim to an employment tribunal increases from three months to six months for the majority of claims, from 1 October 2026.

The instrument doing it is The Employment Tribunal (Extension of Time Limits) (Miscellaneous Amendments and Transitional Provisions) Regulations 2026, which come into force on that date and amend seven statutory instruments to extend time limits for presenting complaints for specified matters from three months to six.

The rationale given is that it provides employees and employers more time to resolve disputes internally or through conciliation, and to consider the merits of a claim before making one.

On the substance, this is a real improvement. The three month limit has always been unusually short by the standards of civil claims, and it runs at the worst possible moment, when someone has just lost their job and is trying to find another one. A large number of otherwise good claims have died on it. Most claims that survive the clock never reach a hearing anyway, so working out what a settlement agreement is worth before you sign the claim away is the sum to do while the time limit still has room in it.

2. Regulation 10, and why it decides everything

Here is the paragraph that most summaries leave out, and it changes who this helps.

Regulation 10 makes transitional provision so that the amendments apply only where the act or failure to act to which the complaint relates occurs on or after 1 October 2026.

Read that against your own situation. The trigger is not when you bring the claim. It is when the thing you are complaining about happened.

Dismissed on 15 September 2026? Three months. Your limit is around 14 December 2026, and the new rules do not touch you.

Dismissed on 15 October 2026? Six months. Your limit runs to around 14 April 2027.

Dismissed on 30 September 2026? Three months, by one day.

So on 1 October 2026, the population of people who benefit is: nobody, yet. It grows by one day for every day that passes. Anyone with a live grievance about something that has already happened is on the old three month clock and will stay on it permanently.

There is a second limb for ongoing situations. Where a complaint relates to a series of acts or failures to act, the extended limit applies only where the last of those acts falls on or after 1 October 2026. That matters for things like a course of harassment or a continuing failure to make reasonable adjustments, where conduct straddles the date. If the last act in the series is on or after 1 October, you get six months.

And to be explicit about the thing people hope for: this does not revive a claim that is already out of time. If your three months expired in August 2026, it is expired. Nothing on 1 October brings it back.

3. The Scotland difference nobody mentions

For most claim types the date is 1 October 2026 across Great Britain. For one type it is not.

Breach of employment contract claims in England and Wales move to six months on 1 October 2026. In Scotland, the same change commences on 9 November 2026.

That is a gap of nearly six weeks in which a breach of contract claim arising in Scotland is still on three months while the identical claim in England is on six. If your contract claim in Scotland arises in mid October, you are on the old limit.

4. Worked example: two dismissals, two weeks apart

Priya and Owen both work at the same warehouse in Coventry. Both are dismissed in what they believe is an unfair process. Priya is dismissed on 24 September 2026. Owen is dismissed on 8 October 2026. Fourteen days apart, same employer, same alleged failing.

Priya's clock. The act complained of is before 1 October, so Regulation 10 keeps her on three months. Her deadline is approximately 23 December 2026.

Owen's clock. His dismissal is on or after 1 October, so he gets six months. His deadline is approximately 7 April 2027.

The difference between them is about 105 days of thinking time, created by a fourteen day gap in dismissal dates. Same facts, same employer, radically different amounts of room.

What this means in practice for Priya: she should behave exactly as if nothing has changed, because for her nothing has. If she reads a headline in November saying she has six months, and acts on it, she loses the claim entirely. That is the actual risk this article exists to flag.

Both of them still have to start Acas early conciliation first, which is unchanged and covered below.

5. What has not changed, and still catches people

Acas early conciliation is still compulsory first. You cannot go straight to a tribunal for most claims. You must notify Acas and get an early conciliation certificate before issuing. This is unchanged by the new regulations and it is a step people miss.

The clock is calculated in a fiddly way. The three month limit has always been "three months less one day" from the effective date of termination, and the Acas conciliation period pauses it. Six months will be calculated on the same kind of basis. Do not eyeball it, and in particular do not assume the last day is the same date six months later.

Not every claim is covered. The regulations extend the limit "for specified matters" across the seven instruments they amend. That covers the majority of claims, but "majority" is not "all". If your claim is unusual, check whether yours is one of the specified matters rather than assuming.

The two year qualifying period rules and other eligibility conditions are a separate question. Time limits govern when you must bring a claim. Whether you can bring it at all is decided by different rules.

Longer does not mean better. Evidence decays, witnesses leave, and memories go. A claim brought at month five is usually a weaker claim than the same one brought at month two. The extra time is insurance against missing the deadline, not an invitation to use it.

The honest verdict

Doubling the time limit is the right change. Three months less one day, running from the day you lose your job, has always been a trap that punished people for being busy or upset rather than for having a bad claim. Six months is a fairer window and the stated reason, giving both sides room to sort it out without a tribunal, is a good one.

But the way this is being reported will hurt someone. The rule that matters is Regulation 10, and it keys off when the thing happened, not when you claim. On 1 October 2026 the number of people who gain is zero, and it builds from there. Everyone with a live problem about something that has already occurred is on three months, permanently, no matter what they read.

So the practical advice is unchanged and unglamorous. If something has happened to you at work, contact Acas now. Do not wait, do not assume the new limit covers you, and do not use the extra time even if you turn out to have it. The deadline was never the reason to move quickly. The evidence was.

Frequently asked

I was dismissed in August 2026. Do I get six months? No. Regulation 10 means the extended limit applies only where the act complained of occurs on or after 1 October 2026. An August dismissal stays on the three month limit.

My claim already ran out of time. Does 1 October help? No. The change does not revive claims that are already out of time. There is a separate and narrow discretion for tribunals to extend time in some claim types, which is a matter for legal advice, not for this rule.

What if the problem is ongoing rather than a single event? Where a complaint relates to a series of acts or failures, the extended limit applies where the last of those acts falls on or after 1 October 2026. So ongoing conduct that continues past the date can qualify.

Do I still have to go through Acas first? Yes. Early conciliation through Acas remains a required step before bringing most tribunal claims, and these regulations do not change that. Acas is free.
