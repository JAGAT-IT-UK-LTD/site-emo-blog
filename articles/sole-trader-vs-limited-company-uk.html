Sole Trader vs Limited Company: For 2026/27 the Sums Say Stay a Sole Trader

Sole trader vs limited company comes down to one question: do you take all the profit out? If you do, the sole trader keeps more in 2026/27 at every level we tested, from £30,000 to £120,000. Dividend tax rose on 6 April 2026 and that changed the old advice. A company still wins for liability, for profit you leave inside, and for some clients.

The short version

  • Dividend tax rose 2 percentage points on 6 April 2026. The ordinary rate went from 8.75% to 10.75% and the upper rate from 33.75% to 35.75%. That is the change that broke the old advice.
  • On profits of £45,000, a sole trader keeps £36,568. The same person through a limited company keeps £35,247. The sole trader is £1,321 ahead before a penny of accountancy fees.
  • The sole trader is ahead at all five levels we tested, by £1,065 at £30,000, by just £20 at £60,000 and by £2,257 at £120,000.
  • A company costs money to run. Since 1 February 2026 it is £100 to incorporate online and £50 a year for the confirmation statement, before any accountant.
  • The company still wins on four things, and none of them is tax on money you take out: limited liability, leaving profit inside the business, staying outside Making Tax Digital, and clients who will not contract with a sole trader.
  • Nothing changes for April 2027 on dividends. The Budget of 28 October 2026 left the rates at 10.75% and 35.75% for 2027/28.

1. What is the difference between a sole trader and a limited company?

As a sole trader, you and the business are the same legal person. You pay income tax and Class 4 National Insurance on your profit, whether you spend it or not.

For 2026/27 that means 20% income tax between £12,570 and £50,270, and 40% above. Class 4 National Insurance is 6% up to £50,270 and 2% on everything above.

A limited company is a separate legal person. It pays corporation tax on its profit: 19% up to £50,000 and 25% above £250,000, with marginal relief sliding between the two.

Then you pay yourself, usually a small salary plus dividends. You pay personal tax again on the dividends.

That second layer is the whole story. For years it was cheap enough that the two step route beat the one step route once profits passed roughly £30,000 to £40,000.

On 6 April 2026 it got 2 percentage points dearer, and the arithmetic changed sides. Our guide to dividend tax, the £500 allowance and the 10.75% rate shows what a shareholder now owes.

Whichever structure you land on, check who the £20,000 Making Tax Digital threshold catches in 2028. It reaches sole traders long before it reaches companies.

2. What does a limited company cost to run?

Many comparison articles still quote Companies House fees that stopped being correct on 1 February 2026. Here is what a company costs now, before you pay anyone for advice.

Confirmation statement, paper (every year)£110
Voluntary strike off, digital£13

Those are the visible costs. The invisible ones matter more.

A company must file annual accounts and a corporation tax return. It must run PAYE if it pays a salary, and keep the two sets of money truly separate.

Most one person companies pay an accountant for this. Fees vary a lot, so get a written quote. A sole trader with tidy records can often file their own return for nothing.

There is also a step that did not exist two years ago. Since 18 November 2025, identity verification at Companies House is mandatory.

Every director and person with significant control must verify through GOV.UK One Login or an authorised provider, and supply a personal code. It is free. The 12 month transition for existing directors ended in November 2026, so every director should now be verified.

The days of setting up a company on a whim in ten minutes are over. Our guide to Companies House identity verification sets out what directors have to do.

3. Sole trader vs limited company tax: which keeps more?

Below is the same profit figure run two ways for the 2026/27 tax year, for someone in England, Wales or Northern Ireland with no other income.

The sole trader column is income tax plus Class 4 National Insurance.

The company column assumes the most common structure. A £12,570 salary, employer National Insurance at 15% on the slice above £5,000, corporation tax on what is left, and the rest taken as dividends with the £500 dividend allowance applied.

ProfitSole trader keepsCompany route keepsDifference
£30,000£25,468£24,403Sole trader +£1,065
£45,000£36,568£35,247Sole trader +£1,321
£60,000£46,111£46,091Line ball, £20 apart
£80,000£57,711£55,765Sole trader +£1,947
£120,000£76,911£74,654Sole trader +£2,257

We reran every row in November 2026. One figure changed from our August version. At £120,000 the sole trader loses £10,000 of personal allowance, because the allowance tapers above £100,000. So the sole trader keeps £76,911, and the gap is £2,257.

Three details drive the table, and all are easy to miss.

Employer National Insurance is not free. A single director company paying a £12,570 salary owes 15% employer National Insurance on the £7,570 above the £5,000 secondary threshold. That is £1,135.50.

The £10,500 Employment Allowance would normally wipe this out. It is not available to a company whose only employee paid above the threshold is the sole director. Guides written for two person businesses quietly assume the allowance applies. For most freelancers it does not.

The upper dividend rate is now brutal. Above £50,270 of total income you pay 35.75% on dividends that have already had corporation tax taken off.

At £80,000 of profit the company's top slice bears 26.5% corporation tax, then 35.75% dividend tax on what is left. Together that takes about 53% of each extra pound. A sole trader on the same slice pays 40% income tax plus 2% National Insurance, so 42%.

The £60,000 row is the odd one out. At that level the company's profit is still under £50,000, so it pays only 19%. The owner's total income also stays just under £50,270, so every dividend is taxed at 10.75%.

The sole trader on £60,000 is already paying 40% on £9,730. The two routes meet for a moment. Nobody should restructure a business over £20.

4. Worked example: Nadia in Luton

Nadia is a freelance graphic designer. Her business made £45,000 profit in 2026/27 and she has no other income. She needs all of it to live on. An online company formation advert has told her she would be better off limited.

As a sole trader. Her personal allowance covers the first £12,570. That leaves £32,430 taxed at 20%, which is £6,486.

Class 4 National Insurance at 6% on that same £32,430 is £1,945.80. Total tax and National Insurance: £8,431.80. She keeps £36,568.20.

Through a limited company. The company pays her a £12,570 salary, which triggers £1,135.50 of employer National Insurance. That leaves £31,294.50 of company profit.

Corporation tax at 19% is £5,945.96. She can pay out £25,348.54 as dividends.

Her personal allowance is already used by the salary. After the £500 dividend allowance she pays 10.75% on £24,848.54, which is £2,671.22. She keeps £35,247.32.

The sole trader route leaves Nadia £1,320.88 better off on tax alone.

Then add the company's costs. £100 to incorporate and £50 for the confirmation statement. Say she is quoted £80 a month by an accountant for accounts, corporation tax and payroll, which is £960 a year. That is our assumption, not a market average.

On those numbers the company costs her £1,110 in year one. Add the £1,321 of extra tax and the company route leaves her about £2,431 worse off.

5. When is a limited company still better?

None of the above means companies are pointless. It means the reason to have one is no longer the tax on money you take out. Four reasons still hold.

Limited liability. If the business could plausibly be sued, or could owe money it cannot pay, a company puts a wall between the business and your house. This is the reason that matters most, and it is worth paying for. A sole trader is personally liable without limit.

You do not need all the profit. The table above assumes every pound comes out. If you can leave money in the business, the comparison turns round.

A sole trader on £80,000 pays 42% on the top slice whether or not they touch it. A company pays 26.5% marginal corporation tax and the rest stays inside. It is there for equipment, for a lean year, or for an employer pension contribution with no dividend tax and no National Insurance.

That is a timing advantage, not a free lunch. Dividend tax still lands whenever you take the money out.

Making Tax Digital for Income Tax. Since 6 April 2026, sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000 must keep digital records and send quarterly updates. The threshold drops to £30,000 from 6 April 2027 and £20,000 from 6 April 2028.

Qualifying income is gross turnover before expenses, not profit. So it catches people well below £50,000 of take home. Limited companies are not in scope.

The Budget of 28 October 2026 softened the start. People joining in April 2027 get no penalty points for late submission of their first four quarterly updates. Our guide to Making Tax Digital for Income Tax and the quarterly updates it requires has the detail.

Clients who require it. Some agencies and larger firms will not engage a sole trader. If that is your market, the decision is made for you.

Once you work through a company for one main client, off payroll status decides who pays the tax. Our guide on setting freelance rates in the UK covers how to price that overhead back in.

A partner who really works in the business can also change the answer. A second employee paid above the £5,000 secondary threshold restores the £10,500 Employment Allowance. Dividends split across two personal allowances and two basic rate bands can beat one. Both only work if the work and the shareholding are real.

6. What do people wrongly believe about companies?

A company does not give you more expenses. The test is wholly and exclusively for the business in both cases. If a cost is not allowable as a sole trader, it is very unlikely to become allowable inside a company.

Incorporating does not reset the VAT threshold. Registration is compulsory once taxable turnover passes £90,000 in any rolling 12 months, whatever your structure. If you are near it, see whether the VAT Flat Rate Scheme is worth it.

A company cannot use the trading allowance. The allowance is for individuals. It is £1,000 of gross trading income now and rises to £3,000 from 6 April 2027. For a small side hustle that makes sole trader the simple choice. Our side hustle tax rules guide covers when you first need to tell HMRC anything at all.

You cannot dodge Making Tax Digital by staying small on paper. You stay outside it only if your qualifying income is under the threshold for the relevant year, or you qualify for an exemption such as being digitally excluded. Check it against turnover from self employment and property combined, not profit.

The honest verdict

If you are a one person business taking all your profit out to live on, the sole trader route is now cheaper at every level we tested. The gap runs from £20 to £2,257 before company running costs, which only widen it.

The rule of thumb that told you to incorporate above £40,000 or £50,000 was written for a dividend tax regime that ended on 5 April 2026. The same arithmetic run on affiliate income specifically lands in the same place, for the same reason.

Incorporate because you need liability protection, because you can leave profit in the business, because Making Tax Digital is about to catch you, or because your clients demand it. Those are good reasons.

Do not incorporate because someone told you it saves tax on money you are going to spend. For 2026/27 it does not, and the dividend rates are unchanged for 2027/28.

If you are near the £60,000 line, or you have a spouse who could really work in the business, that is a conversation for an accountant with your actual figures. The cost of that hour is small next to getting it wrong for three years.

Frequently asked

Is it better to be a sole trader or a limited company in the UK? For a one person business that takes all its profit out, sole trader keeps more in 2026/27. On £45,000 profit the difference is £1,321 a year before company running costs. A limited company is better when you need limited liability or can leave profit inside the business.

At what profit does a limited company save tax? At none of the five levels we tested, from £30,000 to £120,000, if every pound is taken out. The closest is £60,000, where the two routes are £20 apart. A company saves tax only on profit you leave inside, where it bears corporation tax of 19% to 26.5% and no personal tax yet.

Should a side hustle be a sole trader or a limited company? Almost always a sole trader. Individuals get a trading allowance of £1,000 of gross income, rising to £3,000 from 6 April 2027, and a company gets none. A company also brings annual accounts, a corporation tax return and a £50 confirmation statement every year, whatever it earns.

I already have a limited company. Should I close it? Not on the strength of one tax year. Closing has costs, and retained profit inside the company may come out more sensibly another way than as dividends. Also weigh the liability protection you would give up. Get advice with your own numbers before you file a strike off.

Do these figures apply in Scotland? Not exactly. Scottish taxpayers pay Scottish rates and bands on self employment income, which changes the sole trader column. Dividend tax, corporation tax and National Insurance are UK wide. Run your numbers against the Scottish bands for 2026/27 before you decide.
