ONS Labour Market Figures: What September Showed and What Changed Since

The ONS labour market bulletin of 15 September 2026 left unemployment at 4.9%, but the Claimant Count rose to 1.692 million. The October release cut unemployment to 4.7% while claims rose again, to 1.701 million. If you are asking for a pay rise or thinking of moving jobs, the gap between those two numbers is what matters.

The short version

  • Claimant Count: 1.692 million in August, then 1.701 million in September. It rose on the month and the year in both releases. This is the figure that turned.
  • Unemployment: 4.9% in the September release, 4.7% in the October release. The survey rate improved even as the benefit count rose. The ONS still asks users to treat survey moves with caution.
  • Vacancies: 702,000 for June to August, then 691,000 for July to September. That is down 37,000 on the year. Fewer vacancies means a longer shortlist for each one, which is where how recruiters actually search LinkedIn starts to decide who gets seen.
  • Total pay growth: 3.9% in September, 3.7% in October. Regular pay growth went the other way, from 3.5% to 3.7%. Private sector regular pay was 3.0% in the October release.
  • Employment rate: 75.1%, then 75.3%. Economic inactivity held at 20.9%. What does move is the law itself: the employment law changes landing in October 2026 alter day one rights rather than the numbers.
  • Bank Rate: held at 3.75% on 17 September, raised to 4% on 5 November. The next ONS labour market release is 17 November 2026.

1. What did the ONS labour market figures show on 15 September 2026?

The September bulletin was released at 7am on 15 September 2026. The October bulletin followed on 20 October.

Read across the September column first. The survey based rates, the ones the news leads on, did not move.

The administrative counts did move. They come from tax and benefit systems rather than a survey. All of them moved the same way in September, and it was not the good way.

2. What changed in the October labour market release?

The October bulletin, published on 20 October 2026, told a more mixed story. The survey numbers improved for the first time in months.

Unemployment fell to 4.7% for June to August, down 0.1 percentage points on the quarter and the year. The employment rate rose to 75.3%, up 0.2 points on both.

The administrative numbers did not join in. The Claimant Count rose again, to 1.701 million in September. Vacancies fell 21,000 on the quarter to 691,000.

Payrolled employees ticked up 13,000 on the month to about 30.3 million. Yet that is still 66,000 fewer than a year earlier, and the latest month is provisional.

So two ONS labour market releases in a row, and the same gap. People tell the survey they are working. Fewer of them show up on payrolls, and more claim out of work benefits.

3. Why is the Claimant Count the number to watch?

In the August bulletin the Claimant Count for July was 1.665 million. The ONS described it as down on the month and on the year.

The September bulletin put August at 1.692 million, up on the month and the year. The October bulletin put September at 1.701 million, up again.

That is a reversal of direction, not just a change of size. A few months ago the series was falling. It has now risen in two releases running.

Two caveats the ONS attaches are real. The latest month is provisional and gets revised as work capability assessments conclude. Revisions in recent months have tended to be downwards, so each first estimate may shrink. Claimant Count statistics are official statistics in development.

Even with both caveats, a turn is a turn. It fits the rest of the administrative data: payrolls lower on the year and vacancies lower on the quarter.

Where falls of that shape turn into redundancies, the first £30,000 of a termination payment is tax free and everything above it is not. The sums before that point are the ones people get wrong.

What notice pay and garden leave are each worth to you is worth working out before you sign anything. Training budgets move on the same cycle as hiring, so it helps to know what a small employer actually pays towards an apprenticeship once the levy rules are applied.

4. Is UK pay growth slowing?

Total pay is slowing. Regular pay is not, at least not yet.

In the September release, regular pay growth, which excludes bonuses, was 3.5%. The ONS said it had been relatively stable for five three month periods in a row.

Total pay growth, which includes bonuses, fell to 3.9% from 4.2%. The ONS added that it was last lower in September to November 2020, when it was 3.7%.

The October release took total pay down again, to 3.7%. Regular pay rose to 3.7%, so the two figures now match.

The gap tells you where the fall came from. Regular pay held, bonuses did not. That is the normal early sign of employers trimming extras rather than cutting salaries.

If the trimming goes past bonuses and reaches jobs, the step before any employment tribunal claim is Acas early conciliation and the clock it starts running.

In real terms pay is still growing, but thinly. In October, real regular pay grew 0.6% after CPIH inflation and real total pay 0.7%.

The public and private split is wide. Regular pay grew 6.3% in the public sector and 3.0% in the private sector in the October release. In September the private figure was 2.9%. The ONS says public sector growth is affected by the timing of pay awards.

5. What did the Bank of England do next?

The Bank of England saw the September figures early. The ONS granted it exceptional pre release access at 10am on Monday 14 September, in time for that day's Monetary Policy Committee meeting.

On 17 September the Bank held Bank Rate at 3.75%. The vote was six to three, with three members wanting a rise to 4%.

On 5 November the Bank raised Bank Rate to 4%. The vote was eight to one, and the one dissenter wanted a bigger rise to 4.25%.

So the cooling labour market did not stop a rate rise. The Bank weighed it against inflation, which sat well above its 2% target. Our guide on what to do around the 5 November Bank Rate decision covers savers and borrowers.

For anyone with a fixed rate ending, the old lesson still holds. Waiting for a decision rarely gets you a better fixed rate, because lenders price the expectation into swap rates weeks ahead.

6. Worked example: Nneka in Coventry

Nneka works in the private sector in Coventry on £32,000 a year. Her pay review is in November and she wants to know what to ask for.

The number that applies to her is not the 3.7% headline. It is the 3.0% private sector regular pay figure from the October release. That is what employers like hers are paying on average.

Here are the sums on her salary:

If she asks for 6% because of a headline she read, she is asking for double what the average private employer is granting. If she asks for 3.0%, she gets the average and only just keeps ahead of inflation.

The useful framing sits between them. Something around 4% to 5% is above average without being absurd. The case for it has to be about her work, because the national number does not support it. Our guide on how to ask for a pay rise in the UK walks through that conversation.

One more figure matters to her. Workforce jobs fell by 48,000 between March and June 2026, with self employment jobs down 43,000 of that.

If her fallback plan is going freelance, that part of the market is shrinking faster than employee jobs. Our guide to setting freelance rates in the UK is worth reading before she treats it as a safety net.

7. What to be careful about in these numbers

The ONS spends an unusual amount of each bulletin telling users how carefully to read it. That deserves passing on rather than burying.

The Labour Force Survey is still recovering. The achieved sample rose from 80,078 individuals in January to March 2026 to 81,193 in April to June 2026. The ONS still advises focusing on long term movements, not single quarters.

The LFS is moving out of accredited status. On 11 August 2026 the ONS wrote to the Office for Statistics Regulation about it. LFS and Annual Population Survey outputs will move to official statistics, and the ONS will not seek reaccreditation. It is prioritising the Transformed Labour Force Survey instead.

Employee measures disagree with each other. In the September release, LFS employees rose 111,000 on the year to 29.8 million, while RTI payrolled employees fell. The ONS says RTI currently gives the most reliable measure of employees. It scheduled new research linking RTI to the LFS for 28 September 2026.

The 4.7% is a survey figure. A 0.1 point fall in unemployment is within the range the ONS asks users not to over read. The rising Claimant Count is the steadier signal.

The next ONS labour market release is 17 November 2026.

The honest verdict

Nothing in the September release was dramatic, and that was the story. Unemployment and employment did not move. The Claimant Count turned upwards and payrolls kept drifting down.

October looked brighter on the surface, with unemployment down to 4.7%. Underneath, the Claimant Count rose again to 1.701 million and vacancies fell to 691,000. A rising Claimant Count is also why next April matters, and what a 3.8% uprating actually adds to a working age award is a smaller sum than the percentage sounds.

For a household, the practical read is narrow. Private sector pay growth of 3.0% is the number to take into a pay conversation, not the headline. If you were thinking about moving jobs, fewer vacancies argue for lining something up before handing anything in.

If what you can line up is zero hours work, what the guaranteed hours consultation actually decided changes what you are entitled to ask for. For anyone entering the market rather than moving inside it, what an opening shift in retail actually pays and teaches at 18 to 24 is a more honest starting point than the graduate track.

Frequently asked

What did the ONS labour market figures show in September 2026? The 15 September release put unemployment at 4.9% for May to July and the employment rate at 75.1%, both broadly unchanged. The Claimant Count rose to 1.692 million in August, vacancies fell to 702,000, and total pay growth slowed to 3.9%, its lowest since late 2020.

What is the latest UK unemployment rate? The October 2026 release, published on 20 October, put the unemployment rate at 4.7% for June to August 2026. That is down 0.1 percentage points on the quarter and the year. The next release on 17 November 2026 will cover July to September and may revise the picture.

Why did total pay growth fall while regular pay growth held? Total pay includes bonuses and regular pay does not. When total pay falls while regular pay holds or rises, bonus payments are softening rather than salaries being cut. By the October release both measures stood at 3.7%, with private sector regular pay at 3.0%.

Is the Claimant Count the same as unemployment? No. The Claimant Count measures people receiving a benefit mainly because they are out of work, including Universal Credit claimants who are searching for work. Unemployment comes from the Labour Force Survey and uses an international definition. The ONS recommends reading the two together, not on their own.

When is the next ONS labour market release? The next ONS labour market overview is due at 7am on 17 November 2026. It will include unemployment for July to September, the Claimant Count for October and vacancies for August to October. Figures for the latest month are provisional and are often revised later.
