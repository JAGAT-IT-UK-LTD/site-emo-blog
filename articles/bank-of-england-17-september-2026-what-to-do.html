The Bank of England held at 3.75% on 17 September 2026. Seven weeks later it raised to 4%

On 5 November the Bank went further and raised to 4%. Here is what each step meant for a saver and a borrower, with the arithmetic done.

1. What happened on 17 September

At its meeting ending on 16 September 2026, the Monetary Policy Committee voted by 6 to 3 to maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%. The decision and minutes were published on Thursday 17 September.

Andrew Bailey, Sarah Breeden, Swati Dhingra, Clare Lombardelli, Dave Ramsden and Alan Taylor voted to hold. Megan Greene, Catherine L Mann and Huw Pill voted to raise by 0.25 points, to 4%.

So the question this article was asking before the meeting, whether more members would join the three, got a clear answer. Nobody did. The split was the same 6 to 3 as at the meeting ending 29 July.

The data did move. ONS figures published on 16 September showed CPI inflation at 3.1% in August, up from 2.9% in July. The Bank said inflation was likely to rise further over coming quarters. It judged the risks tilted to the upside, more so than in July.

The majority argued that spare capacity and the higher borrowing costs already in place gave time to wait for more evidence. The three who wanted a rise saw it as risk management. In the minutes they argue that an early rise would help anchor inflation expectations.

The same meeting also voted unanimously on the Bank's stock of government bonds. It will be reduced to zero through a multi year plan running to the end of 2034. That affects the gilt market rather than your savings rate directly.

2. What happened next: 4% on 5 November

Seven weeks later the Bank did raise. At its meeting ending on 4 November 2026, the Committee voted by 8 to 1 to increase Bank Rate by 0.25 percentage points, to 4%. Mann voted for a bigger rise, to 4.25%. Nobody voted to hold.

By then CPI inflation had reached 3.5% in September. The Bank now expects around 5% by the middle of 2027, mainly because energy prices kept rising. The full detail, and what to do now, is in our guide to the 5 November 2026 rise to 4%.

The next decision is Thursday 17 December 2026. If you are on a tracker, working out what a rise actually adds to a mortgage payment is worth doing before the announcement rather than after it.

3. Savings: what the rise does to £10,000

Before the September meeting, Moneyfacts published its inflation analysis on 19 August 2026. The Moneyfacts Average New Savings Rate was then 3.63% AER, the top easy access account paid 5.00%, and 1,850 accounts beat the 2.9% inflation rate of the time, roughly four in five.

The picture in November is less kind. Moneyfacts said on 5 November that a typical easy access account paid 2.56% at the start of the month. A selection of easy access accounts from the big banks paid 1.16% on average. The most competitive paid more than 4%. Inflation is now 3.5%.

The arithmetic on £10,000 held for a year, at November rates:

RateInterest on £10,000Real gain after 3.5% inflation
A top easy access account, 4.00%£400about £50
Typical easy access, 2.56%£256about minus £94
Big bank average, 1.16%£116about minus £234

Now the rise itself. Bank Rate went up by 0.25 points on 5 November. If your easy access account passes that on in full, which not all do, it is £25 more a year on £10,000. Useful, not life changing. The gap between the big bank average and a top account is £284 a year on the same money, more than eleven times the size of the rise.

Fixed rate bonds work differently. Providers price them off what the market expects Bank Rate to be over the term, not off today's rate. Moneyfacts says many market leading bonds now pay over 5%.

Tax matters too. Interest above your Personal Savings Allowance is taxable. GOV.UK sets the allowance at £1,000 for a basic rate taxpayer and £500 for a higher rate taxpayer. At 4%, £25,000 in an ordinary account earns £1,000, the whole basic rate allowance.

4. Mortgages: trackers moved in November, fixes moved first

A tracker mortgage follows Bank Rate directly, usually from the next payment. A standard variable rate follows it if your lender chooses to pass the change on. So the September hold changed nothing for tracker borrowers, and the November rise added 0.25 points.

A fixed rate does not change until the deal ends, and new fixes are priced off swap rates, which react to expectations rather than announcements. That is why fixes moved before the Bank did. Moneyfacts records the average two year fix rising from 5.59% at the start of September to 5.97% on 1 November, and the five year from 5.63% to 6.00%. The Bank's November Monetary Policy Report says quoted rates on two year fixes at 75% and 90% loan to value rose by around 60 basis points on average since July.

If you are on a tracker or coming off a fix, what the voting split signals for mortgage pricing tells you more than the headline decision does. If you are buying rather than remortgaging, the rate is only part of the bill: the difference between a homebuyer report and a full structural survey decides several hundred pounds of it.

On a £200,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years, a 0.25 point move is worth about £29 a month, on our arithmetic:

RateMonthly payment
4.25%£1,083
4.50%£1,112
4.75%£1,140
5.00%£1,169

Those are illustrative rates, not quotes. The point is the shape: each quarter point is roughly £28 to £29 a month on that loan, and each is small next to the difference between the rate you are on and the rate you could be on.

If your fix ends within six months, most lenders let you lock a new rate now and switch to a cheaper one if rates fall before completion. That was the useful move before 17 September, and it still is, because it costs nothing to hold the option. If your fix is not near its end and there is spare cash each month, the comparison to run instead is overpaying the mortgage against saving the difference.

5. A worked example in Reading

Tom in Reading has £10,000 in an easy access account paying 2.0% and a £200,000 tracker at 4.50%, with 25 years to run.

What the September hold did: nothing. Tom's payment stayed at £1,111.66 a month. His savings still earned £200 a year and lost value against 3.1% inflation. Moving to a better account was open to him all along, no decision required. One small cost worth knowing if he is buying or selling: a Land Registry title check costs a few pounds and settles who actually owns what.

What the November rise did: his tracker goes to 4.75% and his payment rises from £1,111.66 to £1,140.23. That is £28.57 a month, or £342.84 a year. If his bank passes the rise on, his 2.0% account goes to 2.25%, worth £25. The rise costs him about £318 net a year.

What he can still control: at 2.25% his £10,000 earns £225. At 4.00% it would earn £400. Switching is worth £175 a year, about half of what the rise costs him on the mortgage.

The lesson is not about predicting the MPC. Tom's biggest number is the one he controls.

6. What to watch next

The next decision is Thursday 17 December 2026, without a Monetary Policy Report. The one after is Thursday 4 February 2027, with a Report. The Bank says policy is not on a pre-set path and that it will judge the evidence at each meeting.

The number to watch between now and then is inflation. The Bank's November forecast has CPI at around 5% by the middle of 2027. Each monthly ONS figure tells you whether that is on track, and the December vote split will tell you how many members want to go further.

7. The honest verdict

Do not wait for the Bank to sort your savings. The gap between a big bank easy access account and a top one is around £284 a year on £10,000. The November rise is worth £25.

If your fix ends in the next six months, lock a rate now and keep the right to switch. Waiting for September did not pay. Average fixes rose between September and November.

If you are on a tracker, the rise is now real: about £29 a month per £200,000. Budget for the possibility of more. Nobody can tell you the next vote.

Frequently asked

What did the Bank of England decide on 17 September 2026? It held Bank Rate at 3.75%. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 6 to 3 at its meeting ending 16 September. Megan Greene, Catherine L Mann and Huw Pill voted for a rise to 4%. The summary and minutes were published on 17 September 2026 on the Bank of England website.

What is Bank Rate now? 4%. The Committee raised it by 0.25 percentage points at its meeting ending 4 November 2026, announced on 5 November, by a vote of 8 to 1. One member wanted 4.25%. The next decision is due on Thursday 17 December 2026, according to the Bank of England's Bank Rate page.

Will my savings rate go up now Bank Rate has risen? Variable and easy access rates often rise, but providers decide how much to pass on and when. Fixed rate accounts already price expectations, so they may not move at all. Check the actual rate on your account rather than assuming, and compare it with inflation at 3.5%.

Should I fix my mortgage now? That is a personal decision and we are not advisers. The factual point is that most lenders let you secure a rate up to six months before your deal ends and switch if a cheaper one appears. That removes most of the timing risk, whatever the Bank does on 17 December.
