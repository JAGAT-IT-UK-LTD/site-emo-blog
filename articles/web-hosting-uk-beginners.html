Web Hosting in the UK for Beginners: What You Actually Pay

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Checked August 2026
The short version

  • Long first terms exist to lock in that gap. A 48-month deal is not generosity, it is deferred billing.

The renewal price trap

Almost every mainstream host in the UK runs the same model. A very low headline price for the first term, then a much higher price on renewal, automatically, on a card you already gave them.

The gap is not small. A plan advertised at a couple of pounds a month can renew at four, five or six times that. Nothing about the service changes. You are simply now on the real price.

Three things follow from this:

  1. Find the renewal price before you buy. It is usually in the terms, the FAQ, or a footnote under the pricing table. If you cannot find it in two minutes, that is deliberate.
  2. Multiply the renewal price by twelve and compare hosts on that number, not on the introductory one. The ranking changes completely.
  3. Set a calendar reminder for a month before renewal. That is when you either negotiate, downgrade, or move.

The long first term is the same trick in another shape. A 36 or 48 month deal locks the low rate for longer, which is genuinely good value if you are certain you will still want the site in four years. Most first sites do not last four years. Paying four years up front for a project you abandon in eight months is the most common way people lose money on hosting.

If you are building a static site, a portfolio or documentation with no database, look at static hosting first. Several providers host static sites free on a custom domain with SSL included. It is genuinely the cheapest correct answer for a large number of small sites, and nobody selling hosting will tell you.

What you should actually pay

Ranges, not exact figures, because promotions change constantly and any specific price written down here will be wrong within weeks.

  • Shared hosting, introductory: low single-digit pounds a month, usually paid annually or longer up front.
  • Shared hosting, renewal: commonly £8 to £20 a month. This is the number that matters.
  • Domain: a .co.uk is typically cheaper than a .com and renews at close to its first-year price. Watch for hosts that give a free domain in year one and renew it well above market.
  • Managed WordPress: from around £15 a month, and upward quickly.
  • Small VPS: from around £5 a month unmanaged, which is cheaper than most shared renewals, but you are now the system administrator.

Indicative UK market ranges as at August 2026. Verify current pricing directly, and always check the renewal rate rather than the headline.

Nine things to check before you buy

  1. The renewal price. Written down, in the terms, not in the marketing.
  2. Refund window. Thirty days is common. Anything shorter is a warning.
  3. Free SSL certificate, automatically renewing. Charging for this in 2026 is indefensible.
  4. Daily backups included, and how far back they go. Also check whether restoring from backup costs extra, which is a surprisingly common charge.
  5. Where the servers are. UK or EU servers help latency for UK visitors, and may matter for how you handle personal data.
  6. Bandwidth and storage limits, and what happens when you exceed them. "Unlimited" always has a fair use clause.
  7. PHP version and how you change it. Old PHP is a security problem and a common cause of plugin breakage.
  8. Staging environment. Being able to test a change before it hits the live site saves you eventually.
  9. How you leave. Can you export a full backup yourself, without asking support? If not, you are a hostage.

Why the domain goes somewhere else

Hosts offer a free domain with a hosting plan and it feels like a gift. It is a retention mechanism.

If your host also controls your domain, then a dispute, an outage, a billing problem or a price rise becomes much harder to escape, because moving requires their cooperation on both the site and the name. Registering the domain with a separate registrar costs a few pounds a year and buys you the ability to point the name anywhere, immediately, without asking anyone.

Two related habits: turn on auto-renew for the domain, because a lapsed domain can be picked up by someone else, and keep the registrar contact email on an address that is not on the domain itself. If the domain expires and your email was on it, you cannot receive the recovery message.

Free hosting, and when it makes sense

Free hosting is a reasonable choice in exactly two situations: a static site, or a temporary experiment.

Static sites — a blog built with a static site generator, a portfolio, documentation — can be hosted free on a custom domain with SSL, on infrastructure far better than budget shared hosting. If your site does not need a database, this is the correct answer and it is free permanently.

Free WordPress-style hosting is a different proposition. It typically comes with ads you do not control, a subdomain rather than your own name, limits on plugins, and no straightforward export. Fine for learning. Not fine for anything you want to keep.

Moving host without breaking anything

Migration sounds frightening and mostly is not, provided you do it in this order:

  1. Take your own full backup first, files and database, and download it. Do not rely on either host's copy.
  2. Set up the site at the new host and test it there before changing anything public. Most hosts provide a temporary URL or a hosts-file method for this.
  3. Lower the DNS TTL a day in advance so the switch propagates quickly.
  4. Point the domain at the new host. Keep the old account running for at least a week.
  5. Check the SSL certificate reissued, forms send, and email still works. Email is the thing people forget, particularly if it was bundled with the old host.
  6. Only then cancel, and ask about a pro-rata refund. Some hosts give one.

Many hosts will migrate a site for you free as part of signing up. Take that offer, but still take your own backup first.

Questions people ask

Is cheap hosting always bad? No. Cheap shared hosting is fine for a site with modest traffic. What is bad is cheap hosting with no backups, no SSL, an unfindable renewal price and a support queue measured in days.

Do I need a UK host for a UK site? Not strictly. Server location has some effect on speed and can be relevant to how you handle personal data, but a good host with a CDN in another country will usually outperform a poor one down the road.

How much traffic can shared hosting take? More than a new site will produce. Most sites that "outgrow" shared hosting have actually been broken by a badly configured plugin or an uncached page, not by visitor numbers.

Should I pay for a four-year plan to lock the low price? Only if you are certain about the project. The saving is real, and so is the risk of paying up front for a site you stop working on in month six. A one-year term with a diary reminder is the safer default.

The verdict

Buy shared hosting on a one-year term from a host whose renewal price you have actually read, register the domain somewhere else, and put a reminder in your calendar a month before it renews. If your site is static, host it free and spend the money on something that matters. Everything else in hosting marketing is designed to make a simple decision feel complicated.
