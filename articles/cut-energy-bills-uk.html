How to Cut Energy Bills in the UK: What Really Saves Money

Published 21 July 2026 · Updated 14 November 2026 · 11 min read · Figures checked November 2026

The fastest way to cut energy bills in the UK is free: lower the thermostat by one degree and set the boiler flow temperature to about 60°C. With the Ofgem price cap at £1,723 a year until 31 December 2026, heating is where most of your money goes. Gadgets and standby come a long way after that.

The short version
  • The Ofgem price cap from 1 October to 31 December 2026 is £1,723 a year for a typical dual fuel household paying by Direct Debit. That is up 4% on the £1,663 cap that ran from July to September.
  • Direct Debit cap rates now are 26.32p per kWh and 54.83p a day for electricity, and 7.97p per kWh and 29.68p a day for gas. Your region and meter change the exact figures.
  • There is no VAT on electricity from 1 October 2026 to 30 June 2027. The Budget added three months to the end date. Gas still carries 5% VAT.
  • Ofgem announces the next cap, for 1 January to 31 March 2027, on 25 November 2026. Until then, any figure you read for January is a forecast.
  • The cap limits unit rates and standing charges, not your bill. Use more, pay more.
  • Heating is the prize. In a gas heated home, gas is about 79% of the energy you use. The thermostat and the boiler matter far more than the kettle and the lightbulbs.

1. What does the energy price cap actually cap?

This is the most misunderstood number in British household finance. It is worth being precise.

The cap sets a maximum unit rate in pence per kWh. It also sets a maximum daily standing charge. It does not cap your bill.

The £1,723 headline is what a household using Ofgem's typical amounts would pay over a year. Ofgem's typical household now uses 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas. Use double that and you pay roughly double.

Ofgem warns the two periods cannot be compared directly. The government removed VAT from electricity from 1 October 2026, now until 30 June 2027 after the Budget, so the October electricity rates already exclude it. Gas still includes 5% VAT.

Three consequences follow.

The standing charge is unavoidable. You pay it every day on both fuels, whether you use anything or not. At the current rates that is about £308 a year before you boil a kettle. For a low usage home it can be a large slice of the bill, which is why cutting use has a floor.

The cap only applies to standard variable tariffs. If you are on a fixed deal, the cap is irrelevant until that deal ends. It is worth checking whether you are sitting in the wrong third on a fixed tariff.

The cap moves every three months. Ofgem announces the January to March 2027 level on 25 November 2026. Forecasters expect another rise, but nothing is official until Ofgem publishes. Our January 2027 price cap forecast tracks the predictions, and what the 4% October rise costs you breaks the current cap down by household.

What you replace matters as much as how much you run it. The appliance labels have been rescaled, so a C grade on a new appliance no longer means what it used to.

2. Where does your energy money really go?

People cut the wrong things because the visible energy use is not the expensive energy use.

Here is Ofgem's typical household, priced at the current Direct Debit cap. These are our sums from Ofgem's published rates and typical usage.

Part of the billSumCost a year
Gas used9,500 kWh at 7.97p£757
Gas standing charge29.68p for 365 days£108
Electricity used2,500 kWh at 26.32p£658
Electricity standing charge54.83p for 365 days£200
Total£1,723

Gas is 9,500 of the 12,000 kWh this home uses. That is about 79% of its energy. In most gas heated homes, nearly all of that gas goes on space heating and hot water.

So a thermostat turned down one degree beats every gadget in the house. Electricity is three times dearer per unit, so it still matters. But the big volume is heat.

Two handy rules come straight from the cap rates. Every 1,000 kWh of gas you avoid saves about £80. Every 100 kWh of electricity you avoid saves about £26.

Standby power gets enormous attention. It is worth switching off, but it is not the first job. We put a meter on that one: what standby actually costs and whether smart plugs pay for themselves.

3. How can you cut energy bills for free?

These are the free changes, roughly in order of how much they save in a typical gas heated home.

  1. Turn the thermostat down 1°C. The Energy Saving Trust puts this at around £120 a year for a typical home in Great Britain. Nothing else free comes close. Do not do this if someone in the home is elderly, very young or unwell.
  2. Set the boiler flow temperature to about 60°C. Many combi boilers are left at 70°C or higher. That stops a condensing boiler from condensing properly, so it wastes heat up the flue. Our guide to a 60C flow temperature shows how to do it in ten minutes.
  3. Only heat rooms you use. Turn radiator valves down in spare rooms, keeping them just above the frost setting. Thermostatic valves turn that into a fixed room setting rather than a daily chore.
  4. Time the heating properly. Heating an empty house is pure waste. Two well timed blocks beat all day at a lower temperature in most homes.
  5. Take shorter showers. Hot water is the second biggest use of gas in most homes. Every minute cut comes straight off that.
  6. Wash at 30°C and skip the tumble dryer where you can. A tumble dryer is one of the hungriest appliances in the house. Check the kWh per cycle on its energy label, then multiply by 26.32p to see what each load costs you.
  7. Give the freezer some room and defrost it. A frosted freezer works considerably harder.

None of this costs a penny. Together, the first four are the biggest controllable part of most bills. If they cut a typical home's gas use by 1,000 kWh, that alone is about £80 a year at today's cap rate.

5. What is not worth the money?

Being honest about this matters more than the savings list.

  • Energy saving plugs and voltage optimisers sold online. The plug in gadgets marketed to households do essentially nothing on a modern domestic supply. Be wary of any device that promises a big cut without changing how much heat or power you use.
  • Replacing a working boiler purely to save energy. A new boiler costs thousands. If yours is a working condensing boiler, the payback is very long. Replace it when it fails, not before.
  • Heated clothes airers, as an energy saving. They beat a tumble dryer on running cost. They do not beat a clothes horse, which is free.
  • Solar on the payback argument alone, if you are moving soon. Payback depends on system size, export rate and how much of your own power you use. It can be a good long term investment. It is not a quick saving.
  • Switching supplier for the sake of it. Cheaper deals exist, but check exit fees. Check whether the fix is below the cap over its whole term, not just today.

6. Worked example: the Hussain family, Luton

A three bedroom semi, four people, gas central heating, on the standard variable tariff at the Direct Debit cap. Their meter readings show 3,100 kWh of electricity and 12,500 kWh of gas a year. That is above Ofgem's typical figures because the house is draughty and busy.

At the October cap rates their bill comes to £2,120.63. That is £1,016.05 for electricity and £1,104.58 for gas, standing charges included.

7. What help can you claim this winter?

  • Warm Home Discount. A one off £150 off your electricity bill for eligible households. Most people who qualify get it automatically. See who gets the £150 automatically.
  • Winter Fuel Payment. For winter 2026 to 2027 it is £100 to £300 if you were born on or before 27 June 1960. If your total income is over £35,000, HMRC takes it back. Our guide to what changed this winter has the detail.
  • Cold Weather Payment. £25 for each 7 day period of very cold weather between 1 November 2026 and 31 March 2027, if you get certain benefits. It is paid automatically.
  • Insulation schemes. The Great British Insulation Scheme required installations to be complete by 31 March 2026, and its online eligibility checker has closed. GOV.UK says some suppliers are still taking applications, so ask yours and your council what is open now.
  • Boiler Upgrade Scheme. In England and Wales, a £7,500 grant towards an air source or ground source heat pump. GOV.UK lists an extra £1,500 towards those until March 2027.
  • Priority Services Register. Free, and not means tested. If you are of pensionable age, disabled, chronically ill or have young children, register with your supplier for extra support during outages.
  • Supplier hardship funds. Several large suppliers run grant schemes that can clear energy debt. Check your own supplier's page and the British Gas Energy Trust.

If you are in arrears, contact your supplier before it escalates. They must offer a payment plan you can afford. A plan agreed early is far better than a prepayment meter installed under warrant. Anyone already behind should check who qualifies to have energy debt written off under the Ofgem scheme.

8. Which myths cost people money?

"Leave the heating on low all day, it is cheaper than heating from cold." This is wrong for almost every home. Heat leaks out all the time in proportion to the temperature difference. A warm empty house leaks all day.

The Energy Saving Trust position is plain: heat when you need it. The exception is a well insulated home with a heat pump, where a lower, steadier flow temperature really is more efficient.

The radiator cover belongs to the same folklore, and what it traps is not free either. Here is what a radiator cover actually costs you over a winter.

"The price cap means my bill cannot go above £1,723." It means nothing of the sort. It caps the rate, not the total.

"Turning appliances off at the wall is the big win." Do it. Just do the thermostat first. Dropping a combi boiler to a 60C flow temperature is the free change most households still have not made.

The honest bottom line

Two changes, both free, both under fifteen minutes: drop the boiler flow temperature to about 60°C and drop the thermostat by one degree. That beats every gadget, plug and clever device on the market combined.

Hot water is the same kind of free change, and most people never touch it. A cylinder on a timer against one left on all day is worth checking before you spend anything.

Everything after that is refinement. Draught proofing and a cylinder jacket for about £65. Then check whether a fix beats the cap. If your bulbs are still halogen, replacing the lot in one go beats waiting for them to fail.

And the plain part. If your bill is high because your home is cold, badly insulated and you are already rationing, none of this is your fault. There is a limit to what behaviour change can do.

The grants and hardship funds exist for exactly that, and they are underclaimed. Check whether your postcode has triggered a Cold Weather Payment, because it is paid automatically and plenty of households never notice it arrive.

Frequently asked

When does the next energy price cap change? Ofgem changes the cap every three months. The level for 1 January to 31 March 2027 will be announced on 25 November 2026. Until then, figures for January are forecasts from analysts and suppliers, not Ofgem. Check ofgem.gov.uk on the day before you decide whether to fix.

Should I fix my energy tariff now? Nobody can promise an answer, because it depends on wholesale prices. Compare the fixed rates against the current cap over the whole term, and check the exit fee. If a fix is meaningfully below the cap and you value certainty, it is defensible. See whether fixing beats waiting for 25 November. This is general information, not financial advice.

What uses the most energy in a UK home? In a gas heated home, heating and hot water. Ofgem's typical household uses 9,500 kWh of gas against 2,500 kWh of electricity, so gas is about 79% of the energy used. That is why the thermostat, the boiler flow temperature and draught proofing save more than any appliance swap.

Do smart meters save money? Not by themselves. They save money if seeing the numbers changes your behaviour. They are also needed for most time of use tariffs, which can be worth a lot if you can shift when you use power. We went through what a smart meter actually changes on your bill separately.

Is a heat pump worth it? With the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant it can be, in a well insulated home with a correctly sized system. In a draughty solid wall house with small radiators, it can disappoint. Insulation comes first. Compare combi against system boilers and replacement radiator costs too.
