The Reason Nobody Has Ever Told You an App Is Cheaper on Its Own Website

1. What steering is, and why it is banned

When you buy a subscription inside an app on your phone, the platform takes a cut. That is the business model of both app stores and it pays for the review process, the distribution and the payment handling.

Developers have long wanted to point customers somewhere cheaper: the same subscription bought directly on their website, without the platform's cut. That practice is called steering, and in the UK it is currently banned by Apple and restricted by Google, in the CMA's own words.

The effect is a strange silence. Some services genuinely do cost less bought direct, but the app you use every day is contractually forbidden from telling you, and in some cases from even mentioning that a website exists. You are expected to find out by accident.

The CMA's framing is about competition rather than fairness. Its Executive Director for Digital Markets, Will Hayter, put it as introducing competitive pressure into a part of the mobile ecosystem that otherwise has almost none.

This is not a rule about prices. It is a rule about being allowed to speak. Whether your bill falls afterwards is a separate question with a less certain answer.

2. What the CMA has actually proposed

The consultation opened on 30 June 2026 and responses were due by 5pm on 28 July 2026. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act, the CMA has to consult before it can impose a conduct requirement, so this step was compulsory rather than optional. A decision on whether to impose anything is expected later this year.

The interesting part is not the ban on banning. It is what the CMA said about fees. Apple and Google would still be allowed to charge developers something for steered transactions, because the CMA accepts it is fair for them to be compensated for the services they provide. But the CMA set out that it would expect those steering fees to be lower than current app store charges, with the savings either passed on to UK customers or invested back into developers' businesses.

Note the two possible destinations for the money. Only one of them is you.

Google did not wait. On 24 June 2026 it announced new global Play Store terms, due to take effect in the UK on 30 June, which allow developers to steer users outside the Play Store to complete transactions, subject to certain restrictions, alongside changes to the fees it charges developers including those who use steering. The CMA has said it will assess the likely impact of those changes on users and businesses in the next phase of its work.

For context on how far the regime has got: eighteen months in, the CMA has concluded three strategic market status investigations, launched a fourth into Microsoft's business software ecosystem, imposed three conduct requirements on Google search, and secured commitments from both Apple and Google on mobile.

Where this has actually got to, as of 24 September 2026

The consultation closing is not the same as a rule existing, and the gap between the two is where most coverage of this stops. Read again on 24 September 2026, the CMA's own case page for Apple's mobile platform shows the investigation still at case state: Open, with the steering conduct requirement still described as proposed. The most recent entry under the steering heading is dated 14 August 2026, when the CMA published the responses it received to the consultation. No conduct requirement has been imposed and nothing about what an app is allowed to tell you has changed yet.

That last row is the honest answer to the question most people arrive with. If you are paying for a subscription inside an app today, the app is still not allowed to point you at the web price, and no date has been published for when that changes. The practical move is not to wait for the regulator: for any subscription you already hold, open the service's own website in a browser, signed out of the app, and compare the price there with what the app store charges you. Where a difference exists it is usually the platform commission, and it is usually visible in one minute.

Who this does not help: anything bought outright rather than by subscription, anything where the developer has chosen to price identically on the web, and anyone on an annual plan already paid for, where the only decision is what to do at renewal. For a household with two or three app-billed subscriptions the whole exercise is worth a few pounds a month at best, which is real but is not the story the coverage implies.

3. The other change, on your iPhone's contactless chip

This one got almost no coverage and may matter more in five years.

The NFC chip in an iPhone is what makes contactless payment work. Access to it has been tightly controlled. After hearing from businesses that Apple's fees and terms blocked access, the CMA is designing a potential requirement to open it to other developers.

If that lands, a bank, a fintech or a retailer could support contactless payments from inside its own iOS app rather than routing through Apple's wallet. The CMA lists the possibilities as card payments, account to account transfers, digital currency and stablecoins, and non-financial uses including digital ID and car keys.

The CMA asked for views on two design elements: the technical method by which access is provided, and the price charged for it. Responses on that part were due by 5pm on 21 July 2026.

Worked example: Owen Pritchard in Cardiff

Owen pays for three subscriptions through his phone: a music service, a fitness app and a cloud storage plan. All three renew automatically inside the app store and he has never compared them against anything.

What he cannot currently be told: whether any of the three is cheaper bought direct on the provider's website. The apps are not permitted to say so. He is not being overcharged illegally, he is just being kept uninformed by contract.

What steering would change for him: the app could show him a link and a price. That is genuinely useful, because he would learn in one tap what currently takes deliberate research.

What it would not automatically change: the price. The CMA expects steering fees to be lower than app store charges, but a developer is free to keep that difference rather than cut its price. The regulator's own wording allows for savings to be reinvested in the business instead of passed on.

What he can do today, without waiting for any of this: open each subscription's website and compare it against what his app store receipt says. That takes about ten minutes for three services and does not need a regulator's permission. Cancelling and re-subscribing direct is the part to check carefully, because some plans cannot be moved without losing a legacy price.

4. The honest verdict

The direction is right and it is overdue. A rule that stops a business telling its own customers where else they can buy is hard to defend, and app store terms are under scrutiny in the EU, the US and Japan for the same reason.

But be careful what you expect from it. Removing a restriction on communication is not the same as cutting a price. The most likely first effect is that a few large developers add a link, a smaller number cut their web price to make the link worth clicking, and most apps carry on exactly as they are.

So treat this as a story to watch rather than a saving to bank. The genuinely actionable thing is the one you can do today: check the website price of any subscription you pay for through your phone. On three or four services, that is a real number, and it does not depend on a decision that has not been made yet.

Frequently asked

Has the CMA decided anything yet? No. It consulted until 28 July 2026 and published the responses on 14 August 2026. A decision on whether to impose the requirements is expected later this year.

Does this only affect iPhones? No. The steering proposals cover both Apple and Google mobile platforms. The NFC element is specific to iOS.

Will my existing subscriptions get cheaper automatically? No. Nothing here forces a price change. It changes what developers are allowed to tell you and what they are charged for steered transactions.

Is this the same as the EU rules? No, but the issues overlap. App store terms including steering are under scrutiny in several jurisdictions, and the UK regime is its own framework under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act.
