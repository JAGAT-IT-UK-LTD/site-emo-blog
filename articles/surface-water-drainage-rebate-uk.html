There is a line on your water bill for taking away rain that may never reach a sewer

Surface water drainage is charged to almost every household by default, on the assumption that rain landing on your roof and drive ends up in a public sewer. In a good number of houses it does not. The charge can be removed and backdated six years, it costs nothing to ask, and the reason almost nobody claims it is that the bill does not invite you to.

1. What the charge is for

Severn Trent's surface water drainage page, checked on 9 November 2026, defines it plainly. Surface water is the rainwater that falls onto your roof, driveway and other parts of your property and drains away into public sewers. For most customers the cost of removing and processing that rainwater is charged as part of wastewater services.

How it is calculated depends on how you are billed. If you are billed on the rateable value of your home, the surface water charge is worked out from your RV figure. If you are a water meter customer or on assessed charges, it is based on property type: flat or terraced, semi detached, or detached.

The published 2026/27 figures for a metered Severn Trent household in zones 1 to 8 are £73.44 a year for a detached house including link detached, £48.96 for a semi detached house, and £24.78 for other household premises. There is also a fixed charge of £8.30 a year across all zones for customers charged on rateable value, with a separate rate per pound of RV by zone.

None of that is unreasonable if the rain from your roof does end up in a public sewer. The question this article is about is what happens when it does not, which is a lot more common than the default charging assumes, particularly on older rural properties and on newer developments built with sustainable drainage.

2. When you can get it removed

The test is strict and worth quoting rather than paraphrasing. Severn Trent says you may be able to reduce or remove the charge if you can show that all of the rainwater from your property drains somewhere other than the public sewer, and that this usually means that 100 per cent of the rainwater from your roof, driveway, paths and other hard surfaces drains into a soakaway or another private drainage system.

One hundred per cent is the bar. A property where the roof goes to a soakaway but the driveway runs to a road gully will not qualify, and the company states plainly that if surface water from any part of your property drains to a public sewer, you will have to pay the charge.

Now the part that makes this worth an afternoon. On when the change applies from, Severn Trent says that if you can show no surface water from your property or any shared area nearby drains directly or indirectly into a public sewer, it will stop charging, and the change takes effect from the later of the start of the current financial year plus 6 years backdating, or the date you moved into the property.

The full household scheme of charges for 2026-27 says the same thing in legal language: the charge stops from the start of the year six years before the date you applied, or the date you moved in, whichever is later.

The backdated column is the current year plus six, which is the maximum the published rule allows and assumes you have lived there throughout. If you moved in three years ago, you get three years.

It is also a ceiling rather than a promise. The column multiplies this year's charge by seven, but each earlier year is credited at what you were actually billed then. Severn Trent says its typical bill rose 9.8 per cent in April 2026 alone, so older years were very likely billed at less and the real lump will probably be somewhat smaller. The £150 survey figure is our stated assumption and not a quoted price, included because on an awkward property a survey is sometimes what unlocks the claim.

3. Two traps in the small print

Water butts do not count. Severn Trent says this in terms: water butts are not eligible for a reduction, because although they collect some rainwater from roofs, they only store a limited amount and do not prevent all surface water from entering the drainage system. If you were hoping a butt on each downpipe would do it, it will not, and it is better to know that before you spend a Saturday photographing them.

Highway drainage is a separate charge and it stays. This is the one almost every article on the subject gets wrong. Severn Trent's highway drainage page explains that highway drainage is water draining from roads and footpaths into public drainage systems, and that because customers benefit from road drainage directly or indirectly, every customer connected to its sewerage network pays the same contribution. In 2026/27 that is £19.68 a year. Removing your surface water charge does not remove it, and a successful claim leaves your bill showing wastewater only rather than wastewater and surface water drainage.

The only way out of highway drainage is not being on the public sewer at all. Severn Trent says a home that is not connected, for example one with a septic tank, is not charged for it.

Two smaller points worth knowing. If your sewerage services come from a different company than your water, which happens near regional boundaries, you have to apply to the sewerage company, and your bill will tell you which that is. And Severn Trent notes that on newer developments it can genuinely be hard to tell where surface water goes, because some new builds use soakaways or sustainable drainage, some are connected to the company's surface water sewers, and others go to private systems.

The evidence the company asks for is photographs or drainage plans, and it offers a surface water drainage test to help work out whether a property is connected. Applications are considered individually, which is a polite way of saying a clear photograph of a downpipe disappearing into a gravel soakaway does a lot of work.

There is a third thing that catches people out and it is in the wording of the backdating rule rather than the eligibility test. Severn Trent requires that no surface water from your property or any shared area nearby drains directly or indirectly into a public sewer. On a flat, a terrace with a shared yard, or a small development with a communal parking area, that shared area is part of the test even though it is not your property. It is the most common reason a claim that looks obvious from the front garden fails once somebody checks the deeds, and it is worth establishing before you spend an afternoon photographing your own downpipes.

4. A worked example

Sanjay, Nuneaton. Detached 1970s house bought in 2014, metered, long garden, two downpipes at the back that visibly run into gravel pits and two at the front he has never thought about.

He checks the front first, because that is where a claim usually dies. Both front downpipes go into the ground about a metre from the wall, and neither the drive nor the path has a gully anywhere on it. The drive is gravel, so it soaks away rather than running to the road.

He photographs all four downpipes, the drive and the path, and sends them in. The claim is accepted. The surface water drainage charge of £73.44 a year comes off, and because he moved in during 2014, the backdating is limited by the six year rule rather than by his move, so he gets the current year plus six. At this year's rate that would be £514.08. The older years are credited at what he was actually billed in each of them, so with bills rising his real credit comes in somewhat under that ceiling.

His highway drainage charge of £19.68 stays exactly where it was, and he was expecting that because he read the second page rather than just the first. The bill now says wastewater only.

Total cost of the exercise: an hour, and the postage on nothing, because he emailed the photographs. Cost £0 divided by a saving of £73.44 a year is a payback that is immediate, and that is before the backdated lump. This is the highest return per hour of anything in this series, and the reason it goes unclaimed is simply that the bill line is called surface water drainage and means nothing to most people reading it.

The verdict

Go and look at where your downpipes go this weekend. If every one of them, plus your drive and paths, drains into a soakaway or a private system rather than a public sewer, claim, because it costs nothing and it is worth up to £73.44 a year plus up to seven years backdated, a ceiling of £514.08, on Severn Trent's published 2026/27 figures.

If even one downpipe runs to a gully, do not waste the afternoon, because the test is 100 per cent and a water butt will not close the gap. And whatever happens, do not expect the highway drainage charge to move, because it applies to everyone connected to the public sewer.

Frequently asked

How far back can a surface water drainage claim be backdated? Severn Trent states the change takes effect from the later of the start of the current financial year plus six years backdating, or the date you moved into the property. So a long standing resident can get the current year plus six.

Does a water butt qualify me for the rebate? No. Severn Trent says water butts are not eligible, because they store only a limited amount and do not prevent all surface water entering the drainage system. A butt is still worth having for the garden, but the claim needs every roof, drive and path to drain away from the public sewer.

Can I get the highway drainage charge removed too? Not while you are on the public sewer. Severn Trent charges the same highway drainage contribution, £19.68 a year in 2026/27, to every customer connected to its sewerage network, because everyone benefits from roads being drained. Homes with no sewer connection, such as those on a septic tank, are not charged.

My water and sewerage come from different companies. Who do I ask? The sewerage company, not the water company. Severn Trent notes this applies to customers near the edge of its region and that your bill will tell you who provides your sewerage services. The other company's rules and charges will differ, so check its own pages before claiming.
