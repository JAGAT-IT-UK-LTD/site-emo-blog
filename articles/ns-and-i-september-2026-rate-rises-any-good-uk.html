NS&I rates in November 2026 are 4.35% on Premium Bonds, 3.75% on the Direct Saver and 4.99% on the one year bond. The bonds went up again on 6 October, so the fixed deal now beats the famous prize rate by a clear margin. Only the bond rate is money you can count on.

1. What did NS&I change in August and October?

NS&I moved in two steps. On 18 August 2026 it raised Premium Bonds, the Direct Saver, Income Bonds and its fixed bonds.

Then on 6 October 2026 it raised the fixed British Savings Bonds again. The variable accounts and Premium Bonds stayed where they were.

In September the five year bond paid only three hundredths of a point more than the one year bond. That was no reason to lock up money for four extra years.

The October rise widened the gap. One year now pays 4.99% and five years pays 5.17%, a spread of 0.18 of a point.

On £10,000 that is about £18 a year extra for tying your money up until 2031. Better than £3, but still a thin reward for giving up access.

The Direct Saver at 3.75% remains the plain easy access option. Income Bonds pay 3.69% gross each month, which works out at the same 3.75% AER, for people who want the interest paid out.

NS&I also sells Guaranteed Income Bonds with the same terms. The one year Income Bond pays 4.88% gross, 4.99% AER, paid monthly.

2. What happened in the September to November draws?

The new prize fund rate took effect from the September 2026 draw, as promised. NS&I's published draw figures show what it did to the prizes.

Before the September draw NS&I estimated 6,533,031 prizes worth £497,326,725. The real draw came in a little lower, at 6,529,868 prizes.

By November the pot had grown past £500 million. That is because more money was held in Bonds, not because the rate rose again.

The September draw paid 573,172 fewer £25 prizes than August. The pot got bigger, but the prize most small holders actually win became rarer.

That point is worth dwelling on. £50 and £100 prizes each rose from 1,931,214 in August to 2,365,010 in September. Meanwhile £25 prizes fell from 2,289,959 to 1,716,787.

Total prizes went up, which is good. But the mix shifted towards bigger prizes, which flatters the headline rate.

3. Why is 4.35% not your real return?

The prize fund rate is the total prize pot divided by the total money held in Bonds. It is an average across every Bond, including the two £1 million jackpots each month.

Averages with a very long tail sit above what a typical holder gets. Premium Bonds have one of the longest tails in British finance.

Here is the arithmetic with odds of 21,000 to 1 per £1 Bond per month.

With £1,000 in Bonds, you hold 1,000 Bonds. Your expected prizes each month are 1,000 divided by 21,000, about 0.048.

That is roughly one prize every 21 months. In most years you will win nothing, and any win will almost certainly be £25, £50 or £100. The headline rate implies £43.50 a year, which few small holders ever see.

With the maximum £50,000, you hold 50,000 Bonds. Expected prizes are about 2.4 a month, roughly 29 a year.

Now the law of averages starts to work for you. Your result moves much closer to the headline rate, though it is still usually below it.

That is the honest shape of Premium Bonds. The more you hold, the closer you get to the advertised number. A small holding is a lottery ticket with your capital protected, not a savings account.

4. When do Premium Bonds actually win?

Premium Bonds prizes are free of UK Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax. Savings interest is not, once you pass your Personal Savings Allowance.

That allowance is £1,000 for basic rate taxpayers, £500 for higher rate and nothing for additional rate. Some people on low incomes can also use the starting rate for savings, worth up to £5,000.

So for someone who has already used their allowance, compare like with like.

  • Basic rate taxpayer, allowance used: 4.35% tax free equals about 5.44% gross, because 4.35 divided by 0.8 is 5.44.
  • Higher rate taxpayer, allowance used: 4.35% tax free equals about 7.25% gross, because 4.35 divided by 0.6 is 7.25.

Even after October's rise, the best NS&I bond pays 5.17% gross. It falls short of 7.25%, and for a basic rate taxpayer it only just matches 5.44% once taxed.

That is why Premium Bonds suit people with large cash holdings and a large tax bill. But the sum only works if you get near the prize fund rate, which means holding close to £50,000.

At £1,000 the tax advantage is a rounding error on a return you probably will not get. Our guide to tax on savings interest shows where your own line sits.

Worked example: Callum Rhys in Swansea

Callum is a basic rate taxpayer with £6,000 he wants to keep safe. He has earned £120 of savings interest so far this tax year, so his £1,000 Personal Savings Allowance is far from used.

Option one, Premium Bonds. 6,000 Bonds at odds of 21,000 to 1 gives about 0.29 prizes a month. That is roughly three or four prizes a year, most likely £25, £50 or £100.

A plausible year lands somewhere around £100 to £250. The headline 4.35% would imply £261. He might hit it. He might get £75.

Option two, Direct Saver at 3.75%. £6,000 earns £225 a year, guaranteed while the rate holds. His allowance is unused, so he pays no tax on it.

Option three, the one year bond at 4.99%. £6,000 earns £299.40, guaranteed, if he leaves it alone for twelve months.

That is still tax free for him, because £120 plus £299.40 is £419.40, well under £1,000. In September the same bond would have paid him £289.20, so October's rise is worth £10.20 to him.

The answer: option three beats the Premium Bonds average outright, with no luck involved. Premium Bonds only make sense for him once his interest passes £1,000 a year. At 3.75% that means holding roughly £27,000 in cash.

5. Which NS&I rates are worth having now?

NS&I rates are higher in November 2026 than they were in the summer, and that is good news for savers. But rank them by what you will actually receive, not by the size of the number.

The one year Guaranteed Growth Bond at 4.99% is the strongest choice for most people. It beats the Premium Bonds prize fund rate outright and it is certain.

The Direct Saver at 3.75% is a reasonable easy access home. The longer bonds now pay a little more, but only lock in for years if you are sure you will not need the money.

Premium Bonds are worth it in one specific case. You hold a large sum, you have used your Personal Savings Allowance, and you value tax free prizes and total safety above a certain return.

If you have a few thousand pounds and no tax problem, the maths says take the guaranteed 4.99%. You will know exactly what you are getting.

Frequently asked

Is my money safe with NS&I? Yes. NS&I is backed by HM Treasury, so every pound you hold with it is protected, with no upper limit. Banks and building societies are covered by the FSCS instead, which has protected up to £120,000 per person at each authorised firm since 1 December 2025. Above that sum, NS&I is the safer home.

Are Premium Bonds worth it in 2026? For most small savers, no. With £1,000 in Bonds you would expect one prize roughly every 21 months. A guaranteed 3.75% or 4.99% pays more, for certain. Premium Bonds earn their place when you hold a large sum, have used your Personal Savings Allowance and value tax free prizes.

How much can I hold in Premium Bonds? You can hold up to £50,000 in total, and each purchase must be at least £25. You must be 16 or over to buy Bonds for yourself. Adults can buy Bonds for a child under 16, and a parent or guardian looks after them until the child turns 16.

Will NS&I rates go up or down next? Nobody can promise either. NS&I says it sets rates to reflect the wider market and to meet its Net Financing target, which for 2026 to 2027 is £15 billion, give or take £4 billion. Variable rates can change at any time, but a fixed bond keeps its rate for the whole term.

Are Premium Bonds prizes taxed? No. Premium Bonds prizes are free of UK Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax, and they do not use up your Personal Savings Allowance. Interest from the Direct Saver, Income Bonds and Guaranteed Growth Bonds is taxable. It is paid gross, so you only pay tax if your total interest goes over your allowance.
