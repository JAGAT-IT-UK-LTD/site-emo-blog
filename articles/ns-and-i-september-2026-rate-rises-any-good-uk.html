4.35% Is Not a Rate You Will Receive, and NS&I Never Said It Was

The Premium Bonds prize fund rate rose to 4.35% for the September 2026 draw, its second rise this year. In the same week NS&I put its Direct Saver on 3.75% and its one year bond on 4.82%. Only one of those three numbers is money you can count on, and it is not the famous one.

The short version

  • Premium Bonds prize fund rate is 4.35% from the September 2026 draw, up from 3.80%, with odds shortening to 21,000 to 1 from 22,000 to 1.
  • The prize pot rose by about £63 million to over £497 million, with more than 308,000 extra prizes and more than 6.5 million prizes in total.
  • Direct Saver pays 3.75% gross/AER and Income Bonds 3.69% gross, 3.75% AER, both from 18 August 2026.
  • Guaranteed Growth Bonds pay 4.82% for one year and 4.85% for five years. Locking up for five years buys you 0.03 of a percentage point.
  • 4.35% is a mean, not a median. With £1,000 in Bonds you would expect roughly one prize every 21 months, so most years you win nothing.
  • Premium Bonds prizes are free of Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax. For a higher rate taxpayer who has used their allowance, 4.35% tax free is worth about 7.25% gross.
  • There are fewer £25 prizes in September than in August, down from 2,289,959 to an estimated 1,717,659. The extra money went into bigger prizes.

1. What actually moved, and when

NS&I announced the lot on 18 August 2026. The variable and fixed rates changed that day. The Premium Bonds change applies from the September draw.

Look at the last two rows together. The one year bond pays 4.82% and the five year bond pays 4.85%. You are being offered three hundredths of a percentage point to give up access to your money for four extra years. On £10,000 that is £3 a year. There is no case for the five year bond at that spread unless you are certain rates are about to collapse.

The Direct Saver at 3.75% is the plain easy access option. Income Bonds pay 3.69% gross monthly, which is the same 3.75% AER if you reinvest, and are for people who want the interest paid out.

2. Why 4.35% is not your return

The prize fund rate is the total prize pot divided by the total money held in Bonds. It is an average across every Bond in the draw, including the two £1 million prizes. Averages with a very long tail sit above what a typical holder gets, and Premium Bonds have one of the longest tails in British finance.

Here is the arithmetic with the new odds of 21,000 to 1 per £1 Bond per month.

With £1,000 in Bonds, you hold 1,000 Bonds. Your expected number of prizes each month is 1,000 divided by 21,000, which is about 0.048. That is roughly one prize every 21 months. In most twelve month periods you will win nothing at all, and when you do win the prize will almost certainly be at the bottom of the table. Your realistic return over a year is either zero or something like £25 to £100, which is nowhere near £43.50.

With the maximum £50,000, you hold 50,000 Bonds. Expected prizes are 50,000 divided by 21,000, about 2.4 a month, so roughly 29 prizes a year. Now the law of averages starts to work for you and your outcome moves much closer to the headline rate, though still usually below it.

That is the honest shape of Premium Bonds. The more you hold, the closer you get to the advertised number. A small holding is a lottery ticket with your capital protected, not a savings account.

There are 572,300 fewer £25 prizes in September than in August. The pot got bigger and the smallest prize got rarer, which flatters the headline rate and thins the odds of the outcome most people actually get.

That last point is worth dwelling on. NS&I's own estimate shows £100 prizes rising from 1,931,214 to 2,366,135, £50 prizes rising by the same amount, and £25 prizes falling from 2,289,959 to 1,717,659. Total prizes are up, which is good, but the mix has shifted upward. For a small holder, the single most likely win became slightly less likely.

3. The one argument that does hold up

Premium Bonds prizes are free of UK Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax. Interest from a savings account is not, once you have used your Personal Savings Allowance, which is £1,000 for basic rate taxpayers, £500 for higher rate and nothing for additional rate.

So for someone who has already used their allowance, compare like with like.

  • Basic rate taxpayer, allowance used: 4.35% tax free is equivalent to 5.44% gross, because 4.35 divided by 0.8 is 5.44.
  • Higher rate taxpayer, allowance used: 4.35% tax free is equivalent to 7.25% gross, because 4.35 divided by 0.6 is 7.25.

Nothing on the high street pays 7.25% gross. That is a genuinely strong argument, and it is why Premium Bonds are popular with people who have large cash holdings and a large tax bill. But it only works if you actually achieve something near the prize fund rate, which means holding near the £50,000 maximum. At £1,000 the tax advantage is a rounding error on a return you probably will not get.

Worked example: Callum Rhys in Swansea

Callum is a basic rate taxpayer with £6,000 he wants to keep accessible. He has £120 of savings interest so far this tax year, so his £1,000 Personal Savings Allowance is nowhere near used.

Option one, Premium Bonds. 6,000 Bonds against odds of 21,000 to 1 gives about 0.29 prizes a month, roughly three or four prizes a year, most likely at £25, £50 or £100. A plausible year lands somewhere around £100 to £250. The headline 4.35% would imply £261. He might hit it. He might get £75.

Option two, Direct Saver at 3.75%. £6,000 earns £225, guaranteed, and because his allowance is unused he pays no tax on it either.

Option three, the one year bond at 4.82%. £6,000 earns £289.20, guaranteed, if he can leave it alone for twelve months. Still tax free in his case, because £120 plus £289.20 is under £1,000.

The answer: option three beats the Premium Bonds average outright, with no variance at all. Premium Bonds only start to make sense for him once his savings interest is pushing past £1,000 a year, which at 3.75% means holding roughly £27,000 in cash.

4. The honest verdict

Five rates went up and that is genuinely good news for savers. But rank them by what you will actually receive rather than by the size of the number.

The one year Guaranteed Growth Bond at 4.82% is the strongest thing on this list for most people, and it beats the Premium Bonds prize fund rate outright while being certain. The Direct Saver at 3.75% is a reasonable easy access home. The five year bond at 4.85% is not worth the lock-in.

Premium Bonds are worth it in one specific situation: you hold a large sum, you have used your Personal Savings Allowance, and you value the tax free treatment and the total safety more than certainty of return. If you have a few thousand pounds and no tax problem, the maths says take the guaranteed 4.82% and enjoy the fact that you know exactly what you are getting.

Frequently asked

Is my money safe with NS&I? NS&I is backed by HM Treasury, so capital is 100% protected rather than covered to the £85,000 FSCS limit that applies to banks.

How much can I hold in Premium Bonds? Up to £50,000, including for holders under 16.

Will these rates last? They are not fixed except on the Bonds. NS&I says it moves rates to reflect market conditions and to meet its Net Financing target, which for 2026-27 is £15 billion plus or minus £4 billion. Variable rates can be cut at any time.

Are the September prize numbers final? The prize breakdown NS&I publishes ahead of a draw is an estimate. The rate and the odds are set.
