Your credit score is not a number any lender ever sees

Three companies will show you a free score, on three different scales, with three different ideas of what excellent means. None of those numbers is sent to a lender. Here is what is.

The short version

  • Lenders do not consider your score. Per MoneyHelper, they run a hard check on your credit report, which is your actual history.
  • The scales are not comparable. Experian tops out at 999, Equifax at 1000, TransUnion at 710.
  • So does excellent. Experian 961 to 999, Equifax 725 to 1000, TransUnion 628 to 710.

  • Checking your own report does not affect your score, so there is no reason to ration it.

1. What a lender actually looks at

The score is a product. Credit reference agencies build it, and the free apps show it to you, because a number that goes up and down is engaging. MoneyHelper puts the underlying point plainly: having a good credit score is not something lenders consider when providing loans, and it does not guarantee your application will be accepted. What the lender does is run a hard check, a full search of your credit report.

Your report is the file. Accounts you hold, payment record, applications you have made, defaults, county court judgments, electoral roll registration, financial links to other people. The score is one company's guess at what a lender will make of that file. The lender makes its own, using its own rules, its own appetite for risk that month, and information the agency never sees, such as what you told them your income is.

That is why a high score and a refusal are not a contradiction. They are two different organisations scoring two different things.

2. Why the three numbers disagree by design

MoneyHelper sets out the maximums and the bands each agency treats as excellent:

  • Experian: maximum 999, excellent is 961 to 999.
  • Equifax, shown through ClearScore: maximum 1000, excellent is 725 to 1000.
  • TransUnion, shown through Credit Karma: maximum 710, excellent is 628 to 710.

Now do the arithmetic nobody publishes. As a share of each scale, the excellent band is 3.9 per cent of Experian's range, 27.6 per cent of Equifax's, and 11.7 per cent of TransUnion's. Being excellent on one is simply a different achievement from being excellent on another, and comparing the raw numbers is meaningless.

The scores also disagree because the underlying files disagree. Not every lender reports to every agency, so an account that appears in one file may be missing from another. That is the genuinely useful reason to look at more than one.

3. The free report that actually matters

By law, every credit reference agency must give you a copy of your statutory credit report free of charge. MoneyHelper names four UK agencies that compile one: Experian, Equifax, TransUnion and Crediva. Most articles name three and stop.

You can check as often as you like and it will not affect your score. The statutory report is the file itself, which is the thing a lender searches, so it is more useful than any score.

Callum, Stirling. Refused a car loan despite a 958 on one app, which the app called good rather than excellent. Chasing the score would mean months of waiting. Instead he pulls all four statutory reports. Three match. The fourth shows an old mobile account closed in 2023 still marked open, and a financial association with a former housemate from a joint utility bill. Neither is visible in the app score. Both are fixable: a notice of correction and a disassociation request cost nothing and take weeks, not months, and the second one alone stops a stranger's file being pulled into his application.

If the agency will not correct it, the route out is not another app. Under UK data protection law the file has to be accurate, and the ICO sets out both the notice of correction and your right to have wrong information rectified. Report the error to the agency first, give them the evidence, and complain to the ICO only if they refuse.

Be realistic about the clock. Published Financial Ombudsman decisions include cases where a wrong entry took nine months and three separate disputes to clear, and where the compensation for the distress and inconvenience came to £100. That is the honest reason to pull your reports six months before a mortgage application rather than the week you apply.

That is the whole argument. The score tells you a direction. The report tells you the specific thing that is wrong.

4. Are the paid tiers worth it

The free services are genuinely free to you because they are lead generators. They earn from the credit card and loan offers shown next to your score, which is fine as long as you read the "matched for you" list as advertising, not advice.

The paid tiers typically add daily refreshes, alerts, and a simulated impact tool. Judge them on one question: will an alert change what you do?

  • Worth paying for: if you have had identity fraud, or you are inside six months of a mortgage application, an alert when something new appears on your file has real value.
  • Not worth paying for: watching a number move weekly. Nothing you do this month shows up as a different lending decision next month, and the four statutory reports already give you everything a lender searches.

The honest answer for most households is that the free tier plus one annual sweep of all four statutory reports covers it, and the subscription does not.

One thing genuinely worth an alert now: buy now, pay later agreements have moved onto credit files, so what changed when the FCA rules came in decides whether a string of small instalment plans is visible to your next lender.

The verdict

Use the free apps for one thing only: an early warning that something has changed. Ignore the number itself, because the three scales are not comparable and none of them reaches a lender.

Once a year, pull all four statutory reports, which are free by law, and read them for errors, stale accounts and financial associations you no longer want. If you are refused credit, that is the first thing to do, not the last. And if you are about to apply for a mortgage, do it six months early, because corrections take weeks and applications do not wait.

Frequently asked

Does checking my own credit report lower my score? No. MoneyHelper states you can check your credit report as often as you like and it will not affect your score. Only a lender's hard search is recorded as an application.

Which agency should I check? All of them, at least once. MoneyHelper names Experian, Equifax, TransUnion and Crediva as compiling a statutory report, and they can hold different information because not every lender reports to every agency.

Is a 999 score a guarantee? No. MoneyHelper is explicit that a high score does not guarantee acceptance, because the lender assesses your report and its own criteria, not the agency's score.
