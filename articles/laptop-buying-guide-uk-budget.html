1. Why 2026 is different: the Windows 10 cliff edge

On 14 October 2025, Microsoft stopped issuing security updates for Windows 10. The machines did not stop working. They simply stopped being patched, which over time turns an ordinary laptop into an unpatched one holding your email, your banking and your documents.

Microsoft offered a one year consumer Extended Security Updates (ESU) programme to bridge the gap. That programme ends on 13 October 2026, which is also the last day you can enrol, and Microsoft has not announced a second consumer year. From 14 October 2026 a Windows 10 laptop gets no security patches at all unless it is on a business ESU contract. If you are buying second hand in autumn 2026, treat any machine that cannot run Windows 11 as one with a hard stop six weeks away.

The practical result is a flooded second-hand market. Offices and schools have replaced fleets of Windows 10 machines, and those laptops are now on resale sites at tempting prices. Some are genuine bargains. Some cannot run a supported operating system at all. Telling them apart takes five minutes.

In 2026 the most important laptop specification is not the processor. It is whether the machine can legally and safely run a supported operating system.

2. The five-minute check that saves you £500

Before you buy any used or refurbished Windows laptop, confirm it can run Windows 11. The headline requirements are:

  • TPM 2.0 present and enabled
  • Secure Boot capable
  • A supported 64-bit processor, broadly Intel 8th generation or newer, or AMD Ryzen 2000 series or newer
  • 4GB RAM and 64GB storage as the bare minimum, though you should be aiming far higher

Machines much more than eight years old are generally excluded, even when they run flawlessly. If a listing does not state the exact processor model, ask. If the seller will not say, that is your answer.

There are unofficial workarounds to install Windows 11 on unsupported hardware. They exist, they sometimes work, and they can stop receiving updates without warning. For a main machine that holds your bank details, that is not a risk worth taking to save eighty pounds.

4. What is pure marketing

Enormous storage on a slow drive. A 1TB mechanical hard drive sounds generous and feels dreadful. A 256GB SSD beats it every single day.

Touchscreens on cheap laptops. They add cost, weight and glare, and most people stop using them within a fortnight.

Gaming branding on a budget machine. Red keys and angular vents do not make a slow laptop fast. Real gaming laptops cost real money.

"Up to" battery figures. Manufacturer battery claims are measured in ideal conditions. Assume you will get somewhere around 60% of the quoted number in normal use.

Extended warranties sold at the till. You already have statutory rights in the UK. Read the next section before paying for a policy that may duplicate them.

5. Refurbished versus new: the honest comparison

Refurbished laptops typically cost 40% to 70% less than the equivalent new machine, and for a fixed budget you will nearly always get better specifications by buying refurbished. A three-year-old business laptop is usually built better than a new machine at the same price.

The catch in 2026 is age. Refurbished stock from 2022 and earlier may carry 8GB of RAM and older SATA SSDs, and some falls the wrong side of the Windows 11 line. The bargain is real, but only if you check the model rather than the price.

6. Your UK consumer rights when it goes wrong

Buying from a business, including a refurbisher, gives you protection under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. Goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described. In short:

  1. Within 30 days you can reject a faulty item and get a full refund.
  2. Within six months, a fault is assumed to have been there from the start unless the seller proves otherwise, so the burden is on them, not you.
  3. Buying online normally adds a 14 day right to change your mind under distance selling rules, even if nothing is wrong with it.

Buy from a private individual on a marketplace and almost none of this applies. That is the real difference between a cheap listing and a slightly dearer refurbisher, and it is usually worth the gap.

Worked example: Priya needs a laptop for an online course

Priya has £400 and needs something for coursework, video calls and a lot of browser tabs. The new machines at £400 offer 8GB of RAM and a small, slow drive. Tempting, but they will feel tired within a year.

Instead she looks at refurbished business laptops. For £380 she finds a three-year-old model with an 11th generation processor, 16GB of RAM, a 512GB NVMe SSD, a Full HD matte screen and a 12 month warranty from a refurbisher.

She checks two things before paying: the processor generation, which confirms Windows 11 support, and the seller's returns policy. Both pass. She gets roughly double the useful specification of the new £400 laptop, with rights she can enforce if it fails.

The one compromise is the battery, which is at around 85% of its original capacity. For a machine that mostly sits on a desk, that is a fair trade for £300 of saved money.

7. What to buy at each budget

Under £300. Refurbished only, and expect compromises. Prioritise an SSD and a Windows 11 capable processor over everything else. A Chromebook is a genuine alternative if your work is entirely in a browser.

£300 to £600. The sweet spot for refurbished business machines: 16GB RAM, 512GB NVMe, a decent screen and a real warranty. Better value than anything new at this price.

£600 to £1,000. New becomes sensible. Expect 16GB, a fast SSD, a good screen and a battery that lasts a working day.

Over £1,000. You are buying build quality, screen quality, weight and battery life rather than raw speed. Only worth it if you carry it daily or do heavy creative work.

8. The honest verdict

For most UK buyers in 2026, a refurbished business laptop from a proper retailer is the best value on the market, and it is not close. You get roughly double the specification per pound, a warranty, and full consumer rights.

But only after one check. Confirm the processor generation supports Windows 11, insist on 16GB of RAM and an NVMe SSD, and ignore everything the marketing says about the rest.

Do that and £400 buys a machine that will still feel quick in three years. Skip it and £400 buys something you will be replacing by next Christmas.

Frequently asked

Is it safe to keep using Windows 10? It still runs, but without security updates the risk grows over time. If the machine holds anything sensitive, either move to a supported version, take up whatever extended security option applies to you, or replace it.

Can I add more RAM later? Sometimes on business and gaming models, rarely on thin consumer laptops where memory is soldered. Check before you buy rather than after.

Is a Chromebook a real option? Yes, if your work lives in a browser and you do not need Windows software. They are cheap, secure and fast for that use, and useless for anything that needs a desktop application.

Is a Mac worth it? They hold value well and last a long time, but the entry price is high and the second-hand market is far less generous. If budget is the constraint, refurbished Windows wins on specification per pound.
