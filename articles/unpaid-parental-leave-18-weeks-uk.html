Eighteen weeks per child, and the service requirement everyone still quotes is not on the page any more

Ruhul Amin · Published 13 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Unpaid parental leave is the most ignored entitlement in UK employment, partly because it pays nothing and partly because most write-ups still describe a qualifying period that GOV.UK no longer lists. The entitlement belongs to the child, not the job, and it carries across employers.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • 18 weeks per child, for each child and adopted child, up to their 18th birthday.
  • 4 weeks a year per child is the cap, unless your employer agrees otherwise.
  • It is unpaid. That is the whole design, and it is why so few people take it.
  • A week means the length of time you normally work over 7 days. Three days a week means a week of leave is three days.
  • It follows the child, not the job. Ten weeks used at a previous employer leaves eight at the new one.
  • 21 days notice, and it does not have to be in writing unless the employer asks for it.

1. What you get, and what a week actually means

GOV.UK's entitlement page is short and precise. Parental leave is unpaid. You are entitled to 18 weeks' leave for each child and adopted child, up to their 18th birthday. The limit on how much each parent can take in a year is 4 weeks for each child, unless the employer agrees otherwise.

You must take it as whole weeks rather than individual days, unless your employer agrees otherwise or your child is disabled. You do not have to take it all at once.

The definition of a week is where this gets genuinely useful. A week equals the length of time an employee normally works over 7 days. GOV.UK's own example: if an employee works 3 days a week, one week of parental leave equals 3 days. For irregular weeks, the number of days in a week is the total number of days worked in a year divided by 52.

Craig in Dunfermline works 4 days a week and has two children. One week of leave is 4 days for him. His annual cap is 4 weeks per child, which is 16 days a year per child, or 32 days a year across both. Across the whole of a childhood, 18 weeks per child at 4 days a week is 72 days per child.

None of it is paid. That arithmetic is about entitlement, not income, and it is the reason the honest question is whether you can afford to use it rather than whether you have it.

Because a week means your week, the same entitlement is worth a different number of days to everyone. This is that rule worked out across the common patterns, using the GOV.UK definition that one week equals the time you normally work over seven days.

The last column is the one part-time parents read the wrong way round. Eighteen weeks sounds identical for everybody, but a three-day-a-week parent is entitled to 54 days off for that child and a five-day parent to 90. Nobody is being short-changed, because both are giving up the same eighteen weeks of pay to take it. It only looks unfair if you count the days and forget the leave is unpaid either way.

2. The eligibility list, and what is missing from it

GOV.UK lists four conditions, and says employees qualify if all of them apply:

  • they are named on the child's birth or adoption certificate, or they have or expect to have parental responsibility
  • they are not self employed or a worker, for example an agency worker or contractor
  • they are not a foster parent, unless they have secured parental responsibility through the courts
  • the child is under 18

Read that list again and notice what is not in it. There is no length of service requirement. A great many guides, including ones that look current, still state that you need a year's service with your employer before you can take parental leave. That condition does not appear on GOV.UK's eligibility page as read on 13 September 2026. Pay works on a different test again, and Maternity Allowance, the payment for people Statutory Maternity Pay does not reach, turns on recent work and earnings rather than service with one employer.

We are reporting what the page says rather than asserting how it got there, and the page itself is the thing to check before you rely on it either way. But if your HR handbook or a recruitment blog tells you that you must wait a year, that is a claim worth testing against the government's own list rather than accepting.

Two other points from the same page. Employers can ask for proof, such as a birth certificate, as long as it is reasonable to do so, and GOV.UK says they cannot ask for proof each time you request leave. And employers can choose to offer parental leave to groups who are not eligible, which is worth checking in your staff handbook because it costs them nothing in wages.

3. Notice, carrying it over, and your protected rights

The notice period is 21 days before your intended start date. If you or your partner are having a baby or adopting, it is 21 days before the week the baby or child is expected. You must confirm the start and end dates in your notice, and GOV.UK says that unless your employer requests it, this does not have to be in writing. Putting it in writing anyway is free and removes an argument later.

The entitlement applies to each child, not to an individual's job. GOV.UK's example is direct: an employee is entitled to 18 weeks, they have used 10 with a previous employer, and they can use up to 8 weeks with their new employer if they are eligible. Nobody resets your balance by hiring you, and equally nobody refreshes it.

Your employment rights, including holidays and returning to your job, are protected during unpaid parental leave. GOV.UK has a separate page on employee rights when on leave that covers that in more detail.

Employers can postpone leave in some circumstances, and GOV.UK covers that on its delaying leave page. We have not reproduced the postponement rules here, because they come with conditions that deserve reading in full rather than in summary.

What can parental leave be used for? GOV.UK's own list is broader than people assume: attending the birth of your child, spending more time with your children, looking at new schools, settling children into new childcare arrangements, and spending more time with family such as visiting grandparents.

One thing the guidance says that almost no employer volunteers: your employer can postpone the leave, but only inside tight limits. There has to be a significant reason, and the example GOV.UK gives is serious disruption to the business. If they do postpone, they must write to you explaining why within 7 days of your request, they must suggest a new start date within 6 months of the one you asked for, and they cannot change the amount of leave you asked for. Three weeks requested stays three weeks.

There are three situations where it cannot be postponed at all: where the employer has no significant reason; where it is the father or partner taking it immediately after the birth or adoption; and where postponing it would mean you no longer qualify, for example pushing it past the child’s 18th birthday. That last one is worth knowing if your child is 17. There is nowhere left to move the leave to, so there is nothing to negotiate about.

The verdict

This is a real entitlement that almost nobody uses, and the reason is straightforward: it pays nothing, and most households cannot simply remove four weeks of income. Saying otherwise would be dishonest. If money is the constraint, this does not help you.

Where it does help is at the edges. A school transition week, a childcare gap in August, a few days around a hospital appointment. Those are exactly the situations GOV.UK names, they are usually short, and taking them as parental leave protects your job and your holiday entitlement rather than burning annual leave or asking for a favour.

Two things to do. Check your staff handbook, because an employer offering anything above the statutory minimum will have written it down. And if you have been told you need a year's service, check GOV.UK's eligibility page yourself before accepting it.

Frequently asked

Is parental leave paid? No. GOV.UK states plainly that parental leave is unpaid. Statutory maternity, paternity and shared parental pay are separate entitlements with their own rules.

Can I take it a day at a time? Only if your employer agrees, or if your child is disabled. Otherwise it must be taken as whole weeks, where a week is the length of time you normally work over 7 days.

Does it apply to agency workers and contractors? No. GOV.UK's eligibility list excludes people who are self employed or a worker, giving agency workers and contractors as the examples.

How much notice do I have to give? 21 days before the intended start date, or 21 days before the week the baby or child is expected if you or your partner are having a baby or adopting.
