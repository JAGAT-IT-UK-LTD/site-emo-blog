Premium Bonds Advertise 4.35%. Over Half Of Small Holders Will Win Nothing All Year.

NS&I publishes two numbers on the same page: a 4.35% annual prize fund rate, and odds of 21,000 to 1 per £1 Bond per draw. Almost every article quotes the first. Put them together and the second tells you something the first hides, which is that the advertised rate describes the pot, not the person.

The short version

  • The prize fund rate is 4.35%, variable, and the odds are 21,000 to 1 for every £1 Bond in each monthly draw. Both figures are NS&I's own, checked 3 September 2026.
  • The 4.35% is an average across every Bond in the draw, including the two £1 million prizes. It is not what a typical holder receives, and NS&I does not claim it is.
  • On a £1,000 holding our calculation gives roughly a 56% chance of winning nothing at all across a whole year. The most likely single outcome is zero.
  • If every prize you ever won was the minimum £25, your return would be about 1.43% regardless of how much you hold. That is the floor the advertised rate is averaged above.
  • The tax-free status is worth nothing to most people, because at 4.35% a basic rate taxpayer's £1,000 Personal Savings Allowance already covers about £22,988 of savings.
  • Maximum holding £50,000, minimum £25, and your capital is never at risk in nominal terms.

1. What the prize fund rate actually measures

NS&I's Premium Bonds page states the annual prize fund rate as 4.35%, variable, checked on 3 September 2026. That figure is accurate and NS&I is not being misleading about it. The problem is entirely in how it gets repeated.

The prize fund rate describes the size of the prize pot relative to the total value of all Bonds in the draw. If every Bond in existence were owned by one person, that person would receive 4.35% a year. Spread across millions of holders, it is an average, and the distribution behind that average is nothing like a savings account.

A savings account paying 4.35% pays every holder 4.35%. Premium Bonds paying an average of 4.35% pay most people less than that and a very small number of people spectacularly more. Those are different products described with the same units, which is why the comparison confuses people.

Prizes run from £25 to £1 million, with two £1 million prizes every month. Those two prizes alone pull the average up for everybody else, and they are won by two people out of millions.

2. The second number, and what it does to the first

On the same page, NS&I publishes the odds: 21,000 to 1 for every £1 Bond in the monthly prize draw, variable. Each £1 you hold is a separate entry with that chance, every month.

This is the number that makes the arithmetic possible, and it is the one that gets left out of almost every article on the subject.

With a holding of £X you own X Bonds. Across twelve monthly draws you have 12X entries, each at 21,000 to 1. So your expected number of prizes per year is 12X divided by 21,000. Everything below follows from that and from NS&I's two published figures. The calculations are ours.

Look at the last column, because it is the one that reframes the product. The all-minimum-prize return is 1.43% at every holding size. It does not improve with scale, because the odds per pound do not change. That 1.43% is the floor. The advertised 4.35% is the average. The distance between those two numbers is made up entirely of the larger prizes, and whether you get any is chance.

3. The typical outcome, not the average one

For a small holder the gap between average and typical is the entire story.

On £1,000, our calculation puts the chance of winning nothing across a full twelve draws at roughly 56%. So the single most likely outcome for a £1,000 holder, over a year, is zero. Not 4.35%, not £43.50, nothing at all. And in the 44% of cases where they do win something, it is overwhelmingly likely to be a single £25 prize.

Meanwhile the average across all £1,000 holders really is £43.50, because a handful of them win large prizes and drag the mean up. Both statements are true at once. That is what an average does to a skewed distribution.

By £5,000 the picture changes. The chance of a blank year drops to around 6%, and by £20,000 it is negligible. Larger holdings do not get better odds per pound, but they get enough entries for the law of averages to start doing its work, so their actual return converges towards the low end of the range rather than swinging between nothing and something.

None of this is NS&I hiding anything. Both numbers are on the front of the product page. It is that one of them gets quoted a thousand times more often than the other.

4. Worked example: Gareth in Swansea

Worked example: Gareth in Swansea

Gareth is a basic rate taxpayer with £15,000 in savings and no ISA. He is choosing between Premium Bonds and an easy-access account, and the thing that appeals about Premium Bonds is that the prizes are tax free.

First, is the tax break worth anything to him? His Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000. At 4.35%, £1,000 of interest corresponds to a balance of £22,988. Gareth has £15,000, so a savings account at that rate would pay him about £652 of interest, comfortably inside his allowance.

His tax bill on the savings account would be nil. So the tax-free feature of Premium Bonds, the thing that drew him in, is worth exactly £0 to him. This is the single most common misunderstanding about the product, and it is covered in more detail in our guide to tax on savings interest.

Second, what does he actually expect from each? On £15,000, his expected number of prizes is 15,000 multiplied by 12, divided by 21,000, which is 8.57 prizes a year. If all of them were £25 he would receive £214, a return of 1.43%. The average outcome across all holders like him is £652, matching the 4.35%, but he only reaches that average if he wins something above the minimum.

The comparison, stated plainly. A savings account paying a comparable rate would give him a known amount, tax free in his circumstances because of his allowance. Premium Bonds give him a range whose floor is about a third of that and whose ceiling is a million pounds.

What he is actually choosing is not a better return. It is a worse expected return for most outcomes in exchange for a small chance of a large one, with no risk to the capital itself. That can be a perfectly reasonable thing to want. It is just not what the 4.35% is telling him.

5. When Premium Bonds genuinely win

This piece is sceptical about the headline rate, not about the product. There are situations where Premium Bonds are clearly the right answer.

If you are an additional rate taxpayer. Your Personal Savings Allowance is £0, so every pound of savings interest is taxed at your marginal rate. Tax-free prizes are worth real money to you, and the tax saving can more than cover the gap between the expected return and a taxed account.

If you are a higher rate taxpayer with a large balance. Your allowance is £500, which at 4.35% covers about £11,494. Above that, tax starts biting and the tax-free comparison starts to matter.

If you have already filled your ISA allowance and are looking for somewhere tax-efficient for the next tranche.

If you value the safety more than the return. Premium Bonds are backed by HM Treasury rather than covered by the deposit guarantee scheme, and the maximum holding is £50,000. For someone holding more than the protected limit at a single bank, that is a genuine structural argument.

If you know yourself well enough to know you would spend it. Money that is slightly awkward to reach is money you still have. That is a behavioural reason rather than a financial one, and it is a perfectly good one.

What is not a good reason is believing you will get 4.35%.

6. The honest verdict

Premium Bonds are a safe home for money with a lottery attached. There is nothing wrong with that and a lot of people enjoy it. The advertised 4.35% is a genuine figure describing the prize pot, and NS&I publishes the odds right beside it, so nothing is being concealed.

But if you hold a few thousand pounds, the realistic outcome is a small number of £25 prizes, and on £1,000 the most likely outcome for a year is nothing at all. The floor if you only ever win minimum prizes is about 1.43%, at every holding size.

Before choosing them for the tax break, check whether you would actually pay any tax. For a basic rate taxpayer at these rates, savings below roughly £23,000 generate interest inside the Personal Savings Allowance anyway, and the tax-free feature is worth nothing.

Hold them because you like the possibility, or because your allowance really is used up. Do not hold them because a number that describes the pot got read as a number describing you.

Frequently asked

Are Premium Bonds covered by the FSCS? They are not covered by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme in the way a bank deposit is, because NS&I is backed directly by HM Treasury. In practice that is generally regarded as at least as strong, and it applies to the whole balance up to the GBP 50,000 maximum rather than to a per-bank limit.

Do the odds improve if I hold more Bonds? No. The odds are fixed per GBP 1 Bond per draw, currently 21,000 to 1. Holding more Bonds gives you more entries, not better entries. What changes with a larger holding is consistency, not the rate.

The same gap between a headline rate and a received one shows up next door at the same institution. NS&I's 4.35% is not a rate you will receive, and NS&I never said it was, which is worth checking before moving money out of bonds and into one of its own accounts on the strength of an advertised figure.

Does inflation matter here? Yes, and it applies to any cash savings. Your capital is safe in nominal terms, meaning you will always get your money back, but its purchasing power falls if prizes and inflation diverge. That is true of a savings account too, so it is not a point against Premium Bonds specifically.

Can I buy them for a child? Yes. Bonds can be bought as a gift for a child under 16. The holder must otherwise be 16 or over with a UK bank account.
