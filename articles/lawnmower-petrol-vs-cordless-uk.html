1. What each one costs to fuel, per cut and per year

Petrol. The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero publishes UK average pump prices every week. For the week commencing 7 September 2026, unleaded petrol averaged 164.40 pence per litre, of which 52.95p is fuel duty and 20 per cent is VAT.

A walk behind petrol mower with a typical 125 to 160cc engine uses somewhere around 0.7 litres an hour under load. A medium lawn takes about 40 minutes, so that is 0.47 litres, or 77p per cut. Thirty cuts across a British growing season is £23.10 a year. If that running cost is what pushes you towards taking the lawn out altogether, the ten year arithmetic on artificial grass against real turf is the next thing to read.

Cordless. Battery energy is voltage times amp hours. A common configuration is two 18 volt, 5.2 amp hour batteries used in series to make a 36 volt system, which is 2 × 18 × 5.2 = 187.2 watt hours. Allowing for charger losses of about 15 per cent, a full charge draws roughly 0.22 kWh from the wall.

At the Ofgem price cap electricity rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 26.32p per kWh, that is 5.8p per full charge. One charge per cut, thirty cuts a year: £1.74 a year.

An honest note on that figure. We could not obtain a manufacturer published runtime in minutes for a specific model, so the electricity cost is expressed per charge rather than per hour. It does not matter: at under two pounds a year the number is too small to change any decision, whichever way you slice it. Petrol costs about thirteen times as much to fuel, and thirteen times almost nothing is still almost nothing.

2. The costs that are not fuel, which are the ones that decide it

A petrol mower is an internal combustion engine, so it needs an annual service: oil change, spark plug, air filter, blade sharpen, carburettor check. A shop typically charges £50 to £80 for this. Doing it yourself costs about £20 in parts and half a Saturday.

A cordless mower needs none of that. It needs a blade sharpened occasionally and that is the whole list. But it has a cost the petrol mower does not: the battery is a consumable.

Lithium batteries lose capacity with cycles and with age. A pair of 5 Ah batteries costs somewhere around £150, and a mower battery used thirty times a year will typically be noticeably weaker after four or five years even though it is nowhere near its cycle limit, because calendar ageing does most of the damage. Call it £150 every four and a half years, or £33 a year.

That is the finding this article exists for. The cordless mower's running cost is not the electricity. It is the battery, and it is nineteen times the electricity. We found exactly the same thing when we looked at the true cost of a cordless vacuum, where the sealed battery turned out to be the entire ownership question.

Read the cordless payback cell twice, because it contains the answer. £95 of price premium divided by £48.03 a year is 2.0 years if you pay a shop to service your petrol mower. Divided by £8.03 a year it is 11.4 years if you service it yourself. Same mowers, same lawn, same fuel prices, and a payback that differs by a factor of six depending on one habit.

And look at the corded column, which almost nobody considers and which wins the money argument outright. A corded electric mower costs £120, has no battery to replace, needs no servicing, and costs under four pounds a year to run. It is cheaper to buy and cheaper to own than everything else on the table. What it costs you is the cable, and whether dragging one around your garden is tolerable depends entirely on your garden. On a small flat rectangle it is a non-issue and you are being sold a battery you do not need.

4. The battery platform test

If you buy cordless, this is the question that decides whether you own a mower for ten years or five.

Does the battery fit anything else you own or will own? The major platforms, Makita LXT, Bosch, DeWalt, Einhell Power X-Change and the rest, share one battery across dozens of tools. If your drill, hedge trimmer and strimmer all take the same pack, then the mower's battery is not really a mower cost at all, spares are easy to find for years, and a dead pack is a £75 item rather than a crisis.

If the battery is a proprietary shape used only by that one mower, from a brand you had not heard of before the listing, then you are buying an appliance whose life is capped by the availability of one part. When that part is discontinued, the mower is scrap. This is the same do-not-buy signal that runs through every cordless product we have looked at, and it matters more here than the price.

Two other practical points. Warranty length is a reasonable proxy for a manufacturer's confidence, and Einhell, as one example, offers a three year warranty on its own site. Check whether the battery itself is covered for the same period as the tool, because frequently it is not. And check whether the mower ships with two batteries or one, because a single 5 Ah pack will not finish a medium lawn without a recharge break.

5. Worked example: Gurpreet in Wolverhampton

Gurpreet has a 180 square metre lawn that takes about 45 minutes. His fifteen year old petrol mower has finally died and the shop wants £290 for a like for like replacement or £385 for a cordless model on a platform he already owns, because his hedge trimmer and drill take the same 18 volt packs.

He has always paid the shop £65 for the annual service, because he does not enjoy draining sump oil.

Petrol total: £23.10 of fuel plus £65 of servicing, £88.10 a year.
Cordless total: £1.74 of electricity plus £33.33 of amortised battery, £35.07 a year.
Saving: £53.03 a year.

Price premium £95, divided by £53.03, is 1.8 years.

He buys the cordless one. The platform decision is doing a lot of work here: because he already has four batteries for other tools, he buys the bare unit rather than the kit, which drops the premium to almost nothing and takes the payback under a year.

The thing he did not budget for: the cordless mower is quiet enough to use at eight on a Sunday morning without anybody complaining, which he values more than the £53. That is not a number, and it is the reason most people who switch do not switch back.

6. The honest verdict

If you pay a shop for the annual service, go cordless. It pays back in about two years and removes the service entirely.

If you service your own petrol mower, the cordless payback is 11.4 years and the money argument fails. Switch if you want the quiet and the convenience, but do not pretend it is about the bill.

If you have a small flat lawn near a socket, buy a corded electric mower. It is the cheapest thing on the table to buy and to own, it has no battery to die, and nobody will sell it to you because there is no margin in it.

And whatever you do, buy into a battery platform you already own or one of the big ones. A proprietary battery from an unfamiliar brand is a timer on the whole machine.

If you are buying

The two things worth comparing are the battery platform, because that determines whether spares exist in six years, and whether the battery carries the same warranty as the mower, because often it does not. Cutting width matters more than motor power for how long the job takes. A spare blade and a sharpening file are the only consumables a cordless mower has.

Frequently asked

Will a cordless mower cope with long or wet grass? Less well than petrol, and this is the genuine performance gap rather than a marketing one. A petrol engine holds its power as the load rises; a battery mower slows and drains fast. If you regularly let the lawn get away from you, or you cut a paddock rather than a lawn, petrol still wins on capability. For a lawn cut weekly in season, modern brushless cordless models are entirely adequate.

Is it worth keeping an old petrol mower running instead of replacing it? Usually yes, and this is the greenest and cheapest option by a distance. A mower that starts, cuts and has no major fault is worth servicing at £65 rather than replacing at £290, and the arithmetic above only applies once the old one is genuinely finished. The same logic we applied to repairing or replacing a washing machine holds here.

What about robot mowers? A different product with a different sum. They cut little and often rather than weekly, use very little energy per session, and cost considerably more to buy, with a perimeter wire or satellite guidance to install. The battery platform question applies to them even more sharply, because the battery is usually not user replaceable at all.
