DIY Jobs to Save Money at Home, and the Ones to Never Attempt

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Updated 18 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026

The DIY jobs that save the most money in a UK home are the small, low risk ones: draughtproofing, bleeding radiators, resealing a bath, clearing gutters and decorating. Gas work is off limits, and some electrical jobs in England must be notified to building control. Get that split right and you keep the saving without paying a professional to undo your work later.

This matters more this winter. Ofgem's cap puts gas at 7.97p per kWh from 1 October to 31 December 2026, so every unit of heat you stop leaking is money back in your pocket.

The short version
  • Most DIY savings come from small, frequent, low risk jobs, not dramatic ones.
  • Gas fittings may only be worked on by someone competent, and anyone doing it as work must be in a class approved by HSE, which in practice means Gas Safe registered. Leave gas alone.
  • In England only three kinds of electrical work are notifiable under Part P: a new circuit, a new consumer unit, and changes to a circuit inside a bathroom's special location. Like for like swaps elsewhere are not.
  • At the current cap gas rate of 7.97p per kWh, cutting 5 percent from a 12,000 kWh year saves about £47.82.
  • The expensive mistake is not the job you cannot do. It is the job you half do and then pay someone to undo.

How do you decide which DIY jobs to do yourself?

Before you start any job, ask one question: if I get this wrong, what is the worst outcome?

Filling a crack badly means sanding it back and trying again. Wiring a consumer unit badly can mean a fire, or a house you struggle to sell without certificates.

Those two outcomes are not on the same scale. The difference is not about skill. It is about consequence.

Everything below is sorted by that question rather than by difficulty. Plenty of dangerous jobs are technically easy, and plenty of safe jobs are fiddly.

Which DIY jobs save money around the house?

These are the DIY jobs to save money that carry a low downside. If you are slow or imperfect, you lose an afternoon, not a floor.

What is a DIY afternoon worth? A worked example

Take the Hughes family in a 1930s semi in Leeds. Their annual statement shows 12,000 kWh of gas a year. That is their own reading, used here as an example, not a national average.

At Ofgem's cap rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, gas costs 7.97p per kWh on direct debit. Their 12,000 kWh therefore costs about £956.40 a year in unit charges.

The standing charge is 29.68p a day, or about £108.33 over a year. DIY cannot touch that part. Only the units move.

5 percent600£47.82
10 percent1,200£95.64

We cannot promise any particular percentage. It depends on how leaky the house is to start with.

The point is the shape of the sum. Draughtproofing, a loft top up and balanced radiators are cheap materials and a weekend. If they trim even a small share of gas use, they pay back in a winter or two, and keep paying.

Which home jobs must you never DIY?

Gas. Regulation 3 of the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 says nobody may do any work on a gas fitting unless they are competent. Anyone doing it as an employee or self employed must also be in a class approved by HSE, which means the Gas Safe Register.

HSE's guidance is plain that installation and reinstallation of gas appliances must be done by a Gas Safe registered engineer. Carbon monoxide does not announce itself. No saving is worth it.

Consumer units, new circuits and work in a special location. In England these are notifiable building work. A registered electrician can self certify. If you do it yourself, you must involve building control or a registered third party certifier, which often costs more than hiring an electrician in the first place.

Structural work. Removing a wall, cutting into joists, altering a chimney breast. Even a non load bearing wall can carry pipes and cables. You need a structural engineer and building regulations approval.

Asbestos. HSE says asbestos was used widely until it was banned in 1999, and warns about buildings dating from before 2000. Think Artex ceilings, old floor tiles, garage roofs, boiler flues and soffits. Do not sand, drill or break it. Get it tested. If it is intact and left alone, leaving it is often the right answer. Our asbestos survey and removal guide covers the next steps.

Roof work above single storey. A roofer has scaffolding, insurance and the experience to spot what else is wrong while up there. A ladder and a sunny Saturday are not the same thing.

Part P notifiable work: what counts and what does not

Most guides say "electrical work in a bathroom needs an electrician". That is not what the rule says, and the vagueness costs people money both ways.

Approved Document P for England, the 2013 edition, took effect on 6 April 2013. It cut notifiable work down to three things. Everything else is non notifiable.

JobNotifiable to building control?
Installing a new circuitYes
Replacing a consumer unitYes
Adding to or altering an existing circuit inside a special locationYes
Adding to or altering an existing circuit outside a special locationNo
Replacement, repair and maintenance, anywhere in the houseNo
Connecting fixed equipment to an existing circuit or isolatorNo

A special location is smaller than a bathroom. It is the space around the bath tap or shower head. It runs from the finished floor up to 2.25 metres, or to the shower head if that is higher.

Horizontally, it reaches 0.6 metres from the edge of a bath or shower tray, or 1.2 metres from the centre of the shower head where there is no tray. A room with a swimming pool or sauna heater counts too.

So changing a light fitting on the far side of a large bathroom, on an existing circuit, is not notifiable. Swapping the shower itself, inside the zone, is.

The honest part. Non notifiable does not mean unregulated. The work must still be safe and meet the wiring standard, BS 7671.

A built in cooker shows how fine the line is. Connecting it to an existing cooker circuit is not notifiable. Running a new circuit from the consumer unit to feed it is.

If you have already done notifiable work without telling anyone, the 2013 edition offers a route many people miss. A registered third party can inspect and certify work done by someone who is not registered.

Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland run their own rules, so do not assume any of this applies outside England.

Which jobs are legal but usually a bad idea?

  • Plastering. Perfectly legal, genuinely hard. A plasterer does a room in a day to a finish you will not reach in three. If you must, try a small hidden area first.
  • Tiling a wet area. The tiling is the easy part. The waterproofing behind it is what fails, and it fails out of sight for a long time.
  • Laying laminate or engineered flooring. Achievable and satisfying. Subfloor preparation, expansion gaps and door undercuts are where it goes wrong. Watch full tutorials before you start.
  • Fitting a kitchen. The carcasses are straightforward. The worktop, the plumbing and the electrics are not, and those are the parts you end up paying for anyway.

If a bathroom is on your list, read what a bathroom refit actually costs before deciding which parts to do yourself.

Should you buy, hire or borrow tools?

Tool spending is where DIY savings quietly disappear. A rough rule helps.

  • Buy what you will use more than three times: a decent cordless drill, a screwdriver set, a spirit level, a utility knife, a filling knife, a caulking gun and safety glasses. Mid range is fine. The cheapest is a false economy on anything with a motor.
  • Hire anything large and occasional: floor sander, SDS breaker, tile cutter, scaffold tower, carpet cleaner. A day's hire is a fraction of the purchase price, and you do not have to store it.
  • Borrow. Many UK areas have a library of things or a tool library, often run by a council or community group. They lend tools for a small membership fee and are underused.

Buy consumables at a trade merchant rather than a DIY shed where you can. Most do not need an account, and paint, timber, screws and sealant are often cheaper.

Does DIY affect insurance and selling the house?

This is the part that turns a saving into a cost years later.

  • Home insurance. Many policies exclude damage caused by poor workmanship, including your own. If your DIY plumbing floods the kitchen, read your policy wording before assuming you are covered.
  • Building regulations. GOV.UK warns that you may have to correct the work or pay a fine if building regulations are not followed. Missing certificates also come up in conveyancing questions when you sell.
  • Use a competent person scheme. A tradesperson in one of these schemes can self certify, and will tell the council for you. Check them on the official register, not the side of the van.
  • Keep the evidence. Photograph what is behind a wall before you close it up. Keep receipts and certificates. It costs nothing now and is worth real money later.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do my own electrical work in the UK? In England you can do non notifiable work yourself, such as replacing a light fitting like for like or adding a spur to an existing circuit outside a special location. A new circuit, a new consumer unit, or changes inside a bathroom's special location are notifiable. The work must still meet BS 7671. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own rules.

Is it illegal to do your own gas work? The law says nobody may work on a gas fitting unless they are competent, and anyone doing it as work must be Gas Safe registered. HSE says installing or reinstalling gas appliances must be done by a Gas Safe engineer. For a householder, the safe answer is simple: do not touch gas pipework, boilers, hobs or fires.

Does DIY electrical work affect home insurance? It can. Many policies exclude damage caused by faulty workmanship, and notifiable work without a certificate gives an insurer a reason to question a claim. Read your own policy wording, keep evidence of what you did, and use a registered electrician for anything notifiable. That protects both your claim and a future sale.

What are the best DIY jobs to save money on energy? Draughtproofing, bleeding and balancing radiators, a loft insulation top up and radiator reflectors behind radiators on outside walls. They need cheap materials and little skill. At the cap gas rate of 7.97p per kWh, a household using 12,000 kWh saves about £47.82 for every 5 percent cut.

How do I find a tradesperson who will not overcharge? Get three written, itemised quotes from people who have visited the property. Check Gas Safe or electrical competent person registration on the official register, not on the van. Be wary of anyone asking for a large deposit for materials on a small job, and never pay the full sum upfront.

The verdict

Do the small, reversible, unglamorous jobs and do them properly. Pay a professional for anything involving gas, notifiable electrics, structure, asbestos or heights, and do not treat that as a defeat.
