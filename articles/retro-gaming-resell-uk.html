Selling Retro Games in the UK: Where the Real Profit Is

Published 21 July 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026 · 9 min read

Selling retro games in the UK pays best on boring, complete boxed titles, not famous rarities. Buy at car boots, charity shops and job lots, price from eBay sold listings, and expect a hobby wage rather than a fortune. UK private sellers on eBay pay no final value fee on most items, but buying to resell is trading, so the tax rules apply.

That matters because the hourly rate is lower than most videos suggest. The worked example below shows the real sums.

The short version
  • The money is not in the famous rare games. It is in boring complete boxed titles that sell steadily at £15 to £40 and that nobody writes YouTube videos about.
  • Complete in box is worth far more than a cartridge or disc alone. The box and manual are usually why a listing sells at all.
  • Buying at UK car boots and charity shops still works. Buying on eBay to resell on eBay almost never does, because the price is already the market price.
  • eBay private sellers in the UK pay no final value fee on most categories. The buyer pays a buyer protection fee instead, from 10p plus 7% of the first £20.
  • This is trading, not decluttering. The trading allowance is £1,000 for 2026 to 27 and rises to £3,000 from 6 April 2027. It counts what buyers pay you, not your profit.
  • Counterfeit cartridges are widespread, especially Game Boy and DS. Learning to spot them is the single most valuable skill in this hobby.

The one repeatable eBay angle is badly titled listings. Search misspellings, "job lot", "bundle" and "untested".

Also look for listings with no photographs of the box. Those are underpriced because nobody finds them.

Job lots are where the real margin is. A box of forty mixed games at £60 often holds three or four worth £25 to £40 each. The rest bundles out. That is the actual business model of flipping retro games.

2. Which retro games actually sell?

Forget the headline rarities. You will not find a sealed copy of anything valuable at a car boot.

If you do, you will be competing with people who know far more than you.

The steady middle is where the money is:

  • Complete boxed PAL games for PS1, PS2, N64, GameCube, Mega Drive and SNES, in the £15 to £40 range
  • Original controllers, in working order. Genuinely scarce, always in demand, and people pay well
  • Official power supplies, AV leads and memory cards. Deeply unglamorous, consistently profitable
  • Handhelds that work, especially with the original box
  • Strategy guides and manuals, which people buy to complete a game they already own

What sits and does not sell: sports titles from any year, loose discs with heavy scratching, and mass produced PS2 titles that sold millions.

Add anything with water damage, and consoles missing their leads.

The pattern is simple. Print run and completeness drive value far more than nostalgia does. A game everyone loved that sold millions of copies is worth very little.

3. How do you spot fake retro games?

This is the skill that separates people who make money from people who lose it.

Counterfeit cartridges are widespread. Selling one unknowingly still lands the dispute on you.

Cartridges, Game Boy, NES, SNES, N64, DS:

  • Weight and feel. Genuine cartridges are heavier and the plastic has a specific density. Once you have handled a few real ones, fakes feel wrong immediately.
  • The screw. Nintendo used a proprietary tri-wing or 3.8mm security bit. A standard Phillips screw is a very strong signal.
  • Label quality. Look for pixelation, a slightly wrong colour, a shiny finish where the original was matte, or a label that sits proud of the recess.
  • The board inside. If you can open it, genuine boards have proper manufacturer markings and a battery holder that is soldered, not glued.
  • Save function. Many reproductions cannot save, or lose the save on power off.
  • Region text and rating logos. Fakes frequently mix a PAL label with NTSC board text.

Discs: check the inner ring text and the label print quality. A colour laser printed disc face is a reproduction.

If you are not sure, do not sell it as genuine. List it honestly as unverified, or do not list it.

Selling a counterfeit, even unknowingly, is a consumer protection problem as well as an eBay account problem. Unfair trading rules now sit in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024.

4. Should you clean retro games before selling?

Yes, gently. Cleaning adds value. Over cleaning destroys it.

Safe and worth doing:

  • Isopropyl alcohol on a cotton bud for cartridge contacts. This fixes most "does not work" cartridges and is the single highest return five minutes in the hobby
  • A barely damp microfibre on plastic shells
  • A soft brush for dust in vents and seams
  • Disc resurfacing for light scratches

Do not do:

  • Do not clean labels with anything wet. You will lift the print and halve the value in one wipe
  • Do not use abrasive cleaners or magic sponges on shells. They dull the texture permanently
  • Do not repaint or retrobright unless you know what you are doing. Buyers can tell, and it is treated as damage
  • Do not throw away a battered box. A tatty box is worth far more than no box

Test everything before listing. "Untested" knocks a large slice off the price.

Testing takes two minutes if you have the console set up.

5. Where to sell retro games, and what it costs

For most sellers, eBay is still the deepest market for retro games in the UK. It is also the cheapest place to sell as a private seller.

eBay says UK private sellers pay no final value fee on most categories. You get 300 free listings a month, then 35p each.

The buyer pays instead. eBay's buyer protection fee is 10p plus 7% of the price up to £20, then 4% from £20 to £300.

On a £30 game, the buyer pays £1.90 on top. Buyers notice that, so price a little below rival listings.

Gross margin £423. Then the costs nobody includes:

  • Postage and packaging, about £76
  • Fuel to six locations, about £45
  • eBay private seller fees, £0
  • Time: roughly 26 hours including buying, testing, cleaning, listing and posting

Net about £302, before tax, for 26 hours. That is £11.60 an hour.

That is less than most paid weekend work.

Now the tax side. If Dan keeps this up all year, buyers pay him about £6,816 (£568 times 12). That is well over the £1,000 allowance, and over the £3,000 one from April 2027.

He also passes 30 sales a year easily. So eBay will report him to HMRC, and he needs a Self Assessment return.

The honest reading: this is a hobby that pays for itself and a bit more. People who enjoy the hunt do well, because the time does not feel like work.

People doing it purely for money usually stop within a year, once they work out the hourly rate.

7. How do you price retro games properly?

  • Use eBay sold listings, not active ones. Active listings are what people hope to get. Sold listings are the market.
  • Match the exact condition. Complete in box, cartridge only and sealed are three completely different markets for the same title.
  • Check region. PAL and NTSC prices diverge sharply, and a US price tells you nothing about a UK sale.
  • Auction for anything genuinely scarce, Buy It Now for the steady middle. Auctions on common titles finish at the low end.
  • Price at the median of recent solds, not the peak. The one that went for £90 had a bidding war. The other eleven went for £38.
  • Allow for the buyer fee. Buyers see the protection fee at checkout, so a price just under the crowd wins the sale.

8. Do you pay tax selling retro games in the UK?

Yes, once you go past the allowance. Buying goods with the intention of reselling them is trading.

That is the key difference from a house clearout, where you sell your own old things at a loss.

  • The trading allowance is £1,000 per tax year for 2026 to 27, across all self employed income. GOV.UK confirms it rises to £3,000 from 6 April 2027.
  • The allowance is measured on your gross takings, before costs. It is not your profit.
  • Above it, register for Self Assessment by 5 October after the end of that tax year.
  • You can deduct genuine costs: stock, postage, packaging, fuel to buy stock, eBay fees, a proportion of storage.
  • Keep a simple record from day one. What you paid, what it sold for, what postage cost. A spreadsheet is enough.
  • Platforms report seller data to HMRC unless you make fewer than 30 sales and no more than about £1,700 a year. A report should match a return you have already filed.

Myths that cost people money

"I might find something worth thousands." You almost certainly will not.

Planning on a lottery ticket is why people overpay for job lots. Price every lot on what you can definitely sell, not what might be in it.

"Graded games are the future." Grading costs money and time, and the graded market has been volatile. For the £15 to £40 middle it makes no sense at all.

"Buy now, prices only go up." Will retro games increase in value? Some do, but prices rose sharply during the lockdown years and many came back down.

Treating stock as an investment rather than inventory fills a spare room with money you cannot release.

The honest verdict

This works if you enjoy the hunt, you can get to car boots early, and you have somewhere to store stock.

At around £300 net a month for weekend work, it pays for itself and funds the collection.

This does not work if you want a reliable hourly rate. At roughly £11 to £12 an hour, almost any paid weekend work pays better.

That is the number nobody puts in the video.

Start with £50, one car boot, and a rule. Buy nothing you cannot price on sold listings while standing there.

If it is the reselling you enjoy rather than the games, selling what you already own is a better first step. There is no stock cost and no tax question.

Frequently asked

Where can I sell my retro games in the UK? eBay is the biggest market, and UK private sellers pay no final value fee on most items. Facebook Marketplace suits bulky consoles for local collection. Specialist auction houses suit rare or sealed items. Trade in shops and instant quote sites pay fastest but lowest, because they must resell at a profit.

Who buys retro games for cash? Independent retro shops, high street trade in chains and online instant quote sites all buy games and consoles. Their offers are lower than a private sale, often by a wide margin. They suit stock you cannot shift. Check eBay sold listings first so you know what the item is really worth.

Will retro games increase in value? Some will, but nobody can promise which. Prices rose sharply during the lockdown years and many fell back afterwards. Common titles that sold millions rarely climb. Complete boxed games with a small print run hold value best. Treat stock as something to sell, not a pension.

Do I pay tax on selling retro games? If you buy games to resell, that is trading. Takings over £1,000 a year in 2026 to 27 mean registering for Self Assessment. The allowance rises to £3,000 from 6 April 2027. Selling your own old collection at a loss is not trading and is usually not taxed.

How do I tell if a retro game is fake? Check the weight, the screw, the label and the board. Genuine Nintendo cartridges use a security screw, not a Phillips head. Labels on fakes are often shiny, pixelated or the wrong colour. Many reproductions cannot hold a save. If you are not sure, do not sell it as genuine.
