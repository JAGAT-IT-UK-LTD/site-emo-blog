The £30,000 Tax Free Rule Covers Less of Your Redundancy Payment Than You Think

Ruhul Amin · Published 16 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Almost everyone facing redundancy has heard that the first £30,000 is tax free. It is true, and it applies to a narrower slice of the money than the phrase suggests. Several of the biggest items in a typical settlement sit entirely outside it and are taxed like ordinary pay.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The £30,000 exemption is a combined limit covering statutory redundancy pay, enhanced or additional severance, and non-cash benefits you keep.
  • Notice pay is not in it. Payment in lieu of notice and garden leave pay are taxed and NIable as earnings.
  • Nor are unpaid wages, holiday pay or bonuses. All three are earnings in full.
  • Post-Employment Notice Pay is the trap. It taxes the part of a severance payment equal to what you would have earned in your notice period.
  • Above £30,000 you pay income tax but not employee National Insurance. Your employer pays Class 1A at 15% on the excess.
  • Employer pension contributions and your legal costs paid direct to a solicitor are outside the £30,000 entirely, and not taxed.

1. What is inside the £30,000

GOV.UK sets out the exemption as a combined limit. You do not usually pay tax on the first £30,000 in total of:

  • Statutory redundancy pay.
  • Additional severance or enhanced redundancy payments your employer gives you above the statutory amount.
  • Non-cash benefits, for example company property you keep after your employment ends.

The word doing the work is "combined". It is one £30,000 across all three, not £30,000 each. A generous enhanced redundancy scheme can use the whole exemption on its own.

Two further items sit outside the £30,000 and are not taxed at all, which preserves your exemption for other things:

  • Employer contributions to a registered pension scheme made as part of your termination payment. You pay tax only on employer contributions above the Annual Allowance.
  • Legal costs relating to the settlement that your employer pays directly to your solicitor. Paid directly is the condition.

A termination payment made because of an injury, illness or disability that prevents you doing your job is also fully exempt.

2. What is taxed as earnings regardless

This is the list that surprises people, because several of these are large and all of them are outside the exemption completely. GOV.UK is direct: some parts of a termination payment are considered earnings, meaning they are subject to tax and National Insurance. Those are:

Unpaid wages. Money you had already earned.

Holiday pay. Including accrued and untaken leave paid out on exit.

Bonuses.

Payments for agreeing to a restrictive covenant. A non-compete clause you are paid to accept is taxable pay.

Any payment instead of working your notice period. Payment in lieu of notice, or the equivalent element inside severance pay.

Read that last one carefully. It is why a settlement comfortably under £30,000 can still generate a tax bill.

3. Post-Employment Notice Pay, the part that catches people

If you do not work your full notice period, you pay tax and National Insurance on the part of your termination payment equivalent to what you would have earned if you had worked it. That applies to lump sum payments in lieu of notice, to garden leave pay, and to the slice of any severance or enhanced redundancy payment that corresponds to your notice. That slice has a name: Post-Employment Notice Pay, usually shortened to PENP.

Three things make it behave in ways people do not expect.

  • PENP does not apply to statutory redundancy pay, which is protected from this calculation.
  • It eats into the severance payment instead. The employer works out what you would have earned in your notice period, and that much of your severance becomes taxable.
  • It cannot exceed what you actually received. If the PENP figure is larger than the severance and non-cash benefits you get, you only pay tax on the amount you actually get.

Your employer does the calculation. You should be able to read the settlement and see which number is which, because "the first £30,000 is tax free" is not a sufficient description of a document you are being asked to sign.

The formula is not hidden and it is worth checking against your own settlement. It sits in section 402D of the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003 as ((BP × D) ÷ P) − T, and HMRC defines each term in its Employment Income Manual at EIM13880. Four inputs, every one of which you can read off a payslip and a notice clause:

TermWhat it meansWhere you read it
BPBasic pay in the last pay period ending before the trigger date. Basic pay only, so overtime, commission and bonus are left out.Your last payslip before notice
DCalendar days in the post-employment notice period, meaning the notice you did not work.The notice clause in your contract
PCalendar days in your last pay period. On a monthly payroll that is 30 or 31, not 20 working days.Your payroll frequency
TTermination payments already taxable as earnings outside Chapter 3 of Part 6, principally a contractual payment in lieu of notice. It is subtracted, so the same money is not taxed twice.The PILON line in the settlement

Two rules sit on top of that arithmetic and both work in your favour. If the formula produces a negative number, PENP is nil rather than a deduction from anything. And if it produces more than the total of the relevant termination awards, it is capped at that total, so PENP can never tax you on money you did not actually receive. Both limits are in section 402D itself, not in guidance that could be withdrawn.

The honest limit of all this: the formula assumes an ordinary monthly salary and a notice period that lands in whole months. Pay in arrears, a change of pay date, a pay rise part way through notice, or anyone paid other than monthly can move BP and P enough to change the answer materially, and HMRC operates an alternative calculation for some of those cases. If your notice period is not a whole number of months and the sum at stake is four figures, that is the point to buy an hour of advice rather than trust a formula off a web page.

4. Worked example: Gareth in Newport

This follows the structure of the worked examples GOV.UK publishes on its termination payments page.

Gareth, Newport. Made redundant. Statutory redundancy pay £10,000. His employer adds a severance payment of £5,000. Total £15,000. He earns £500 a week and is not required to work his four week notice period.

Step one. PENP does not apply to the £10,000 statutory redundancy pay. That element is not taxed on this basis.

Step two. Four weeks at £500 is £2,000 he would have earned by working the notice. So £2,000 of the £5,000 severance is taxable as earnings, with National Insurance.

Step three. The remaining £13,000 is under £30,000 and is not taxed.

Now change one number. If Gareth earned £1,000 a week and the severance were £3,000, his PENP would be £4,000, which is more than the £3,000 severance he actually receives. He would pay tax on the full £3,000, and no more, because the charge cannot exceed what he was actually paid.

The lesson in both. Neither settlement is anywhere near £30,000, and both produce a tax bill. The exemption was never the binding constraint.

5. What happens above £30,000

Once the combined total passes £30,000, two charges apply to the excess, and they fall on different people.

You pay income tax on the amount over £30,000, at your marginal rate.

Your employer pays Class 1A National Insurance on the amount over £30,000. For 2026 to 2027 the Class 1A rate on termination awards is 15%.

The point worth holding onto is that the excess above £30,000 does not attract employee National Insurance. That makes it cheaper for you than ordinary salary of the same size, and more expensive for your employer than you might assume, which is useful context when a package is being structured.

One practical warning. A large payment landing in a single month can push you into a higher band for that pay period and produce an over deduction under PAYE. That usually resolves through the tax year or a repayment claim, but it means the amount reaching your account can be well below the figure on the agreement.

The honest verdict

The £30,000 figure is real and it is repeated in a way that misleads. It covers statutory redundancy pay, enhanced severance and non-cash benefits, combined. It does not cover notice pay, holiday pay, unpaid wages, bonuses or money paid for accepting a restrictive covenant, and the Post-Employment Notice Pay rule reaches into severance to tax the notice element even when the total is nowhere near £30,000.

If you are handed a settlement agreement, the useful question is not "is it under £30,000". It is "which of these lines is earnings, and what is the PENP figure". The employer has to do that calculation, so it exists somewhere, and asking to see it is reasonable. For anything substantial, an hour of employment law advice, often funded by the employer as part of the settlement, is the best money in the process.

Frequently asked

Is statutory redundancy pay taxed? Statutory redundancy pay under £30,000 is not taxable, and Post-Employment Notice Pay does not apply to it. It is the most protected element of a termination payment.

Do I pay National Insurance on the amount over £30,000? You do not pay employee National Insurance on the excess. Your employer pays Class 1A National Insurance on the amount over £30,000, at 15% for 2026 to 2027. You pay income tax on the excess at your marginal rate.

My employer is paying my legal fees. Is that taxable? Legal costs relating to the settlement that your employer pays directly to your solicitor are not taxed and do not use up your £30,000. Paid directly to the solicitor is the condition, so how the payment is routed matters.
