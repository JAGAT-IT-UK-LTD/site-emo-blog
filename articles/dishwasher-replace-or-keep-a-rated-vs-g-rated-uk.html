1. £35 against £70: what the Energy Saving Trust figures actually measure

The Energy Saving Trust's appliances blog, updated 3 August 2026 and read again on 5 September 2026, gives one dishwasher comparison: an A-rated 12-place dishwasher costs £35 a year to run in Great Britain, and the G-rated equivalent costs £70, which the Trust describes as almost double. Washing machines, dishwashers and tumble dryers together are about 14 per cent of a typical bill, the largest share of any appliance group, which is why the comparison is worth making.

Those figures come from the energy label, which since 2021 has rated dishwashers A to G on the energy used per 100 eco cycles, and the Trust prices that at a typical usage of a few cycles a week and the current unit rate. So the £35 and £70 are the eco programme, a full standard load, and about 280 cycles a year. Change any of those and the numbers move, and section 3 explains why that matters more than the label. The same label logic plays out differently on cookers, where we compared the running cost gap between an induction hob and gas.

The gap between the two, £35 a year, is the whole saving available from replacing a working G-rated machine with an A-rated one. It is a real saving. It is also the third smallest annual figure on this site after radiator foil and pipe lagging, and the machine that produces it costs several hundred pounds. That is the replace-or-keep sum, and it is the same shape as the one in our fridge freezer article, where the answer was also to measure first and keep.

3. Why the programme button matters more than the label letter

The label figure is the eco programme. Eco runs long, three to four hours on most machines, because it heats the water less and relies on time and detergent instead. The intensive or auto programme heats to 65 or 70 degrees and typically uses half as much again to double the electricity per cycle. So a G-rated machine on eco every night can cost less per year than an A-rated machine on intensive every night, and a household that switched to eco on its existing G-rated machine would capture most of the £35 without buying anything.

The other two habits are just as large. Full loads: the cycle costs the same for six plates as for twelve, so a half-loaded machine doubles the cost per plate. No hot pre-rinse: rinsing under a running hot tap before loading can use more hot water than the machine does, particularly on an electric shower or immersion system; scrape, do not rinse. The Energy Saving Trust's advice is exactly this, eco setting or short cycle, wait until full, and it is where the money is for anyone who already owns a dishwasher.

Put those three together and a G-rated owner can get the running cost from £70 towards £45 without a purchase. The A-rated replacement would take them to £35 with the same habits. The habits are worth £25 a year for nothing; the machine is worth £10 a year on top for £450.

4. When it does fail: how to read the label and choose

Dishwashers fail on pumps, heaters, door seals and control boards, and a repair on a machine over eight years old is often not worth it against a new one with a warranty. When that day comes, the decision is different from the one above, because the £300 to £700 is being spent regardless and the only question is which machine.

Read the kWh, not the letter. The label carries energy per 100 eco cycles in kWh; at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p, each 10 kWh per 100 cycles is worth about £7 a year at 280 cycles. Two machines both marked D can differ by 15 kWh per 100 cycles. Compare the number.

Size to your household. A 12 or 13 place machine full is cheaper per plate than a 9 place slimline run twice. A 14 or 15 place machine you never fill is not.

Check the eco cycle time and the auto-open dry. Eco at four hours suits a household that loads after dinner and empties in the morning; a machine whose eco is under three hours suits one that runs it twice a day. Auto-open doors dry without a heating element and are worth having.

Compare the retailers on the things that do not change hourly. The manufacturer's warranty is typically one or two years, some brands offer five on registration, and some retailers add a second year of their own; installation and removal of the old machine are sometimes included and sometimes £20 to £40; the returns window on a plumbed-in appliance is short everywhere. A dishwasher that fails in month 14 outside its warranty costs more than the A-to-G gap saves in a decade, so the warranty is the second number to read after the kWh.

5. Worked example: Callum in Aberdeen

Two adults, one child, a nine-year-old 12-place dishwasher on the old A+ label that runs every evening on the auto programme, no water meter

Callum's machine works. He has seen a £449 A-rated 13-place model and wants to know whether the label pays for it.

His running cost now. The old A+ label is roughly a modern E to F. On auto every night rather than eco, we put his cost at about £70 a year, the Energy Saving Trust's G-rated figure, which is fair for an old machine on a hot programme.

The replacement sum. £449 for a saving of £35 a year: 12.8 years. The machine is nine years old already and the new one carries a two year warranty. No.

What he does instead. Switches to eco, which on his machine is a 3 hour 40 minute programme, and stops rinsing under the hot tap. That is worth perhaps £20 to £25 a year on the Energy Saving Trust's figures, for nothing. He puts the £449 in the savings account the fridge freezer article told him to open, against the day the pump goes.

When it goes. He buys A or B rated, reads the kWh per 100 cycles rather than the letter, picks the retailer that includes the second year's warranty and takes the old one away, and the £35 a year gap pays for the premium over a cheap G-rated machine in about four years.

6. The honest verdict

Do not replace a working dishwasher to save electricity. On the Energy Saving Trust's figures the gap between the worst and best label class is £35 a year, a £450 machine takes 13 years to recover that, and dishwashers do not reliably last 13 years. Switch to the eco programme, run it full, stop pre-rinsing under the hot tap, and you will capture most of the gap without spending anything.

When the machine fails, buy A or B rated and compare the kWh per 100 cycles on the label, not the letter; the premium over a G-rated machine pays back in three to four years once you are buying anyway. Choose the retailer on warranty length and installation, not on a price that will have changed by the time you read this.

Frequently asked

How much does a dishwasher cost to run per year in the UK? The Energy Saving Trust, page updated 3 August 2026, puts an A-rated 12-place dishwasher at £35 a year in Great Britain and £45 in Northern Ireland, and a G-rated equivalent at £70 and £90. Those are eco-programme figures; the intensive programme on any machine costs considerably more per cycle.

Is it worth replacing a G-rated dishwasher with an A-rated one? Not while it works. The saving is £35 a year on Energy Saving Trust figures, so a £450 A-rated machine takes about 13 years to pay back and a £300 one about 8.6, both longer than most dishwashers last. When the old machine fails, the same £35 a year gap pays for the premium over a G-rated replacement in three to four years, so buy A or B rated then.

Does the eco programme really save money? Yes, and usually more than the label class does. Eco heats the water less and runs longer, and the label's kWh per 100 cycles is measured on it. Running a G-rated machine on eco, full, without hot pre-rinsing can bring its cost close to an A-rated machine run on intensive. The programme you choose and how full the machine is move the bill more than the letter on the door.

What should I compare when buying a new dishwasher? The kWh per 100 eco cycles on the label, which at the October 2026 cap is worth about £7 a year per 10 kWh at 280 cycles; the place settings against your household; the eco cycle time; the manufacturer's warranty and any retailer extension; and whether installation and removal are included. Compare those rather than a price that changes by the hour.
