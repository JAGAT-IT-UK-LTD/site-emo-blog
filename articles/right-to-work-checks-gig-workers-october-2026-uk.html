Right to work checks since October 2026: the app now checks you, not just your bank details

Since 1 October 2026, right to work checks cover gig apps, subcontractors and agency style work, not just employees. The change arrived on time, with no delay. Most write ups speak to employers, so here it is from the side of the person doing the work, because the paperwork lands on you first.

The short version

  • It happened on 1 October 2026. SI 2026/683 brought section 48 of the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 into force on that date, as planned. There was no delay.
  • Three new groups are now covered: worker's contracts, individual subcontractors, and people matched with customers by an online matching service that charges a fee or commission.
  • The penalty falls on the business: a starting point of £45,000 per worker for a first breach and £60,000 for a repeat within three years, under the code of practice that came into force the same day.
  • Penalties only bite on new engagements: that code says a civil penalty in the three new categories may only be imposed where the engagement commenced on or after 1 October 2026.
  • It does not change your immigration status and does not make you an employee of the platform. It changes who has to check, not what you may do.
  • What you will be asked for: a passport, current or expired, if you are a British or Irish citizen, or a share code if you are not.

1. What changed for right to work checks in October 2026?

Right to work checks for gig and agency work became law on 1 October 2026. The date held. Nothing was postponed.

The instrument is the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 (Commencement No. 4) Regulations 2026, SI 2026/683 (C. 57). It was made on 24 June 2026 and signed by Alex Norris, Minister of State at the Home Office.

It brought section 48 of that Act into force. Section 48 amends the Immigration, Asylum and Nationality Act 2006 by inserting a new section 14A.

Sections 15 to 24 of the 2006 Act are the illegal working rules. They produce referral notices and civil penalties. Since 1 October, a reference in them to employing someone also covers:

  • engaging an individual under a worker's contract, meaning a contract other than a contract of service or apprenticeship under which the individual does the work personally, and where the engager is not simply a client or customer of a business the individual runs;
  • engaging an individual subcontractor, where the business has contracted with a third party to provide the work and the individual has not;
  • an online matching service passing a service provider's details to potential clients or customers.

So the checking duty that applied only to employees now applies far more widely. If you work through an agency, a subcontract or an app, the people paying you now have to see proof of your right to work.

2. Is my delivery or gig app now caught?

The definition of an online matching service decides whether an app is caught. It has three limbs, and all three must be met. In the course of a business, the service must:

  1. keep a register of service providers for the purpose of matching them with potential clients or customers;
  2. provide an online service through which potential clients or customers can submit enquiries to be matched with a suitable provider; and
  3. charge a fee or commission in return for making such matches.

A "service provider" is a person providing, or seeking to provide, work or services for remuneration.

The fee or commission limb does real work. A free noticeboard that takes nothing from either side does not obviously meet limb three. A platform that takes a cut of every job does.

The code of practice adds one line that matters for app workers. It says the exemption for people running their own business does not include people who get work through an intermediary or platform without operating an independent business in their own right. If your work comes through an app that keeps a percentage, assume you are inside the rules.

Put that against a real business. Rashid runs a two van courier round in Luton. He uses three regular subcontract drivers, each invoicing him as self employed.

Until October he checked nothing, because none of the three is his employee and the law did not ask him to. Now he is engaging individual subcontractors, which is exactly what section 48 brought into scope.

Say he takes on three new drivers in November and skips the check, and all three turn out not to have the right to work. At the repeat breach starting point his exposure is 3 × £60,000, which is £180,000. Even at the first breach starting point it is 3 × £45,000, which is £135,000. His business does not turn over that in a year.

The check that removes the exposure costs him nothing and takes a few minutes per person on the Home Office online service. There is no version of this arithmetic in which skipping it is the cheaper option.

The honest caveat is that these figures are starting points and a maximum, not a flat fee. The Home Office sets the actual amount case by case. But the statutory excuse only exists where the check was done in the prescribed way, so the useful move is to hold the evidence beforehand, not to argue about the number afterwards.

3. Who can be fined, and from when?

The money side of this change is not in section 48 at all. It sits in a code of practice, and that code stopped being a draft on the day section 48 commenced.

The Illegal working penalties: codes of practice for employers page was last updated on 1 October 2026, and it had not changed when we checked again on 9 November 2026.

The code in force is dated 1 October 2026. It runs to 37 pages, calls itself the seventh version of the code, and is issued under section 19 of the Immigration, Asylum and Nationality Act 2006.

The change note GOV.UK published that day is one line: "Removed 'draft' from the title in line with legislation change." The 13 February 2024 edition, which said nothing about any of this, has been replaced.

Two things in that code change the picture for anyone already doing this work. Neither is in the commencement regulations.

First, the penalty is two numbers, not one. The code sets the starting point for a first breach at £45,000 per worker. It is £60,000 per worker where a breach was found within the previous three years. The £60,000 that every headline quotes is the repeat breach figure, not the opening one.

Second, and this is the part almost nobody reported. For the three new categories, the code says a civil penalty may only be imposed where the employment commenced on or after 1 October 2026.

It says that applies both to liability under section 15 of the 2006 Act and to the extended liability that section 48 inserted as a new section 15A. Section 15A can reach a business further up a contracting chain.

On the face of the code, an engagement that was already running before 1 October does not expose the business to a civil penalty under the new categories.

A gig engagement that started before 1 October 2026 cannot attract a civil penalty under the new categories, on the face of the Home Office's own code.

Read that carefully, because it cuts one way only. It is a limit on fines, not permission to work without status. It does not stop a platform or an agency asking you anyway.

Most will ask everybody, because working out who is in scope costs more than checking. But if you are told you must be checked today or lose work you have been doing for a year, the document that decides the money says something narrower than that.

That is the code in force now, not a proposal. What does not change is the thing that makes this painless from your side: one share code, generated online, sent to everyone who asks.

4. What does the change not do?

This matters as much as what it does, because the scare version of the story is still circulating.

  • It does not change your immigration status, or the hours and work types your permission allows.
  • It does not make you an employee of the platform or the agency. Employment status is decided elsewhere, on the usual tests.
  • It does not put the fine on you. GOV.UK is clear the civil penalty of up to £60,000 for each illegal worker goes to the business that failed to check. The separate criminal offence, carrying up to five years in prison and an unlimited fine, applies to someone who knew or had reasonable cause to believe a person did not have the right to work.
  • It does not touch genuine business to business work. The worker's contract limb excludes the case where the engager is a client or customer of a business you carry on.
  • It does not change your pay rights. If you are an agency worker, your right to equal treatment after 12 weeks is a separate rule, set out in our guide to the agency worker 12 week rule.

Marek, Slough. He does two or three warehouse shifts a week through an agency, plus weekend delivery work through an app that takes a commission on each drop.

Until October only the agency had to check him. The app is now caught too, because it keeps a register of drivers, matches them through its own software, and charges commission.

So he is asked twice for the same thing. He generates one share code and sends it to both. It costs him ten minutes.

The failure mode is not the check. It is being asked on a Friday night for a document he has not looked at since 2023. If his agency pays him through an umbrella company, it is also worth reading umbrella company or agency PAYE, and what the difference costs you.

The verdict

This was an administrative change with a hard date and a wide reach, reported almost entirely as a compliance headache for businesses. It arrived on 1 October 2026 exactly as planned.

For the person doing the work it is one sentence. More of the places you get work from are now legally obliged to check your right to work, so have the document or the share code to hand.

Students get caught here more than anyone, because their permission carries an hours limit as well as an expiry date. The student visa maintenance rules changing on 30 November 2026 sit alongside it.

If your status is settled, this is a ten minute job. If it is not, get free advice from a named source now, rather than at the point of being asked.

Frequently asked

Did the right to work check changes start on 1 October 2026? Yes. SI 2026/683 brought section 48 of the Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025 into force on 1 October 2026, with no delay. The Home Office code of practice and the employer's guide both took effect the same day. Worker's contracts, individual subcontractors and online matching services are now covered.

What exactly will I be asked for? British and Irish citizens can use a British passport, or an Irish passport or passport card, and GOV.UK confirms these can be current or expired. Everyone else proves their right to work with a share code, generated online through the GOV.UK service. You send the code and your date of birth, and the business checks it online.

Will a company I already work for ask me again? Probably. The commencement regulations contain no transitional carve out, but the code of practice says a civil penalty in the three new categories may only be imposed where the engagement commenced on or after 1 October 2026. That limits the fine, not the asking. Most platforms check everyone, and being asked is not an accusation.

Which document should the platform or agency be working from? The Employer's guide to right to work checks, dated 1 October 2026, which replaced the 16 July 2026 version on the day section 48 commenced. It is on GOV.UK and names worker's contracts, individual subcontracting and online matching services directly. If an engager says they are still waiting for guidance, that is the guidance.

I am self employed and invoice my own clients. Am I caught? Usually not. The worker's contract limb does not apply where the engager is a client or customer of a business you genuinely carry on. But the code says people who get work through a platform without running an independent business are not in that exemption. Ordinary client work is outside, app work usually is not.
