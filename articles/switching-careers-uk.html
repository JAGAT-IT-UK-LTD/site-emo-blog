1. Who changes career in the UK, and at what age?

The most common age to change career is 31. That comes from a Michael Page survey of 5,000 UK workers, published in August 2023.

The same survey found 26% were considering a career change. So wanting out is common. Actually moving is rarer.

What holds people back is less about money than you might think. In that survey 23% blamed a lack of confidence and 20% doubted their skills.

You will see bolder numbers quoted online, often with no source behind them. We left those out. The honest position is that nobody publishes a reliable count of UK career changers by age.

What we can say is simpler. There is no legal age limit on retraining, on student finance for a first degree, or on an apprenticeship in England. Being 42 is not the obstacle. Not having done the arithmetic is.

2. How do you work out if a career change pays?

Before any course, any application, any conversation, work out this one number.

Payback period = (cost of retraining + earnings lost while retraining) divided by the annual pay uplift.

Here is that sum for five common routes. The fee for a full time year is the 2026 to 2027 tuition fee cap in England. The other rows are examples to show the method.

ScenarioCostLost earningsAnnual upliftPayback
Evening course, keep working£1,200£0£4,000Under 4 months
Funded Skills Bootcamp, keep working£0£0£3,000Immediate
Full time year of study£9,790£26,000£8,000About 4.5 years
Trade retraining, part time£6,000£2,000£5,000About 1.6 years
The often quoted bad case£15,000Not counted£5,0003 years, before lost earnings

Two rules fall out of that table.

Anything you can do while still earning has a far shorter payback. The lost earnings column is usually larger than the course fee.

And a payback longer than about five years needs a reason beyond money. That is a perfectly good reason. It should be a decision, not a surprise.

One route people rarely cost properly is what it takes and costs to qualify as a driving instructor. If you are leaving because your job is at risk, work out how much redundancy pay you are owed first, because it can fund the gap.

The course fee is rarely the expensive part. The year you did not get paid is the expensive part, and most career change advice leaves it out of the sum entirely.

3. Do you really lack the skills?

One in five people in the Michael Page survey doubted their skills. Nearly one in four lacked the confidence to move.

Those are feelings, not audits. Most people compare themselves with a job advert. The fairer comparison is with the person doing the job now, who did not tick every box either.

These skills transfer, and the people who hold them tend to undervalue them:

  • Managing people. It transfers to every sector and it is scarce everywhere.
  • Dealing with angry customers. Retail and hospitality staff have this. Many office workers do not.
  • Working to a hard deadline with consequences. Rarer than employers expect.
  • Regulated environments. Care, finance and aviation experience carries into other regulated sectors.
  • Spreadsheets and systems. Capable spreadsheet users are still uncommon.

Try this exercise. Find five job adverts for the role you want and list every requirement.

Mark each one as have it, could learn in three months, or need real training. Most people find the third list has two or three items, not fifteen.

Then put the first list at the top of your CV. Our guide to writing a CV that gets interviews shows how to word skills from one sector for another.

4. What funding is there to retrain in the UK?

Paying full price for retraining is often unnecessary. Check these routes before you hand over a card.

  • Skills Bootcamps. Government funded courses of up to 16 weeks in digital, technical and other skills, for people aged 19 or over in England. The official page says you are guaranteed a job interview with an employer. Ask the provider to confirm your place is fully funded before you sign.
  • Advanced Learner Loan. For college courses at Level 3 to 6 if you are 19 or older. You repay only when your income is over the threshold. Some Level 3 learners get an Adult Skills Fund grant instead of a loan.
  • Lifelong Learning Entitlement. New for courses starting on or after 1 January 2027. It replaces the Advanced Learner Loan at Levels 4 to 6 and gives tuition fee loans up to a total of £39,160. It funds single modules as well as whole courses, which suits a career changer. Applications opened on 30 October 2026.
  • Apprenticeships at any age. You need to be 16 or over and living in England. There is no upper limit, and a previous degree does not rule you out. At least 20% of your working hours go on training.
  • Your current employer. Many will fund training that is even loosely relevant, and most people never ask. The worst outcome is no.
  • Free taster learning. OpenLearn from the Open University lets you test whether you like the subject before you spend anything.

Know the apprentice pay rule before you apply. If you are 19 or over, you get the apprentice rate of £8.00 an hour for the first year only.

After that you must get the minimum wage for your age. For anyone 21 or over that is the National Living Wage of £12.71. From April 2027 the rates rise to £8.31 and £13.21.

Many employers pay adult apprentices more than the minimum. Our guide to apprenticeships and what they really pay has the detail.

Check the funded routes before the paid ones. A bootcamp that costs you nothing and a course that costs £6,000 often lead to the same first job.

5. How do you change career without a pay gap?

The mental picture of a career change is a leap. You quit, retrain and emerge transformed.

That version has the worst payback and the highest risk. The version that works is usually a bridge.

  1. Move sideways inside your current employer. Cheapest and fastest. You keep your salary, your pension and your service. You are a known quantity, not a stranger with no track record.
  2. Move to the target sector in your current role. An accountant moving into a tech company is one change, not two. Then move roles a year later from inside.
  3. Do the new work at the edges first. Evenings, weekends, one paying client. This tests whether you enjoy it. People do retrain into work they then find they dislike.
  4. Take the pay cut with your eyes open, and time limit it. "I will accept £4,000 less for eighteen months" is a plan. "It will work out" is not.

When the interview comes, expect the question about why you are moving. Our UK job interview tips cover how to answer it without running down your old career.

6. A worked career change, with the numbers

Michelle, 38, retail supervisor in Stoke on £25,400. She wants to move into IT support. The salaries here are assumptions, chosen to show the sum.

The plan she nearly took. A £7,500 full time course lasting twelve months. Cost £7,500 plus £25,400 of lost earnings, so £32,900.

Say her first IT support job pays £27,000. That is an uplift of £1,600 a year. Payback: over twenty years. The maths kills the plan at once, which is what the maths is for.

What she did instead. A fully funded Skills Bootcamp in IT support, part time over 14 weeks, while still working. Cost £0, lost earnings £0. It ended with a guaranteed interview.

The bridge. Before finishing, she applied inside her own company for a role supporting the till and stock systems. She got it at £26,500. A small uplift, but now with IT support on her CV and an employer that already trusted her.

Eighteen months later. She moved to a dedicated IT support role at £31,500. Total spend on the change: £0. Total lost earnings: £0. Uplift: £6,100 a year.

The comparison that matters. The leap cost £32,900 for a £1,600 uplift. The bridge cost nothing for a £6,100 uplift and took eighteen months, not twelve. The extra six months saved her the whole £32,900.

7. Which career change advice should you ignore?

  • Courses sold with salary figures from the top of the range. Ask what the typical first job pays, not what is possible. If they will not say, that is the answer.
  • "Follow your passion" as a plan. Passion tells you the direction. It does not tell you whether the payback works, and plenty of enjoyable sectors pay badly at entry level.
  • Ignoring the pension. Leaving a job mid year can interrupt contributions and employer matching. Check before you resign, not after.
  • Quitting before testing. Do the work at the edges first. The most expensive career change is the one you reverse in year two.
  • Treating age as the barrier. No funding route above closes at 40 or 50. The barrier is almost always the payback sum, not the birth certificate.
  • Not asking your employer. Free training and a sideways move is the cheapest version of every plan in this article.

If you decide to stay after all, the same sums help you argue for more money where you are. See our guide to asking for a pay rise with the numbers to back it.

The honest verdict

Do the payback sum before anything else, and put lost earnings in it. That one calculation removes most bad career change plans in about ten minutes. It also tells you which version of your idea is affordable.

Then look for the funded route. Skills Bootcamps are government funded, apprenticeships pay you while you train at any age, and the loans are repaid only when you earn enough. Full price, self funded retraining should be your last option, not your first.

And bridge rather than jump wherever you can. A sideways move inside your employer, or the same role in the target sector, keeps your income while you change. It feels less decisive and it works far more often.

Frequently asked

Is 40 too old to change career in the UK? No. There is no upper age limit on apprenticeships in England, and the Advanced Learner Loan is open to anyone aged 19 or older. What changes with age is the payback period. You have fewer working years to win back the cost, so keep earning while you retrain where you can.

How do I change career with no experience? Start from what transfers. List the requirements in five real adverts and mark what you already have. Then get evidence of the rest cheaply, through a Skills Bootcamp, a short evening course or a sideways move at your current employer. A first job in the new field matters more than a perfect qualification.

Can I get funding to retrain in the UK? Often, yes. In England, Skills Bootcamps are government funded for people aged 19 or over. The Advanced Learner Loan covers Level 3 to 6 college courses. From January 2027 the Lifelong Learning Entitlement offers loans of up to £39,160 in total for Level 4 to 6 courses and modules.

Can I do an apprenticeship as an adult? Yes. You need to be 16 or over and living in England, with no upper limit. Aged 19 or over, you get at least £8.00 an hour in the first year. After that you must be paid the minimum wage for your age, which is £12.71 from age 21.

What if I take a pay cut and regret it? Set the limit in advance. Decide how much less you will accept, for how long, and what must be true by then. A time limited cut is a decision you can review. An open ended one quietly becomes your new normal, so write the review date in your diary.
