This page first went up in September, when Bank Rate was 3.75% and the vote of 17 September was still to come. That date has passed, and so has the next one. Below is what actually happened, and what it means for your savings now that Bank Rate is 4%.

1. What is the Bank of England interest rate now?

Bank Rate is 4%. The Monetary Policy Committee raised it by 0.25 percentage points at the meeting that ended on 4 November 2026. The decision was published on Thursday 5 November.

The vote was 8 to 1. Eight members voted for 4%. Catherine Mann voted against, but only because she wanted a bigger rise, to 4.25%. Nobody on the committee voted to hold or to cut.

The Bank pointed to inflation. CPI was 3.5% in September, and the Bank expects it to rise to around 5% by the middle of 2027 before it falls back. The target is 2%.

The next Bank of England interest rate decision is due on Thursday 17 December 2026. The Committee says policy is not on a pre set path and it will look at the evidence at each meeting. Some members, including Huw Pill and Clare Lombardelli, said further rises may be needed in the near term. Alan Taylor said this was not the start of a hiking cycle.

What Bank Rate does not do is set the rate on your savings account. It sets the rate the Bank of England pays on reserves that commercial banks hold with it. Everything after that is a commercial choice by your bank. The same logic is why banks pay cash to move your current account while paying next to nothing on the balance already sitting in it.

2. How did Bank Rate get from 3.75% to 4%?

When this guide was first written, Bank Rate had been held at 3.75% on 30 July 2026, on a 6 to 3 vote. Three members wanted a rise even then.

On 17 September 2026 the Committee held again, by the same 6 to 3 margin. Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill voted for 4%. CPI had reached 3.1% in August. The Bank was given early access to the September labour market figures from the ONS so it could use them at that meeting.

By November the balance had tipped. Inflation had risen to 3.5%, and eight of nine members voted to raise. So the warning in the summer was real. A committee that splits 6 to 3 towards a rise is not drifting towards cuts, and in the end it did not.

3. Will my savings rate go up straight away?

Probably not, and not by the full amount. The gap between Bank Rate and an ordinary easy access account is not a scandal. It is the business model.

A bank pays what it needs to pay to keep the deposits it wants. If it already has plenty, it pays less. A rise in Bank Rate gives it room to pay more, but no duty to.

The pattern is consistent. New accounts and accounts with a bonus rate get priced close to Bank Rate. Accounts you opened years ago get repriced quietly, often later and by less. The notice arrives by email or in an app message you did not open.

Fixed rate bonds tend to move first, because banks compete hard for a set pot of money for a set term. That is where a rise shows up soonest.

So the honest answer to "should I wait to see if my bank passes on the rise" is no. Check what you are paid today. If it is poor now, waiting will not make your bank generous.

4. At what balance does tax start eating the gain?

This is the part most savings coverage leaves out. It decides whether chasing another 0.25 points is worth an afternoon.

GOV.UK sets the Personal Savings Allowance at £1,000 of interest a year for a basic rate taxpayer and £500 for a higher rate taxpayer. An additional rate taxpayer gets nothing. Above the allowance, interest is taxed at your usual rate.

Turn that into a balance. At a rate of 4%:

  • A basic rate taxpayer uses up the full £1,000 allowance at £25,000 of savings.
  • A higher rate taxpayer uses up the £500 allowance at £12,500.

At 3.75% those balances were about £26,700 and £13,300. So the rise to 4% pulls more savers over the line without them adding a penny.

The tax itself is going up too. HMRC published a policy paper on 29 October 2026 setting savings income tax rates of 24%, 44% and 49% from the 2027 to 2028 tax year, which starts in April 2027. Today they are 20%, 40% and 45%. We set out the timing in savings tax rising to 24% in April 2027.

There is one more allowance for lower earners. The starting rate for savings can make up to £5,000 of interest tax free, but only if your other taxable income is below £17,570. There is more on how HMRC collects the tax in our guide to tax on savings interest in the UK.

Interest inside a cash ISA sits outside all of this. The ISA allowance for 2026 to 2027 is £20,000. Read the cash ISA changes coming in 2027 before this tax year runs out on 5 April.

5. Worked example: Farhana in Luton

Farhana is a higher rate taxpayer. She has £30,000 in an easy access account she opened in 2023 and has not looked at since. For this example, say it pays 2.1%.

Where she is now. At 2.1% she earns £630 a year. Her allowance covers £500. The other £130 is taxed at 40%, which costs £52. She keeps £578.

Option one, move it all to NS&I Direct Saver. NS&I lists Direct Saver at 3.75% gross, variable. On £30,000 that is £1,125. The allowance covers £500. Tax at 40% on £625 is £250. She keeps £875, which is £297 more than now.

If the allowance stays as it is, from April 2027 the same £625 would be taxed at 44%, which is £275. She would keep £850. The rise in savings tax eats part of the gain before she has done anything.

Option two, split it. If she has not used this year's ISA allowance, she puts £20,000 in the NS&I Direct ISA at 3.80% tax free. That earns £760 with no tax. The other £10,000 goes in Direct Saver at 3.75% and earns £375. That is under her £500 allowance, so no tax at all.

She keeps £1,135 a year. That is £557 more than her old account and £260 more than putting everything in Direct Saver. The split does more for her than the Bank of England did.

6. What should savers do before 17 December?

Five things, in order of how much money they are worth.

  1. Find out what your account actually pays. Not what it paid when you opened it. Log in and read the rate. Five minutes, and the best paid five minutes on this list.
  2. Check yourself against £25,000 or £12,500. Those are the balances where your Personal Savings Allowance runs out at 4%.
  3. If you are over, use your ISA allowance first. It shelters interest from the higher tax rates arriving in April 2027.
  4. Keep within the FSCS limit. FSCS protects up to £120,000 per person, per bank, building society or credit union. Banks that share a licence share one limit.
  5. Only think about timing if you are about to fix. A fixed bond locks in a rate for the whole term, so the outlook matters there. NS&I lists one year Guaranteed Growth Bonds at 4.99% fixed. A regular saver is a different sum again, as we showed in what an 8% regular saver actually pays.

What about Premium Bonds? NS&I lists the prize fund rate at 4.35%, with odds of 21,000 to 1 per £1 bond number. Prizes are tax free, but the rate is an average, not a promise. Your own return can be well below that, or above it, depending on luck. We compare them in Premium Bonds versus a savings account.

One thing not to do: do not lock everything into a long fix because you expect rates to keep climbing. The Bank says the next move is not decided. If you are wrong, your money is stuck.

The honest verdict

Bank Rate at 4% is good news for savers on paper. In practice it is still background noise for most people. Two numbers decide what you keep: the rate your own bank chooses to pay, and the point where the Personal Savings Allowance runs out. At 4% that is £25,000 for a basic rate taxpayer and £12,500 for a higher rate taxpayer.

If your money sits in an old account you have not checked, fix that this week. If you are over the allowance, use your ISA before April 2027 makes the tax bigger. If your money is already in a good account and under the allowance, the truthful answer is that the 17 December decision does not need you to do anything.

Frequently asked

What is the Bank of England base rate today? Bank Rate is 4%. The Monetary Policy Committee raised it from 3.75% on 5 November 2026, by 8 votes to 1. It was the first change after holds in July and September. The next announcement is due on Thursday 17 December 2026, and the Bank publishes the decision on its own website at the time.

Will savings rates go up after the rise to 4%? Some will, but not automatically. Banks choose whether to pass on a rise, and they usually move new and fixed rate accounts first. Older easy access accounts tend to move later and by less, or not at all. Check your own rate rather than assuming it has followed Bank Rate.

When is the next Bank of England interest rate decision? The next decision is on Thursday 17 December 2026, according to the Bank's list of MPC dates. The Committee says policy is not on a pre set path. Some members signalled further rises may be needed, while one said the next move could be up, down or sideways.

How much can I save before paying tax on interest? It depends on your tax band. At a 4% rate, a basic rate taxpayer reaches the £1,000 Personal Savings Allowance at £25,000. A higher rate taxpayer reaches the £500 allowance at £12,500. Additional rate taxpayers have no allowance. Interest inside a cash ISA does not count towards any of this.

Should I fix my savings now or wait? Waiting only matters if you are about to fix. Fixed bonds lock a rate for the whole term, and markets usually price in an expected move before it happens, so the gain from waiting a few weeks is often small. For easy access money, a better rate today beats a possible rise later.
