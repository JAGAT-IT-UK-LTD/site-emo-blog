The Bus Fare Cap Drops From £3 to £2 on 1 January 2027: £520 a Year for a Five-Day Commuter, and Who Gets Nothing

Ruhul Amin · Published 5 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The cap on a single bus fare in England is £3 today and stays there until 31 December 2026. From 1 January 2027 it falls to £2, which is the level it was at until the end of 2024. GOV.UK's guidance page, updated 1 September 2026, carries both dates. Here is what the change is worth at your journey count, the routes and cities the cap has never covered, and the two cases where a £2 cap still is not the cheapest way to travel.

The short version

  • £3 until 31 December 2026, £2 from 1 January 2027. Source: GOV.UK, "£3 national bus fare cap" guidance (updated 1 September 2026): "single bus tickets in England will be capped at £2 from 1 January 2027" and "the £3 cap will continue until the end of 2026".
  • The £2 cap runs to the end of 2027, according to the 22 July 2026 press release announcing it, backed by £400 million of extra funding. Before the announcement the £3 cap had been due to end on 31 March 2027.
  • What it saves: £1 a single. Ten singles a week for 52 weeks is £520 a year. Two singles a day on a five-day school run in term time (39 weeks) is £390.
  • What it does NOT cover: London, where Transport for London sets its own fares; Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland; return, day and weekly tickets; and any route an operator has left out of the scheme. GOV.UK lists included and excluded routes by operator.
  • It is a maximum, not a price. A single that already costs £1.80 stays £1.80. The saving is biggest on long rural and inter-town routes where a single would otherwise be £5 to £10.
  • Our verdict: from January, buy singles unless a weekly or monthly ticket costs less than £2 per journey you actually make. For most five-day commuters on one route it will not, and the cap wins.

1. The two dates, and where they come from

The national single fare cap in England started at £2 in January 2023, rose to £3 on 1 January 2025, and was due to run at £3 until 31 March 2027. On 22 July 2026 the Prime Minister announced that the cap would fall back to £2 from 1 January 2027 and run at that level for the rest of 2027, backed by £400 million of extra funding. GOV.UK's scheme guidance page, updated 1 September 2026, now carries the two dates in plain words: £3 until the end of 2026, £2 from 1 January 2027.

The Department for Transport's own estimate from the first £2 period is worth knowing because it is the only hard number about behaviour: the press release says the last £2 cap saw an estimated 30 million more bus journeys in ten months. That is why the cap keeps coming back. It is not a discount on a fare, it is a maximum on a single, and the operators that take part are reimbursed for the difference.

2. What it saves at your journey count

The saving is exactly £1 per single on any route where the uncapped fare is £3 or more, and nothing on a route where the single is already under £2. The table assumes you are currently paying the full £3 cap on every leg, which is true on most town and inter-town routes and on almost every rural route.

3. Who gets nothing

London. The cap is, in the press release's words, "the cap on fares outside of London". Transport for London sets its own single fare and Hopper rule and is not in the scheme. If you commute across the boundary into London on a TfL route, the capped leg is the one outside.

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. The scheme is England only. GOV.UK's page is headed "Applies to England" and lists operators by English region. Fare policy elsewhere belongs to the devolved governments.

Returns, day tickets, weekly and monthly tickets. The cap applies to a single adult fare and nothing else. Operators price their multi-journey tickets however they like.

Routes an operator has left out. Joining is voluntary, and GOV.UK's guidance lists, for every participating operator, both the routes included and the routes excluded. The excluded lists are mostly airport services, park and ride, tour buses, school contract services and a scattering of long-distance routes. Before you plan around £2, find your operator on that page and check your route number is in the included column, because a £2 cap on a route that is not in the scheme saves you nothing.

Anyone whose single is already under £2. Plenty of short urban hops are £1.80 or less. The cap does not lower them.

4. When a season ticket still beats the cap

Take the ticket's price and divide it by the number of journeys you will actually make, not the number you could make. A £16 weekly ticket is £1.60 a journey for someone making ten trips, and beats the cap. It is £2.67 a journey for someone making six, and loses. The same weekly ticket looks different in a week with a bank holiday or a day working from home. From January the honest rule is: singles at £2 unless a period ticket works out under £2 per journey you are certain to make. Most operators' app-based weekly tickets sit between £15 and £25 outside London, so the crossover is usually between eight and twelve journeys a week.

Multi-operator journeys are the other case. The cap is per single on one bus. A trip that needs two buses is two singles, £4 from January, and a multi-operator day ticket in the same area may cost less. In the mayoral areas that have taken buses back under public control, such as Greater Manchester's Bee Network, local fares and daily caps are set locally and may already be at or below £2.

5. A worked example in Doncaster

Kemi lives on the edge of Doncaster and takes a First South Yorkshire bus into the town centre and back five days a week, 46 working weeks a year. Her single is £3 today. From 1 January 2027 it is £2, so her 460 singles fall from £1,380 to £920, a saving of £460 a year, or just under £9 a week.

Her alternative is a weekly ticket. On a week when she makes all ten journeys, a weekly ticket priced at £18 would be £1.80 a journey and beat the cap by £2 for the week. In a week with a day off it is £2.25 a journey and loses. Over a year with about six short weeks, the difference between always buying the weekly and always buying singles is under £20 either way, so the practical answer is to buy singles by default and the weekly only in a full five-day week. What she should check first, on GOV.UK's operator list, is that her route number sits in First South Yorkshire's included column, because that operator excludes a long list of school and special services.

6. The honest verdict

From 1 January 2027 a single bus fare on a participating route in England costs at most £2, down from £3, and stays there through 2027. For a five-day commuter paying the full cap that is £460 to £520 a year back, for doing nothing differently.

It does nothing for London, Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, nothing for anyone whose fare is already under £2, and nothing on the routes operators have kept out. Check your route on GOV.UK's list, then from January buy singles unless a period ticket comes in under £2 per journey you will actually make.

Frequently asked

When does the £2 bus fare start? 1 January 2027, in England, on participating operators' included routes. The £3 cap runs until 31 December 2026. Source: GOV.UK guidance updated 1 September 2026.

Does the £2 cap apply in London? No. The press release describes it as the cap on fares outside London. Transport for London sets its own fares.

Is every bus route included? No. Joining is voluntary and GOV.UK lists included and excluded routes for each operator. Airport, park and ride, tour and school contract services are the usual exclusions.

Will a return or a weekly ticket also be capped? No. The cap applies to single adult fares only. A weekly ticket beats the cap only if it costs less than £2 per journey you actually make.
