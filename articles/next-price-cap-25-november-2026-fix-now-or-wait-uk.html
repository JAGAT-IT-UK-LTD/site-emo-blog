Fix now, or wait for 25 November? The date that matters is not the one you think

Ofgem announces the January to March 2027 price cap on 25 November 2026. Plenty of people are holding off until then. There is a stronger reason to think carefully, and it lands on 31 March 2027.

The short version

  • The announcement date is 25 November 2026, covering the cap for 1 January to 31 March 2027.
  • The cap you are on now is £1,723 a year for typical dual fuel use on direct debit, for 1 October to 31 December 2026.
  • The real complication is VAT. The zero rate on domestic electricity runs to 31 March 2027. Any fix running past that date has to absorb what happens next.
  • A fix is insurance, not a prediction. You are paying for certainty, and certainty has a price whether or not rates fall.
  • Check the exit fees before anything else. A fix you cannot leave cheaply is a very different product from one you can.

1. The dates that actually matter

There are three, and only the middle one gets any coverage.

  • Now to 31 December 2026. The current cap applies, at £1,723 a year for a typical dual fuel household on direct debit.
  • 25 November 2026. Ofgem announces the cap level for 1 January to 31 March 2027. Ofgem has also published the following announcement dates: 23 February 2027 for the April to June quarter, and 26 May 2027 for July to September.
  • 31 March 2027. The zero rate of VAT on domestic electricity is in place for 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. After that, unless it is extended, electricity bills carry VAT again.

Most "fix or wait" articles stop at the second date. The third one is the one that can change your answer, and we will come back to it.

2. What a fix really is, and what it is not

A fixed tariff is not a bet that prices will rise. It is insurance against them rising, and like all insurance it normally costs something even when the risk does not materialise.

Suppliers price fixes off the forward wholesale market. They already know roughly what the market expects the next few quarters to look like, and they add a margin for the risk they are taking on. So a fix priced below the current cap is not a supplier being generous. It is the market expecting the cap to fall.

Two things a fix does not do. It does not cap your bill, any more than the price cap does, because you still pay for every unit you use. And it does not protect you from a standing charge, which a fixed tariff has as well.

If you are on a fix already, note that Ofgem is explicit that the price cap does not protect you. Around 35% of households, roughly 11 million, are in that position and were unaffected by the October rise.

3. The VAT date almost no comparison table shows

This is the part worth reading twice.

Ofgem's own price cap explainer lists the costs the cap is built from, and among them is VAT at 5% on gas for 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. The corresponding relief on electricity, the zero rate, is also time limited to that same window.

The practical consequence is this. If you fix today for twelve months, roughly six of those months sit inside the VAT relief window and roughly six sit outside it. Your supplier knows that and has priced it in. If you fix for twelve months and the relief is extended in the Budget on 28 October, you have paid for a risk that did not arrive. If you stay variable and the relief ends as scheduled, the cap from April 2027 has to absorb the VAT coming back.

Nobody can tell you which way that goes. The point is that a twelve month fixed price quoted today is not a clean comparison against £1,723, because £1,723 covers a period that is entirely inside the relief window and the fix is not.

4. How to decide, in four checks

  1. Exit fees. Find the exit fee per fuel before you look at the price. A fix with no exit fee is a free option: you can leave if the cap falls below it. A fix with a high exit fee on both fuels is a commitment.
  2. Compare against the right number. Compare the fix to the cap for the period the fix covers, not to your last bill. Your last bill reflects usage as well as price.
  3. Compare unit rates and standing charges separately. A fix can beat the cap on unit rate and lose on standing charge, which matters enormously if you use very little energy.
  4. Ask what certainty is worth to you. If a £60 swing either way would genuinely disrupt your budgeting, paying a small premium for a known number is a rational purchase, not a mistake.

5. A worked example: the same decision, two households

Rhodri lives alone in a flat in Swansea and uses well below typical amounts of energy. On the current cap his annualised level would be well under £1,723, because the cap figure assumes typical consumption and he is not typical. For him the standing charge is a large share of the bill, so a fix advertising a low unit rate and a high standing charge would leave him worse off even though the headline looks good. His check is the standing charge, not the unit rate.

Marta and Josef run a four bedroom house in Peterborough with a gas boiler and two teenagers. Their consumption is above typical, so their annualised bill is above £1,723. The unit rate dominates their bill, and a swing in the cap moves their money meaningfully. For them a fix is genuinely worth pricing, and the exit fee question matters most, because it decides whether they can leave if the January or April caps come in lower.

Here is the honest conclusion. Waiting until 25 November costs you nothing if you are on a capped variable tariff, because the cap is the cap until 31 December whatever happens. It costs you something only if a good fixed deal is withdrawn in the meantime, which does happen.

And for a large group of readers the answer is simply that there is nothing to do. If you are already on a fix that runs past March, none of this changes your price. If your usage is low and your provider's standing charge is competitive, the difference between fixing and not fixing is likely to be a few pounds a quarter, which is not worth an evening of research.

Frequently asked

When exactly is the next price cap announced? 25 November 2026, for the period 1 January to 31 March 2027. Ofgem has also scheduled 23 February 2027 and 26 May 2027 for the following two quarters.

Does waiting until 25 November cost me anything? If you are on a capped standard variable tariff, no. The current cap applies until 31 December whatever is announced. The only risk of waiting is that a particular fixed deal is withdrawn.

Why does 31 March 2027 matter? The zero rate of VAT on domestic electricity runs to 31 March 2027. Any fixed tariff lasting beyond that date covers a period where the VAT position may be different, and the supplier has priced that in.

Is a fix always cheaper than the cap? No. A fix is priced off forward wholesale markets plus a margin. Sometimes it is above the cap, sometimes below. It buys certainty, which is not the same as buying cheapness.
