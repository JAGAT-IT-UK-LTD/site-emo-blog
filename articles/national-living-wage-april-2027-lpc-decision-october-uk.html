Between £13.02 and £13.34: What the April 2027 National Living Wage Will Probably Be, and What It Means on a 37.5 Hour Week

The National Living Wage is £12.71 an hour today. What it will be from 1 April 2027 is not decided, but the body that decides it has published its own range: £13.02 to £13.34, with a central estimate of £13.18. Its recommendation is due by October and the Chancellor is expected to confirm the figure at the Budget on 28 October. Here is what each end of the range means in pounds a year, who the rate covers and who is still on less, and why you should plan on the range rather than the headline.

1. The range, and where it comes from

The Low Pay Commission is the independent body that recommends minimum wage rates each autumn, and since 2024 the government's remit has told it to keep the National Living Wage at no less than two-thirds of median hourly earnings for workers aged 21 and over. The 2026 remit, published 16 March 2026, keeps that benchmark and asks for recommendations "by October 2026" for rates taking effect on 1 April 2027.

The LPC's report "The National Minimum Wage in 2026", published on GOV.UK on 1 April 2026, does the sum in public: "We project that two-thirds of median earnings for those aged 21 and over in October 2027 will fall somewhere in the range of £13.02 to £13.34, with a central estimate of £13.18." That is the whole basis for the numbers in the headlines. It is a projection of where wages will be, and the remit also asks the Commission to weigh the labour market, the cost of living including inflation forecasts to March 2028, and the effect on business, so the final recommendation can sit anywhere in that band and, in principle, outside it.

Those are gross figures, rounded to the pound, from rate times hours times 52. For a full-time worker whose personal allowance is used up, each pound of the rise is taxed at 20% and carries 8% employee National Insurance, so the £916 central rise is about £660 in the pocket. Because the personal allowance and the National Insurance thresholds are frozen until 2031, a bigger share of every minimum wage rise now goes in tax than it did three years ago, which is the quiet reason a 3.7% headline feels like less. 3. The timetable to 1 April 2027

The LPC consults through the summer, visits employers, and hands its recommendation to ministers in October. Last year it submitted on 27 October 2025 and the government announced the £12.71 rate on 26 November 2025, accepting the recommendation in full. This year the Budget falls on 28 October, and it is the natural place for the Chancellor to announce the April 2027 rates, which is why our Budget 2026 guide lists the National Living Wage as the one figure certain to be settled on the day. The regulations follow in the new year and the rate applies to pay reference periods starting on or after 1 April 2027. Nothing changes on your payslip before then.

4. Who the rate covers, and who is on less

The National Living Wage covers workers aged 21 and over. The 18 to 20 rate is £10.85, the 16 to 17 rate and the apprentice rate are £8.00, all from 1 April 2026. The government has said it wants a single adult rate from 18, and the LPC's advice last November set out a path to extend the NLW to 20-year-olds in 2027 and to 18 and 19-year-olds in 2028 or 2029, "subject to economic conditions". So a 20-year-old on £10.85 today may be on the full adult rate next April, a bigger jump than anyone over 21 will see, but that is a separate recommendation and not yet made.

The rate is a floor for every hour worked, including for salaried staff. If your salary divided by your contracted hours falls below the new rate on 1 April 2027, your employer must raise it. Uniform deductions, unpaid time on site before a shift and training you must attend all count against the floor, and HMRC's enforcement team can recover arrears going back six years. Anyone on salary sacrifice should note that the sacrificed salary cannot take them below the minimum wage either.

5. A worked example in Carlisle

Dean is 34, works 37.5 hours a week in a Carlisle distribution centre on the National Living Wage, and wants to know what to expect in April. Today he earns £12.71 an hour, £476.63 a week, £24,785 a year. If the LPC lands on its central £13.18, that becomes £494.25 a week and £25,701 a year, £916 more. After 20% income tax and 8% National Insurance on the increase, he keeps about £660, or £12.70 a week.

If the recommendation comes in at the bottom of the range, £13.02, the rise is £604 gross and about £435 net, £8.37 a week. At the top, £13.34, it is £1,228 gross, about £884 net, £17 a week. His employer, planning a 2027 wage bill for 60 staff on the same rate, is looking at somewhere between £36,000 and £74,000 of extra basic pay before employer National Insurance, which is why the sensible budget is the top of the range. The number Dean should not plan around is any of them until 28 October, and the number he should check on 1 April is his hourly rate on the first payslip after the change.

6. The honest verdict

The April 2027 National Living Wage will probably sit between £13.02 and £13.34, because that is the Low Pay Commission's own projection of the two-thirds-of-median benchmark it is told to hit. The central figure, £13.18, is a 3.7% rise worth about £916 a year gross and £660 net on a 37.5 hour week.

It is indicative, not decided. The LPC recommends by October, the Chancellor confirms at the 28 October Budget, and the rate starts on 1 April 2027. Workers should expect a modest real-terms rise; employers should budget for £13.34; and 20-year-olds should watch for the separate decision that could move them onto the adult rate.
