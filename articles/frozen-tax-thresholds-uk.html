Nobody Raised Your Tax Rate. You Are Still Paying More. Here Is Why

Ruhul Amin · Published 18 August 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked August 2026

The personal allowance and the 40% threshold have not moved, and will not move until 2031. Meanwhile wages rise. The result is 7.7 million higher-rate taxpayers, up from 5.75 million, without a single rate increase. Here is how to check where you stand and the four legal levers you actually have.

The short version

  • The numbers are frozen until 5 April 2031. Personal allowance £12,570, higher rate starts at £50,270 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • Higher-rate taxpayers go from 5.75 million to 7.70 million between 2023/24 and 2026/27. Nobody voted for a rate rise. The threshold simply stopped moving.
  • Total income taxpayers rise from 36.7 million to 40.8 million, and a record 10.2 million over-65s now pay income tax.
  • The danger zone is roughly £48,000 to £52,000. If a pay rise takes you across £50,270, every extra pound is taxed at 40%.
  • The main lever is pension contributions, which reduce the income that counts against the threshold. That is the whole trick, and it is entirely legitimate.

1. What fiscal drag actually is

Normally tax thresholds rise each year with inflation, so a pay rise that just keeps pace with prices does not push you into a higher band. Freeze the thresholds and that protection disappears.

Your wage goes up. The threshold does not. More of your income falls above it. You pay more tax, at a higher rate, without any politician announcing a tax rise. That is fiscal drag, and it is why a pay rise can feel like it vanished. The sharpest version of it hits families crossing £60,000, where the high income child benefit charge can now be paid through PAYE instead of a self assessment return.

A tax rise you can see gets debated. A tax rise that happens because a number stayed still gets ignored. That is precisely why it is used.

Two groups get hit hardest. Mid-career professionals approaching £50,270, who often do not realise the next raise is taxed at 40% rather than 20%. And pensioners, whose state pension plus a modest private pension now exceeds the frozen personal allowance, dragging people into the tax system who were never in it before. The same frozen allowance is what makes a second job feel punitive: the BR code on a second job is not an extra tax, it simply means the personal allowance has already been used up by the first one. The running cost of the freeze is added up in what frozen thresholds will have taken by the 28 October Budget.

3. Check in 60 seconds whether it affects you

  1. Find your total taxable income for the year: salary, plus bonus, plus any rental or freelance profit, plus savings interest above your allowance.
  2. Compare it to £50,270. Above that line, every additional pound is taxed at 40% rather than 20%.
  3. If you are within about £3,000 of it, you are in the zone where a bonus or overtime is worth far less than it looks, and where the levers below matter most.
  4. Scotland is different. Scottish income tax has its own bands and rates, so check the Scottish thresholds rather than these.

4. Four legal levers that actually work

1. Pension contributions. This is the big one. Money paid into a pension reduces the income assessed for the threshold, so it can bring you back under £50,270. You also get tax relief on the contribution. The money is locked until pension age, which is the trade-off.

2. Salary sacrifice. A formal arrangement where you take a lower salary and your employer pays the difference into your pension. It reduces income tax and National Insurance for you, and NI for your employer. Note that from 2029, salary sacrifice pension contributions above £2,000 a year are set to attract National Insurance, so the benefit narrows in future. Anyone dragged over the higher rate line by the freeze should read how the higher rate relief on pension contributions is claimed, because HMRC does not hand it over on its own.

3. Marriage Allowance. If one partner earns under the personal allowance and the other is a basic-rate taxpayer, some allowance can be transferred. It is small, it is free, and a lot of eligible couples never claim it. We have set out who actually qualifies and what the £252 is worth, including the four years of backdating most couples never claim.

4. Gift Aid on donations. If you are a higher-rate taxpayer, donations made under Gift Aid extend your basic-rate band, meaning you can claim back the difference through Self Assessment. Many people donate and never claim it. If your employer runs a scheme, payroll giving gives the relief at source instead, with no Self Assessment step at all.

What does not work

Turning down a pay rise. Even at 40%, you keep 60p of every extra pound, so more gross pay is still more net pay. The only genuine cliff edges in the UK system are around £100,000 and at the Child Benefit charge, not at £50,270. Note the asymmetry the freeze creates: working-age benefits are uprated every April while these thresholds are not, and the April uprating is worth putting beside them.

Worked example: Sam crosses £50,270

Sam earns £49,000 and is offered a £3,000 rise, taking him to £52,000.

The first £1,270 of that rise is taxed at 20%. The remaining £1,730 is taxed at 40%. So instead of keeping around £2,400 of the rise, he keeps roughly £2,054 after income tax, before National Insurance.

The lever: if Sam pays £1,730 into his pension, his assessed income drops back to £50,270. He keeps the full basic-rate treatment on his take-home, and £1,730 goes into his pension with tax relief rather than partly to HMRC.

He is not better off in cash today. He is better off overall, and the money is his rather than gone. Whether that suits him depends on whether he needs the cash now.

5. The 60% trap at £100,000

If your income goes above £100,000, the personal allowance is withdrawn gradually, at a rate of £1 for every £2 earned above it. The practical effect is an effective marginal tax rate of around 60% on income between £100,000 and roughly £125,140.

This is the closest thing the system has to a genuine cliff edge, and pension contributions are the standard response, because reducing assessed income below £100,000 restores the allowance. If you are near this line, it is worth proper advice rather than guesswork.

Frequently asked

Are the thresholds definitely frozen until 2031? That is the current framework, with the freeze extended to 5 April 2031. Policy can change at a Budget, and the next one is on 28 October 2026.

Does this apply in Scotland? Scotland sets its own income tax bands and rates, so the £50,270 figure does not apply there, and the Scottish higher rate starts far earlier: see Scotland's six bands against three. The fiscal drag principle still does.

I am a pensioner. Why am I suddenly paying tax? Because the personal allowance stayed at £12,570 while pensions rose. A record 10.2 million over-65s are expected to pay income tax in 2026/27 for exactly this reason. The next increase is covered in what the triple lock adds to the state pension in April 2027.

6. The honest verdict

You cannot vote your way out of a frozen threshold, and you should not turn down a pay rise over it. What you can do is control how much of your income is assessed.

If you are anywhere near £50,270 or £100,000, pension contributions are the one lever that reliably works, and it is entirely within the rules. Check your total taxable income today, see how close you are, and if the numbers are large, get proper advice rather than acting on an article.
