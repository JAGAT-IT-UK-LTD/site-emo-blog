Frozen Tax Thresholds UK: Why a Pay Rise Now Costs You More

Frozen tax thresholds mean the £12,570 personal allowance and the £50,270 higher rate line stay put until April 2031, and the Budget on 28 October 2026 did not change that. As pay rises, more of it is taxed, and HMRC projects 7.7 million higher rate taxpayers this year. Here is how to check where you stand and the four legal levers you actually have.

The short version

  • The freeze runs to 5 April 2031. The personal allowance stays £12,570 and the higher rate starts above £50,270 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • The Budget on 28 October 2026 changed nothing on wages. Its rates annex shows the same thresholds for 2027 to 2028 as for this tax year.
  • HMRC projects 7.7 million higher rate taxpayers in 2026 to 2027, up from 5.76 million in 2023 to 2024. Total income taxpayers rise from 36.7 million to 40.8 million.

1. What are frozen tax thresholds?

Frozen tax thresholds are tax bands held at the same cash level instead of rising with prices. In the UK, the personal allowance has been £12,570 since April 2022.

Normally the bands rise each year with inflation. A pay rise that only keeps pace with prices then leaves your tax position about where it was.

Freeze the bands and that protection goes. Your wage goes up. The threshold does not. More of your income lands above each line.

You pay more tax, and some of it at a higher rate, without any rate being raised. That is fiscal drag. It is why a pay rise can feel as if it vanished.

The sharpest version hits families crossing £60,000, where the high income child benefit charge can now be paid through PAYE instead of a Self Assessment return.

A tax rise you can see gets debated. A tax rise that happens because a number stayed still gets ignored. That is precisely why it is used.

2. What did the 28 October 2026 Budget decide?

It kept the freeze. The Chancellor, John Healey, presented Budget 2026 on 28 October 2026.

The rates and allowances annex shows the personal allowance at £12,570 for both 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028. The basic rate band stays £37,700 in both years.

So the higher rate threshold stays £50,270. The additional rate still starts above £125,140. The £100,000 point where the personal allowance starts to taper is unchanged.

The end date did not move either. The freeze was extended to 5 April 2031 at the 2025 Budget, and the 2026 Budget left that in place.

The weekly National Insurance thresholds are frozen alongside. The primary threshold stays £242 a week and the upper earnings limit £967 a week in 2027 to 2028.

Two changes do touch income tax. From 6 April 2027, savings interest and property income are taxed at 24%, 44% and 49%, up from 20%, 40% and 45%. Our guide to savings tax at 24% from April 2027 covers what to do first.

The second is a new Pension Tax Allowance from 6 April 2027, covered further down. The full day is set out in our Autumn Budget 2026 summary, and the year by year cost of the freeze is costed in what frozen thresholds cost you after the Budget.

3. How many people does the tax threshold freeze hit?

HMRC's own projections, published on 15 July 2026, show how far the freeze has reshaped who pays.

Put another way, more than one taxpayer in five will pay at 40% or more. In 2023 to 2024 the higher rate share was 15.7%. By 2026 to 2027 HMRC expects 18.9%.

HMRC says the rise comes mainly from population and income growth combined with frozen thresholds. Nobody voted for it. The lines simply stopped moving.

Two groups feel it most. Mid career workers nearing £50,270 often do not realise their next rise is taxed at 40% rather than 20%.

And pensioners, whose State Pension plus a small private pension now passes the frozen personal allowance. Section 8 covers them.

The same frozen allowance makes a second job feel punitive. The BR code on a second job is not an extra tax. It just means your first job has already used the allowance.

4. Does the freeze affect you? A 60 second check

  1. Add up your total taxable income for the year: salary, bonus, rental or freelance profit, and savings interest above your allowance.
  2. Compare it with £50,270. Above that line, every extra pound is taxed at 40% rather than 20%.
  3. If you are within about £3,000 of it, a bonus or overtime is worth less than it looks, and the levers below matter most.
  4. Check £60,000 and £100,000 too if you claim Child Benefit or earn near six figures. Those lines bite harder.
  5. Scotland is different. Scottish income tax has its own bands, so check Scotland's six bands against three rather than these.

5. What can you legally do about fiscal drag?

You cannot unfreeze a threshold. You can change how much of your income is measured against it. Four levers do that.

1. Pension contributions. This is the big one. Money paid into a pension reduces the income assessed against the threshold, so it can bring you back under £50,270.

You also get tax relief on the contribution. The trade off is that the money is locked away until pension age.

Higher rate taxpayers in a relief at source scheme must claim the extra relief themselves. Read how the higher rate relief on pension contributions is claimed, because HMRC does not hand it over on its own.

2. Salary sacrifice. You take a lower salary and your employer pays the difference into your pension. It cuts your income tax and National Insurance, and your employer's National Insurance too.

From 6 April 2029, salary sacrifice pension contributions above £2,000 a year will attract National Insurance. The income tax relief stays. Our salary sacrifice guide explains the change.

3. Marriage Allowance. If one partner earns under £12,570 and the other pays basic rate tax, £1,260 of allowance can move across. That cuts the couple's tax by up to £252 a year.

It is free, and many eligible couples never claim. We set out who qualifies and what the £252 is worth, including the backdating most couples miss.

4. Gift Aid on donations. If you pay higher rate tax, Gift Aid donations extend your basic rate band. You claim the difference back through Self Assessment.

Many people donate and never claim it. Our guide to higher rate relief on Gift Aid shows the sums. If your employer runs a scheme, payroll giving gives the relief at source with no return to file.

What does not work?

Turning down a pay rise. Even at 40% income tax and 2% National Insurance, you keep 58p of every extra pound. More gross pay is still more take home pay.

The real cliff edges sit at £60,000 for Child Benefit and £100,000 for the personal allowance, not at £50,270.

Note the asymmetry the freeze creates. Working age benefits are uprated every April while these thresholds are not, so the April uprating is worth putting beside them.

Worked example: Sam in Leeds crosses £50,270

Sam is a project manager in Leeds. He earns £49,000 and is offered a £3,000 rise, taking him to £52,000 in 2026 to 2027.

The first £1,270 of the rise is taxed at 20%, which is £254. The other £1,730 sits above £50,270 and is taxed at 40%, which is £692.

So the rise costs £946 in income tax. Had it all stayed in the basic rate band, the tax would have been £600.

National Insurance comes next. Roughly the first £1,270 is charged at 8%, about £102. The rest is above the upper earnings limit and charged at 2%, about £35. That is about £136 in all.

What Sam actually keeps: £3,000 minus £946 minus about £136, which is about £1,918. That is roughly 64p of each pound of the rise.

The lever: Sam puts £1,730 gross into his workplace pension through relief at source. He pays £1,384 and his provider adds £346 of basic rate relief.

His basic rate band is then extended by £1,730. He claims a further £346 back from HMRC. His real cost is about £1,038 of take home pay.

For that £1,038, his pension gains £1,730. None of his income is now taxed at 40%.

He is not richer in cash today. He is better off overall. Whether it suits him depends on whether he needs the money now.

6. What is the 60% trap at £100,000?

Above £100,000 of adjusted net income, the personal allowance falls by £1 for every £2 you earn. It is gone completely at £125,140.

That creates an effective income tax rate of 60% on income between £100,000 and £125,140. National Insurance comes on top.

The £100,000 line has been frozen since 2010, so more people drift into this band every year. Parents can lose tax free childcare at the same point.

Pension contributions are the standard response, because bringing adjusted net income below £100,000 restores the allowance. If you are near this line, get proper advice rather than guess.

7. Why are pensioners paying tax for the first time?

Because the personal allowance stayed at £12,570 while the State Pension rose each April under the triple lock.

HMRC projects 9.58 million income taxpayers above State Pension age in 2026 to 2027, 23.5% of all payers. LCP's reading of the same HMRC tables puts taxpayers aged 65 and over above 10 million for the first time.

The Budget softened this in one place. A new Pension Tax Allowance applies from 6 April 2027 to 5 April 2030.

For 2027 to 2028 it gives full relief to people over State Pension age with income up to £13,037. It is withdrawn at £1 for every £3 above that and has gone at £13,319.

So a pensioner whose only income is the full new State Pension should not pay income tax in 2027 to 2028. A pensioner with a small private pension on top still will. The next increase is in what the triple lock adds in April 2027.

Frequently asked

How long are UK tax thresholds frozen?

Until 5 April 2031. The personal allowance of £12,570, the basic rate limit of £37,700 and the matching National Insurance thresholds are all held. The freeze was extended at the 2025 Budget, and the Budget on 28 October 2026 left it in place, with the same figures for 2027 to 2028.

What does frozen tax thresholds mean?

It means the points where each tax rate starts stay at the same cash amount every year instead of rising with inflation. As wages rise, more of your income falls above each line. You pay more tax without any rate going up. Economists call this fiscal drag.

If I cross £50,270, is all my income taxed at 40%?

No. Only the part above £50,270 is taxed at 40%. Income below it is still taxed at 0% and 20%. In our example, Sam pays 40% on £1,730 of a £52,000 salary, not on the whole amount. This is the most common misunderstanding in UK income tax.

Does the freeze apply in Scotland?

The personal allowance of £12,570 is set UK wide, so that part is frozen in Scotland too. The bands above it are set by the Scottish Parliament and differ, and the Scottish higher rate starts lower than £50,270. Fiscal drag still works the same way on any band that does not rise.

Is it worth turning down a pay rise to stay under £50,270?

No. Even above £50,270 you keep about 58p of each extra pound after 40% income tax and 2% National Insurance. A pension contribution is the better tool, because it keeps the rise and moves the taxed slice into your pension, with relief at 40%.

The honest verdict

The Budget on 28 October 2026 kept the freeze, and it runs to April 2031. You cannot vote your way out of a frozen threshold, and you should not turn down a pay rise over it.

What you can control is how much of your income is assessed. If you are near £50,270, £60,000 or £100,000, pension contributions are the one lever that reliably works, and it is entirely within the rules.

Check your total taxable income today and see how close you are. If the sums are large, get proper advice rather than acting on an article.
