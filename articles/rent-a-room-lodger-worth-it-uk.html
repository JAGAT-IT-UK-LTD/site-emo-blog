Rent a Room Scheme: Is Taking In a Lodger Worth It in 2026?

The Rent a Room Scheme lets you take £7,500 a year from a lodger tax free, and a room at £700 a month still leaves about £7,082 after tax, lost council tax discount and running costs. The short version

  • The Rent a Room Scheme limit is £7,500 a year, frozen at that level since 6 April 2016. Before then it was £4,250. It has not moved in more than ten years.
  • The average UK room rent was £769 a month in Q3 2026, according to the SpareRoom rental index. That is £9,228 a year, which is £1,728 above the limit.
  • The £7,500 is measured on gross receipts, not profit. Rent, bills contributions, meals, laundry, all of it counts.
  • If you live alone today, a lodger usually costs you the 25% single person council tax discount. On the England average Band D bill of £2,392 for 2026/27, that is about £598 a year.
  • If you own the home jointly, the limit halves to £3,750 each. Couples are far more likely to breach it than a single owner.
  • From 6 April 2027 property income is taxed at 24%, not 20%, at the basic rate. The £7,500 limit itself was not changed at the Budget of 28 October 2026.
  • Lodger income is ignored for Universal Credit. That part is better than most people expect.

1. What is the Rent a Room Scheme?

The Rent a Room Scheme lets you take up to £7,500 a year from letting furnished accommodation in your only or main home, without paying income tax on it.

For the 2026/27 tax year, which runs from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, that figure is unchanged. We found no change to it in the Budget of 28 October 2026 either.

Three details do most of the damage when people get this wrong.

It is gross receipts, not profit. HMRC counts everything the lodger hands over. Rent, their share of the gas and electric, a charge for meals or laundry. Charge £600 rent plus £80 towards bills and that is £680 a month towards the limit, not £600.

It halves if the income is shared. If two people receive it, typically a couple who jointly own the house, the limit is £3,750 each. Not £7,500 each.

It applies per home, not per room. There is no limit on how many rooms you let, but the £7,500 covers all of them combined. Two lodgers at £450 a month each puts you at £10,800, well over the line.

Below the limit, the relief is automatic. There is nothing to tell HMRC and no return to file because of it, unless you are already in Self Assessment for another reason. That simplicity is the best thing about the scheme.

Fostering has a far larger version of the same idea. It is worth setting what a fostering allowance pays after qualifying care relief beside the lodger figures.

The allowance has not risen since April 2016. Room rents have. That single sentence explains why the advice you read five years ago no longer describes what happens to your money.

2. How much can you charge a lodger before paying tax?

£625 a month if you own the home alone. That is £7,500 a year. For a couple who own jointly it is only £312.50 a month each.

Almost every guide still implies an average room fits inside the scheme. That was true in 2016. It has quietly stopped being true.

According to the SpareRoom rental index, the average UK room rent reached £769 a month in Q3 2026, up 1.0% on a year earlier. Seven of the UK's nine regions hit record highs. Greater London averaged £932.

So the honest position in 2026 is this. If you charge the average UK room rent, you are not fully inside the tax free scheme.

You are in Self Assessment, you have a deadline to tell HMRC, and you have a choice to make about how the excess is taxed.

Below £625 a month the scheme does exactly what it says. Above it, the scheme becomes a partial relief, not an exemption. Stop calling it tax free in your head.

3. What does a lodger cost you?

Tax is not the only thing that eats the number. Four things do, and only one is usually mentioned.

The single person council tax discount. If you live alone, you get 25% off your council tax bill. A lodger is a second adult in the property, and in most cases the discount goes. You must tell the council.

The average Band D bill in England for 2026/27 is £2,392, up 4.9% on the year. So the discount is worth about £598 a year. That cost appears on your next bill and never appears in the "£7,500 tax free" headline.

There is one exception worth knowing. If your lodger is disregarded for council tax, for example a full time student, you keep the discount. Our guide to the council tax single person discount lists who does not count.

If you already live with another adult, you were not getting the discount anyway and this cost is nil.

Running costs. An extra adult uses hot water, heating, electricity and, on a meter, water. Work out a monthly figure from your own bills. It will be higher if they work from home.

If you charge them a contribution towards those bills, it counts towards your £7,500. And GOV.UK says you can only charge a lodger what you paid for gas and electricity, plus VAT. You cannot make a profit on reselling energy.

Permission and insurance. Tell your mortgage lender and your home insurer. Neither usually costs much, but not telling them can cancel your cover at the worst moment. If you rent, you need the landlord's permission, and many tenancy agreements forbid it outright.

Setting the room up. The accommodation must be furnished for the relief to apply. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are strongly advised, and we would not take a lodger without them.

4. What are the legal rules for taking in a lodger?

A lodger is not a tenant. Someone who shares your kitchen or bathroom is generally an excluded occupier. That means reasonable notice and no court order to end the arrangement.

The Renters' Rights Act does not turn a lodger into a tenant. On 1 May 2026 assured shorthold tenancies became assured periodic tenancies. A letting cannot be an assured tenancy if the landlord lives in the property. SpareRoom's own guidance says the Act does not apply to lodger landlords.

Deposits. A resident landlord does not have to protect a lodger's deposit in a scheme. So the deposit protection rules that govern tenancies do not cover the money you hold. Write down what it is for and when it comes back.

Right to Rent, in England. You must check that any lodger aged 18 or over has the right to rent before they move in. The civil penalty for a lodger in a private household is £5,000 for a first breach and £10,000 for further ones. Knowingly letting to someone with no right to rent is a crime, with up to 5 years in prison.

Gas safety. Taking a lodger makes you a landlord for gas safety. An annual check of the gas appliances by a registered engineer becomes a legal requirement. See the price of a gas safety certificate and what the engineer checks.

More than two lodgers. GOV.UK says your home may be classed as a house in multiple occupation if you let rooms to more than 2 people. That brings extra duties and sometimes a licence.

Earning from your home often brings a rule with it. Home dog boarding needs a council licence before the first booking, which is the same trap in a different room.

Put the terms in a written lodger agreement. Rent, notice, bills, guests, cleaning. The agreement is not there for the good months.

5. What happens if you go over £7,500?

Once your gross receipts pass £7,500, you must register for Self Assessment if you are not already in it.

The deadline to tell HMRC is 5 October after the end of the tax year in which you first went over. If you cross the line during 2026/27, that is 5 October 2027.

You then choose between two ways of being taxed.

Method A is the ordinary property income calculation. You ignore the Rent a Room Scheme and pay tax on your actual profit, which is gross receipts minus a fair share of allowable expenses. HMRC applies this by default.

Method B is the Rent a Room election. You pay tax only on the amount by which your gross receipts exceed £7,500, and you deduct no expenses at all.

For a normal lodger arrangement Method B usually wins by a wide margin. £7,500 is far larger than any honest share of your bills.

Method A only makes sense in unusual years, for example one with major repair costs for the let part of the house. You have until one year after the 31 January following the tax year to make or change the election.

What changes in April 2027. Property income gets its own tax rates of 24%, 44% and 49% from 6 April 2027 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. That was confirmed at the Budget of 28 October 2026 and is due to be legislated in the next Finance Bill.

So the slice over £7,500 will cost a basic rate taxpayer 24p in the pound, not 20p. Our guide to the 24% property income tax rate from April 2027 covers the detail.

Worked example: Nadia in Luton

Nadia owns her three bedroom house outright in her own name and lives alone. Her council tax is at the England Band D average of £2,392. With the 25% single person discount she pays £1,794.

She lets her spare room at £700 a month, a little under the UK average.

Gross receipts: £700 x 12 = £8,400. That is £900 over the limit, so she is in Self Assessment.

Tax, using Method B: £8,400 less £7,500 = £900 taxable. Nadia is a basic rate taxpayer, so 20% of £900 = £180 for 2026/27.

Council tax discount lost: 25% of £2,392 = £598.

Extra running costs: she budgets £45 a month for the extra person from her own bills = £540. That does not include the annual gas safety check, which she must add.

What she keeps: £8,400 less £180, £598 and £540 = £7,082 a year, or £590 a month.

That is still a very good outcome, and better than nearly any side hustle for the hours involved. But it is £1,318 less than the £8,400 headline. It is also below the £7,500 the adverts imply she will keep tax free. The headline is about 19% higher than what she keeps.

If Nadia had stayed on Method A, with roughly £1,200 of apportioned expenses, she would be taxed on £7,200 of profit. That is a tax bill of £1,440. The election matters.

From 2027/28, if her £900 excess is taxed as property income at the new 24% rate, the tax rises to £216. She keeps £7,046, or £587 a month.

6. Who does a lodger suit, and who should not bother?

It works well if you own your home, live alone or with one other adult, have a truly spare furnished room, and are comfortable sharing a kitchen and bathroom.

At £590 a month net, as in Nadia's case, it beats almost every side hustle on this site for money per hour. After the first fortnight the effort is close to zero.

Furnished is the word doing the work in that sentence, and the bed is where the money goes. Before buying a new one, weigh up replacing a mattress against adding a topper. The difference is most of a month of the income you are setting out to earn.

The usual alternative is short lets, so compare it honestly. What Airbnb hosting costs a UK host takes a very different shape once cleaning and empty nights are counted.

It has a useful feature for people on benefits. Lodger income is ignored for Universal Credit. It does not reduce your award, which makes a lodger one of the few ways to raise household income without the taper clawing most of it back.

Housing Benefit is different. The first £20 a week from a lodger is ignored and the rest counts as income. Council Tax Reduction is decided by your local council, so check with them before you commit.

It does not work if you rent and your tenancy forbids it, if the "spare" room is where your work or your children's things live, or if what you keep is too small to be worth the loss of privacy.

Charging £350 a month in a low rent area, after £598 of council tax and £540 of running costs, leaves about £3,062 a year. Real money, but a very different proposition from £7,000.

One worry you can usually drop. A single lodger who shares your living space does not normally restrict Private Residence Relief when you sell, so your home stays free of capital gains tax. That can change if you take more than one lodger or let a self contained part, so get advice before going further.

Frequently asked

How much can I charge a lodger before paying tax? Up to £7,500 a year in total, which is £625 a month, if the income is yours alone. If you share the income with a joint owner, the limit is £3,750 each. The limit counts everything the lodger pays you, including contributions to bills, meals and laundry.

Do I need to declare lodger income under £7,500? No. Below the limit the relief is automatic and there is nothing to report to HMRC. The exception is if you already complete a Self Assessment return for another reason. In that case you note on the return that you received Rent a Room income within the limit.

Do I lose my single person council tax discount with a lodger? Usually, yes. A lodger is a second adult, so the 25% discount ends and you must tell the council. On the England average Band D bill that costs £598 a year. You keep the discount if the lodger is disregarded, for example a full time student.

Does taking in a lodger affect Universal Credit or Housing Benefit? Lodger income is ignored for Universal Credit, so your award does not fall. Housing Benefit ignores only the first £20 a week and counts the rest as income. Council Tax Reduction rules are set by each council, so confirm your own council's position before anyone moves in.

Do I need to tell my mortgage lender about a lodger? Yes. Tell your lender and your home insurer before the lodger moves in. Most allow it, but a lodger they do not know about can put your cover at risk when you claim. If you rent your home, you need your landlord's permission first.

The honest verdict

Taking in a lodger is still one of the highest returning things a UK homeowner with a spare room can do. Nadia keeps £7,082 a year for what amounts to a few hours of admin. No normal side hustle gets near that.

But stop treating £7,500 as the number in your head. It has been frozen since April 2016 while the average UK room went to £769 a month. The two lines crossed some time ago.

At an average rent you are over the limit, in Self Assessment, and choosing between Method A and Method B. Add the £598 council tax discount you lose if you live alone, and the gap between the headline and the reality is well over £1,000 a year. From April 2027 the tax on the excess rises to 24%.

Do the sum on your own room rent, your own council tax band and your own household. If the answer is still comfortably four figures, it is worth it.

If you are letting a small room in a cheap area for £350 a month, look at that net figure honestly before you give up your spare room and your privacy for it.
