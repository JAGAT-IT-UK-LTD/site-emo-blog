Apply Before You Buy. That One Rule Is Worth More Than Any Discount

Ruhul Amin · Published 13 September 2026 · 8 min read · Rules checked September 2026

Stairlift pricing gets a lot of attention and it is largely the wrong thing to focus on. The decision that moves the most money is not which model you choose, it is whether you contact your council before or after you arrange the work. GOV.UK says plainly that you might not get any grant if you start work on your property before the council approves your application.

1. The order to do things in

Most households approach this backwards, for entirely understandable reasons. Somebody has a fall or comes out of hospital, the stairs become the problem, and within a week a salesperson is in the front room with a tablet. The work happens quickly because it needs to, and the grant conversation happens afterwards, if at all.

By then it may be too late. GOV.UK's guidance on Disabled Facilities Grants states it directly:

You might not get any grant if you start work on your property before the council approves your application.

Not a reduced grant. No grant. So the correct order is:

  1. Contact your council first, before you accept any quote or pay any deposit. The route is usually an occupational therapy assessment arranged through adult social services.
  2. Get the occupational therapy assessment. The OT decides what adaptation is actually needed, which may not be a stairlift. Sometimes it is a second handrail, a level access shower, or a downstairs bedroom.
  3. The council processes the grant application, including the means test where one applies.
  4. Approval.
  5. Then the work happens.

This is slower than buying privately, and that is a genuine cost when somebody cannot get upstairs today. If the wait is not survivable, say so to the council, ask about their urgent cases process, and ask in writing whether anything can be done to protect a future application. Some councils operate discretionary assistance for exactly this gap. What you should not do is assume the grant can be applied for retrospectively, because the guidance says it may not be.

2. What the grant is and who gets it

All of the following is from GOV.UK's Disabled Facilities Grants guidance, read on 13 September 2026.

The amounts. England up to £30,000. Wales up to £36,000. Northern Ireland up to £25,000. Scotland does not have Disabled Facilities Grants and has a separate system of support for equipment and adaptations. GOV.UK also notes that some councils may give more than the stated maximum.

What it covers. The guidance lists examples including widening doors and installing ramps or grab rails, improving access to rooms and facilities for example with a stairlift, or level access shower, improving access to your garden, building an extension such as a downstairs bedroom, providing a heating system suitable for your needs, and adapting heating or lighting controls. A stairlift is named explicitly.

Who it is for. Not only physical disability. The guidance lists physical disability, learning disability, age related needs, autism, cognitive impairment such as dementia, progressive conditions such as motor neurone disease, terminal illness and mental health conditions.

The means test, and its three exceptions. How much you get usually depends on household income and household savings over £6,000. But:

  • Disabled children under 18 can get a grant without their parents' income being taken into account. This is the single most under known fact in the whole guidance.
  • Landlords can get a grant without their income and savings being taken into account. The council may ask for the property to be let to another disabled person if the current tenant moves within five years.
  • Depending on income, you may need to contribute towards the cost. A contribution is not a refusal.

Two more things worth knowing. A Disabled Facilities Grant will not affect any benefits you get. And you can apply for another grant for the same property if you need to, for example if the condition changes.

How it is paid. Either in instalments as work progresses or in full on completion, and the council may pay the contractor directly. Normally, if you or a relative do the work yourselves, the council will only accept invoices for materials or services bought.

4. If you are paying yourself

Some households will not qualify for meaningful help after the means test, or cannot wait. If you are buying privately, four things move the money and none of them is the brand.

Straight or curved is the whole price difference. A straight rail is a stock item cut to length. A curved rail is manufactured for your particular staircase and cannot be reused, which is why curved lifts cost two to four times as much and why reconditioned curved lifts are rare and rarely a bargain.

Ask what happens at the top. A hinged or folding rail at the bottom, so the rail does not block a doorway or hall, is a common and worthwhile extra. A powered hinge costs more than a manual one and is worth it if the person using the lift is the one who will be lifting the rail.

Check the servicing and the buy back before you sign. Ask what an annual service costs, what the callout response time is, whether parts are guaranteed available for the life of the machine, and whether the company will remove the lift at the end, and at what cost. Removal is a real cost that almost never appears in a sales conversation.

Never pay in full up front. Stage payments, or a deposit and the balance on satisfactory commissioning, is normal and reasonable. If you pay by credit card, the protections in our guide to Section 75 and chargeback apply, which is a good reason to put at least part of it on a card.

Also worth checking before you buy: whether there is VAT relief. Certain products and adaptations supplied to a disabled person for personal or domestic use can be zero rated for VAT. Ask the supplier directly whether your purchase qualifies and on what basis, because on a £6,000 curved lift the difference is substantial.

5. Worked example: the Doherty house in Newcastle

A 1950s terrace in Newcastle. Mrs Doherty is 79, lives alone, has arthritis in both knees and had a fall in June. Her daughter gets two quotes within a fortnight: £2,150 for a straight stairlift, and £5,400 for a curved one because of the quarter turn at the top.

They are about to accept the curved quote when the daughter reads the GOV.UK guidance.

What they did instead. Contacted the council, asked for an occupational therapy assessment, and paid no deposit.

What the OT recommended. A curved stairlift, plus a second handrail on the landing and a grab rail by the front door.

Means test. State pension and a small occupational pension, savings of £4,100, which is under the £6,000 threshold at which savings start to count.

Outcome. Grant approved, covering the adaptation in full. Mrs Doherty paid £0.

What it would have cost had they signed first. Up to £5,400, and GOV.UK is explicit that starting work before approval may mean no grant at all.

The wait. Fourteen weeks from first phone call to installation, during which she slept in the front room. That is the honest cost of doing it in the right order, and it is a real one.

Two details from that case are worth generalising. Her savings mattered but were below the threshold where they begin to count, so a household with £5,000 put by should not assume they are disqualified. And the OT added two rails costing under £200 that the stairlift salesperson had not mentioned, because a salesperson is not assessing the whole journey through the house.

Frequently asked

How long does a DFG take? It varies enormously by council and by how urgent the case is judged to be. Weeks to many months. Ask at first contact what the current timescale is and whether there is an urgent route, and get the answer in writing so you can plan around it.

Do I have to pay it back if I sell the house? Councils can place a charge on the property in certain circumstances for larger grants, recoverable if the property is sold within a set period. This is a local matter and the rules vary, so ask your council directly whether a charge will be registered against your grant and on what terms. Do not assume either way.

My relative is a tenant. Does this still apply? Yes. Tenants can apply, and separately, landlords can apply without an income and savings test, with the condition described above about re letting to another disabled person within five years. Either route can work, and it is worth the landlord and tenant having the conversation early.

Does a stairlift damage the stairs? A stairlift rail fixes to the treads, not the wall, so the fixing holes are in the staircase. On removal these are filled and are usually invisible under carpet. It is not a reason to avoid one, but ask about making good at removal when you ask about the buy back.

What does it cost to run? Very little. A stairlift charges a battery from a standard socket and uses a handful of kilowatt hours a year. At the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 26.32p per kWh, the electricity is a few pounds annually. The servicing contract is the real running cost.

The honest verdict

Phone the council before you phone a stairlift company. That single decision is worth up to £30,000 in England, and doing it the other way round can forfeit the lot, on GOV.UK's own wording.

If you have a disabled child under 18, apply regardless of what you earn, because their grant is assessed without your income. If you are a landlord, apply regardless of what you own, because yours is assessed without an income and savings test. Those two exceptions are where the most money goes unclaimed.

If you are buying privately, get the occupational therapy view anyway if you can, because the cheapest correct answer is sometimes two handrails and not a machine. And if it is a machine, ask what the annual service costs and what removal costs before you sign, not after.
