Overpay the mortgage or save the difference? One comparison decides it

Ruhul Amin · Published 6 September 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked September 2026

There is no tax relief on a residential mortgage, so the rate on your statement is already a net rate. Savings interest usually is not. Line those two numbers up properly and the decision stops being a matter of opinion.

The short version

  • Compare the mortgage rate with the savings rate after tax. Nothing else in this decision is arithmetic. Everything else is about risk and flexibility.
  • The Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 at basic rate and £500 at higher rate (GOV.UK, page updated 2 September 2026). Interest above it is taxed at your normal rate.
  • At a 4% savings rate, a basic rate taxpayer runs out of allowance at £25,000 of savings. After that the effective rate is 3.2%. A higher rate taxpayer runs out at £12,500 and drops to 2.4%.
  • A cash ISA changes the answer because ISA interest never counts towards the allowance, so the headline rate is the net rate.
  • Keep an emergency fund first. An overpayment is gone: most lenders will not hand it back, and Bank Rate has been held at 3.75% since 30 July 2026 with the next decision on 17 September, so nobody should be betting the household float on a rate call.

1. The only comparison that matters

A residential mortgage carries no tax relief. That relief was phased out for homeowners decades ago and it is not coming back. So if your fix says 4.29%, then 4.29% is what every extra pound you throw at the balance earns you, guaranteed, with no tax to pay on the gain and no risk that the return fails to materialise.

Savings interest is different. It is taxable income. You have allowances that shelter some of it, and above those allowances HMRC takes 20%, 40% or 45%. So the number advertised on a savings account is a gross number, and the number you should be comparing is what is left after tax.

That gives you a single test:

Overpay if your mortgage rate is higher than your savings rate after tax. Save if it is lower.

It really is that plain. The arguments people have about this topic are almost never about the arithmetic. They are about whether you should have the money accessible, which is a separate question and is dealt with further down.

2. Where the Personal Savings Allowance flips the answer

GOV.UK's tax on savings interest page, updated 2 September 2026, gives three allowances that can shelter interest.

The Personal Allowance, usually £12,570, if your wages or pension have not already used it up.

The starting rate for savings, up to £5,000, but reduced by £1 for every £1 of other income above your Personal Allowance. If your other income is £17,570 or more, you get none of it. For anyone in full-time work this is usually zero.

The Personal Savings Allowance, which is the one that matters to most people:

Income Tax bandPersonal Savings Allowance
Basic rate£1,000
Higher rate£500
Additional rate£0

Turn that into a balance. If your savings pay 4%, a basic rate taxpayer generates £1,000 of interest on £25,000. That is the point where the allowance runs out. Every pound of savings above £25,000 earns 4% gross and 3.2% after 20% tax. A higher rate taxpayer hits £500 of interest on £12,500 and drops to 2.4% after 40% tax above that.

So the same household, with the same mortgage and the same savings account, can get two different right answers depending on how much they already have saved. Below the allowance, saving at 4% and paying a mortgage at 3.9% is a small win. Above the allowance, that same pair becomes a loss.

Two practical notes. Joint accounts split the interest equally between holders for tax, so a couple effectively has two allowances. And ISA interest does not count towards the allowance at all, which is why a cash ISA at the same headline rate is worth more to anyone who is past their limit.

3. Priya in Wakefield: the same £200 both ways

Priya has £180,000 left on her mortgage with 22 years to run. Her fix is at 4.29%, which is what her own paperwork says rather than any market average. Her monthly payment is £1,054.57. She is a basic rate taxpayer, she already has £30,000 in an easy access account paying 4%, and she has £200 a month spare.

Route one, overpay £200 a month. The mortgage clears in about 16 years and 10 months instead of 22 years. That is roughly 5 years and 2 months early, and it saves about £25,500 of interest she would otherwise have paid.

Route two, save £200 a month in the same easy access account. She is already past her £1,000 allowance on the £30,000 she holds, so every pound of new interest is taxed at 20%. Her effective rate is 3.2%. Over the same 16 years and 10 months she would build about £53,300, while still owing about £58,900 on the mortgage. Net position: about £5,600 worse off than route one.

Route three, save £200 a month in a cash ISA at 4%. No tax, so the full 4% compounds. She would have about £57,400 against about £58,900 of debt. About £1,530 worse off than overpaying. Much closer, because the gap between 4.29% and 4.00% is small.

The shape of that result is the general lesson. When the savings rate is taxed, overpaying wins comfortably. When it is sheltered in an ISA, the two routes converge and the deciding factor becomes flexibility rather than money. If Priya found an ISA paying more than 4.29%, saving would win outright.

4. Four things that beat the arithmetic

An emergency fund. Money paid into a mortgage is not accessible. Some lenders offer a borrow-back or offset facility, most do not, and a redraw usually means a full remortgage application at whatever rate applies then. Three to six months of essential spending should sit in cash before a single pound of overpayment, even if the arithmetic says otherwise. This is not a rounding error in the maths, it is the reason people end up on a credit card at 24%.

Expensive debt. A credit card, an overdraft or a car finance agreement at 15% or 20% beats both routes by a distance. Clear that first and the mortgage question can wait.

Your workplace pension. If you are not getting the full employer match, that match is free money at a rate no mortgage or savings account can touch. Take it before you overpay.

Sleep. Some people simply want the mortgage gone and will accept a slightly worse financial outcome for it. That is a legitimate preference, not a mistake, provided you know what it is costing. In Priya's ISA case it costs about £1,530 over 17 years, which is about £90 a year. Plenty of people would pay £90 a year to not think about it.

5. What to check on your own mortgage first

The 10% rule. Most fixed-rate deals allow overpayments of up to 10% of the outstanding balance each year without penalty. Go over it and you pay an early repayment charge, typically a percentage of the amount overpaid. On Priya's £180,000 balance the allowance is about £18,000 a year, so £200 a month is nowhere near it. On a small balance near the end of a term it is much easier to breach.

Whether the overpayment shortens the term or cuts the payment. These are not the same thing. Reducing the term saves far more interest. Reducing the monthly payment gives you cash flow now. Lenders differ on which is the default and some apply it silently, so tell them in writing which you want.

When the overpayment is credited. Some lenders apply it to the balance immediately, others hold it until an annual recalculation, which quietly costs you up to a year of interest on the money. Ask.

What happens at the end of your fix. If your fix ends soon, the comparison you should be running is against your likely next rate, not your current one. Bank Rate has been held at 3.75% since 30 July 2026, with the Bank of England's next decision on 17 September 2026 and inflation at 2.9% against a 2% target. Nobody, including the Monetary Policy Committee, knows where it goes next, so plan on the rate you can see rather than the one you hope for.

The verdict

Work out your savings rate after tax, then compare it with your mortgage rate. If you are a basic rate taxpayer with more than about £25,000 in ordinary savings, or a higher rate taxpayer with more than about £12,500, your real savings rate is a fifth or two fifths lower than the number on the advert, and overpaying will usually win. If your savings sit in a cash ISA the two routes are close enough that accessibility should decide it. In every case: emergency fund first, expensive debt first, employer pension match first, and check the 10% overpayment limit before you set up the standing order.

Frequently asked

Does overpaying hurt my credit file? No. Reducing a mortgage balance faster does not damage your credit record. What can cause a problem is breaching an early repayment charge and then disputing it, or overpaying so aggressively that you miss other commitments.

Should I overpay or put the money in a pension instead? A pension gets tax relief going in, which a mortgage overpayment does not, so on pure arithmetic a pension usually wins for a higher rate taxpayer. The catch is that you cannot touch it until at least age 55, rising to 57 from April 2028. They are answering different questions: one buys a lower cost of living now, the other buys income later.

My mortgage rate is 1.9% on an old fix. Should I still overpay? Almost certainly not while that rate lasts. At 1.9%, even a basic rate taxpayer past their allowance earning 3.2% after tax on savings is comfortably ahead. Build the cash, and when the fix ends you will have a lump sum to reduce the balance with if the new rate is higher.

Is the Personal Savings Allowance going to change? It has been £1,000 and £500 since 2016 and is not indexed, so it shelters less every year that rates stay high. The Budget on 28 October 2026 could change it. The figures on this page are the ones published on GOV.UK on 2 September 2026.
