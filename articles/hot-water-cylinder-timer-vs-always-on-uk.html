The Hot Water Argument Is About One Number, and It Is Printed on Your Tank

Ruhul Amin · Published 13 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Every household with a hot water cylinder has had the same argument: is it cheaper to heat it twice a day on a timer, or leave it on all the time? Both sides are usually reasoning from feel. The answer is decided by a single figure, the cylinder's standing heat loss per day, and on a modern tank it is written on the manufacturer's badge.

The short version

  • Only one thing is wasted by leaving it on: standing loss. The heat that leaks out of the tank into the cupboard. The hot water you actually use costs the same either way.
  • Modern cylinders declare their standing loss in kWh per day on the badge. Go and read yours. That is the whole calculation.
  • A well insulated modern cylinder loses roughly 1 to 2 kWh a day. A timer might save half of that, which at electric immersion rates is around £48 to £96 a year, and at gas rates around £16 to £33.
  • An old bare copper tank is a different question entirely. A jacket on an uninsulated tank is one of the best paybacks in the house, usually under a year.
  • Never reduce cylinder temperature below 60C. Stored water needs to reach 60C periodically to control Legionella. This is the one place where saving energy is the wrong priority.
  • If the tank is bare, buy a jacket before you touch the timer. £20 to £40 beats any amount of scheduling.

1. Why standing loss is the only thing in play

Here is the part that settles most kitchen table arguments.

If you have a bath, the energy to heat that bathful of water is the same whether the cylinder was kept hot all day or heated an hour beforehand. Water does not get cheaper to heat because you heated it recently. The energy content is set by how much water and how much you raise its temperature, and neither changes.

So the only thing that leaving it on can possibly waste is the heat that escapes from the tank while nobody is using it. That is the standing loss, and it is the entire subject.

Leaving the hot water on does not cost you the hot water. It costs you the insulation's failure to hold it.

Which means the size of the prize depends entirely on how well your tank is insulated, and the range across British homes is enormous. A bare copper cylinder from the 1970s bleeds heat continuously into an airing cupboard. A modern factory foamed cylinder loses a fraction of that. Same argument, wildly different answer, which is exactly why generic advice on this topic is useless.

There is one real qualification. A timer does not save the full standing loss, only the portion that occurs while the tank would otherwise have been reheated to stay hot. Let the tank cool overnight and it is colder in the morning, so the morning reheat is longer. You save the difference, not the whole thing. Roughly half of the standing loss is a fair working assumption for a typical two period timer, and any figure quoted to you without that caveat is overstating the case.

2. Finding the number on your own tank

Go to the airing cupboard and look for the manufacturer's data badge or plate, usually on the front of the cylinder near the top. On any reasonably modern unvented or vented cylinder you are looking for a figure labelled something like standing heat loss, heat loss or standby loss, expressed in kWh per 24 hours, or sometimes in watts.

Then one multiplication, using the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026:

Electric immersion, at 26.32p per kWh. standing loss in kWh/day × 365 × 0.2632 = annual cost of the loss.

Gas boiler heating the cylinder, at 7.97p per kWh and 88% efficiency. (standing loss ÷ 0.88) × 365 × 0.0797 = annual cost of the loss.

A timer saves roughly half of that.

If the badge shows watts instead, multiply by 24 and divide by 1,000 to get kWh per day. A 60W standing loss is 1.44 kWh a day.

If there is no badge, or you cannot read it, the tank is probably old enough that the answer is not a timer at all. Look at what is on the outside:

  • Bare copper, nothing on it. The worst case, and the best opportunity. Buy an insulating jacket.
  • An old jacket, thin, gaping at the seams or slipped. Refit it or replace it. Gaps matter more than thickness.
  • A hard foam coating bonded to the tank, usually green or grey. This is a modern factory insulated cylinder. Your standing loss is already low and the timer argument is worth a few tens of pounds, not hundreds.

While you are there, check the pipes. The first metre or so of pipework off the top of the cylinder gets hot every time the tank does, and uninsulated copper pipe in a cold cupboard is a small chimney for your money. Foam pipe lagging is a couple of pounds a length and takes minutes, and our guide to cylinder jackets and pipe lagging has the arithmetic.

And if you have a combi, there is no cylinder, no standing loss and no argument. Water is heated on demand. Whoever in the household is switching the hot water on and off is achieving nothing at all.

4. The safety limit on all of this

There is one hard floor under every saving in this article and it is not negotiable.

Stored hot water needs to reach 60C periodically. Below about 50C, Legionella bacteria can multiply in stored water. This is the reason cylinder thermostats are set where they are, and it is why the advice in our guide to turning your boiler flow temperature down to 60C explicitly excludes homes with a hot water cylinder from going any lower.

Practically, that means three things:

  1. Do not turn the cylinder thermostat below 60C to save money. The saving is small and the risk is not a money question.
  2. If you use a timer, make sure the tank still gets fully up to temperature in at least one of its heating periods each day. A timer that only ever part heats the tank, leaving the bottom tepid, is worse than leaving it on.
  3. If the house has been empty for a while, for example after a holiday, run the hot water up to full temperature and run the taps and shower for a few minutes before normal use.

Anti scald is the mirror image of this. Water stored at 60C is hot enough to injure, particularly for children and older people, which is why thermostatic mixing valves exist at the point of use. Store hot, deliver cooler. If you have vulnerable people in the house and no mixer valves, that is worth asking a plumber about, and it is also the kind of adaptation covered by the grants in our guide to stairlifts and Disabled Facilities Grants.

5. Worked example: the Mbeki house in Swindon

A 1990s house in Swindon, gas boiler with a vented cylinder in the airing cupboard, wrapped in the original jacket which has slipped and gapes at the top. Two adults, two teenagers, showers morning and evening. The hot water has been left on constantly for years.

Estimated standing loss with the gaping jacket. About 3 kWh a day.

Cost of that loss on gas. 3 ÷ 0.88 = 3.41 kWh of gas a day, × 365 × 7.97p = £99 a year.

Fix one, refit and top the jacket. New jacket £28. Loss drops to about 1.8 kWh a day, costing £60 a year. Saves £39 a year for £28. Payback under nine months.

Fix two, add a timer, two periods a day. Saves roughly half the remaining loss, about £30 a year, for £0 because the programmer already has a hot water channel nobody had set.

Fix three, lag the first metre of pipework. £14, worth perhaps £10 a year.

Combined. £42 spent, about £79 a year saved, and it all pays back inside seven months.

Two honest points about that example. The £79 is not going to change anybody's life, and it would be dishonest to present it as if it will. But it is a better return than most of the things households do spend money on for energy reasons, it took an afternoon, and the jacket keeps working for a decade.

And the shape of it is what matters. The jacket did more than the timer, and the timer was free. If they had done the arithmetic the way the internet usually presents it, arguing about the timer and ignoring the tank, they would have got the smaller half of the saving and spent longer doing it.

Frequently asked

Is it cheaper to leave the heating on low all day? Different question, same fallacy, opposite answer. A house loses heat to outside continuously, so keeping it warmer for longer genuinely does use more energy. A cylinder is inside the thermal envelope, so its losses partly warm the house anyway, which is why the hot water version of this argument is so much closer than the heating version. Do not transfer the answer from one to the other.

Does the loss really go to waste in winter? Not entirely. Heat leaking from a cylinder into an airing cupboard inside your house is heat your heating system then does not have to supply, so in the heating season some of the standing loss is recovered. In summer it is wasted completely, and in summer you may also be paying to make a cupboard uncomfortably warm. This is a good argument for using the timer more aggressively between April and October than in January.

What about Economy 7 or a time of use tariff? That changes everything, because the price per kWh is no longer one number. On Economy 7 the right pattern is usually to heat the cylinder overnight on the cheap rate and coast through the day, which is closer to "always on" than to a daytime timer. Our guide to storage heaters and Economy 7 covers the tariff arithmetic.

Should I just fit a new cylinder? Only if yours is failing, or if you are having other heating work done anyway. Replacing a functioning insulated cylinder to save 0.5 kWh a day would take decades to pay back at the figures above. Replacing a bare tank with a modern foamed one as part of a boiler replacement, on the other hand, is sensible.

The honest verdict

Go and look at your cylinder before you take a side in the argument. If there is a badge, read the standing loss in kWh per day and do the one multiplication above. That is your answer, and it is yours rather than a national average.

If the tank is bare or the jacket has slipped, stop thinking about timers. A £20 to £40 jacket is the best paying thing in this article and on an uninsulated tank it returns its cost in weeks. Fix the insulation, then set the timer, then lag the pipes, in that order.

On a modern factory insulated cylinder, a timer is worth having and worth roughly £48 to £96 a year on an electric immersion or £16 to £33 on gas. Set it, use it harder in summer than in winter, and never take the cylinder below 60C to chase a few more pounds.
