Everyone Quotes 24 Hours. The Same Sentence Also Says 89 Days And Zero

Ruhul Amin · Published 19 September 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Amazon's cookie window is the most repeated fact in affiliate marketing and the most widely misunderstood. The clause that defines it contains three numbers, not one, and the two nobody quotes change how you should write, what you should link to, and why a 30 day window is not automatically better.

The short version

  • 24 hours is when the session ends, not when your claim dies. Amazon's clause ends a session at the first of three events, and elapsed time is only one of them.
  • Add to basket inside the session and you have 89 days. Amazon pays if the customer completes the order no later than 89 days after the initial click through.
  • Another affiliate's link ends your session instantly. Clicking through somebody else's Special Link is one of the three ending events, with no time attached.
  • Placing any non digital order also ends it. Your commission is earned, and the window shuts for anything they buy afterwards.
  • There is a 180 day backstop. The product must be shipped, streamed or downloaded and paid for within 180 days of purchase.
  • A 30 day window on a competing network is not automatically better, because a long window on a product nobody buys twice is worth less than a short one on a basket someone fills.

1. What the clause actually says

The 24 hour figure comes from the Amazon Associates Program Commission Income Statement, a Program Policy that sits underneath the Operating Agreement. It defines a Session, and the definition is more interesting than the summary of it.

A session begins when a customer clicks through your Special Link. It ends on the first of three things happening:

  • 24 hours elapse from that click;
  • the customer places an order for a product other than a digital product;
  • the customer clicks through a Special Link to an Amazon Site that is not yours.

Almost all affiliate advice reports the first bullet and drops the other two. That is how the phrase 24 hour cookie became the standard shorthand, and the shorthand is what leads people to write the wrong kind of article.

Notice what the second bullet means in practice. If someone clicks your link for a kettle and then buys a kettle, your session is over. If they come back four hours later and buy a £900 laptop, that purchase is outside your session. The window does not stay open for the rest of the day just because 24 hours have not passed.

The third bullet is the one with the sharpest teeth, and it gets its own section below.

There is also a backstop that applies to everything: the product must be shipped to, streamed or downloaded by, and paid for by the customer within 180 days of the purchase. That is why pre orders and long lead time items sometimes never pay out, and it is not a tracking failure when they do not.

2. The 89 days almost nobody mentions

In the same clause, immediately after the session definition, Amazon sets out how a qualifying purchase can happen. One route is the 1-Click feature. The other is this: the customer purchases a product by adding that product to their shopping cart and completing the order for it no later than 89 days after their initial click through of your Special Link.

Read that again, because it reverses the usual advice. The 24 hours is the window to get something into the basket. Once it is in the basket, you have 89 days to be paid for it.

For a UK affiliate that has direct, practical consequences.

Adding to basket is the conversion event that matters, not buying. The reader who adds a £300 item to their basket at 11pm on Tuesday and buys it on payday three weeks later is still your sale. The reader who bookmarks the product page and comes back is not.

This changes what a good affiliate article looks like. The standard advice, aimed at a 24 hour window, is to create urgency so people buy immediately. The clause suggests something calmer and more honest: help people decide, and give them a reason to put the thing in the basket while they are still reading, even if they intend to think about it. That is a better article as well as a better commercial outcome, which is a rare combination.

It is especially relevant to expensive, considered purchases. Nobody buys a £700 mattress in the four minutes after reading a review. Plenty of people add one to a basket while deciding. Under a strict 24 hour reading of the rule, high value considered items look like a bad category for Amazon affiliates. Under the actual rule, they are often the best one.

Two honest caveats. This is Amazon's stated rule, not a guarantee about how any particular basket behaves across devices and sign in states, and none of it overrides the session ending events above. If the reader clicks another affiliate's link before adding to basket, there is no session left to add to.

3. The number that is zero, and why it matters most

The third session ending event has no duration attached at all. Your session ends the moment the customer clicks through a Special Link to an Amazon Site that is not your Special Link.

In other words the window is not really yours for 24 hours. It is yours until the next affiliate link the reader touches, whoever that belongs to. Effectively, last click wins, and the clock is irrelevant to that.

This is the honest explanation for something that frustrates every new affiliate: traffic that clearly converts, and commission that does not appear. A reader who opens three comparison articles in three tabs, as readers actually do, has clicked three affiliate links. Only the last one has a live session. The other two writers see the click and no sale, and conclude their audience is not buying.

What to do about it is narrower than the problem, and it is worth being honest about that too.

Be the last thing they read, not the first. Articles that resolve a decision tend to be read last. Articles that open a decision tend to be read first and then left behind in a tab.

Do not scatter links. Eleven links to eleven products invites comparison shopping across other people's articles. One or two confident recommendations, placed at the point the reader has decided, respect the actual rule.

Stop reading raw click counts as a performance signal. Clicks measure interest. Under a last click rule, they do not measure your share of the sale, and optimising for clicks can actively mislead you.

And the wider point about window length. A 30 day cookie on another network sounds four weeks better than 24 hours, and often is not, because most networks also operate last click and most purchases that happen at all happen quickly. A long window on a product nobody adds to a basket is worth less than a short window on a basket someone fills. Judge a programme on the commission rate, the approval rate and whether its products get added to baskets, not on the length of the cookie. 4. Worked example: Dev in Hull, the same 1,000 clicks two ways

Dev writes about home coffee equipment from Hull. In a month he sends 1,000 clicks to Amazon UK. He has two article formats and the same traffic behaves very differently on each.

Format one, a roundup: The 11 Best Espresso Machines. Eleven products, eleven links. Readers arrive mid decision, click two or three of his links, open other sites in other tabs, and go away to think. Of 1,000 clicks, a high proportion end in the reader clicking a different affiliate's link somewhere else, which ends Dev's session at once. A share of the rest expire at 24 hours untouched. The purchases that do happen are mostly cheap accessories bought on impulse in the same visit.

Format two, a single decision piece: Should You Buy a £600 Machine or a £200 One and a Good Grinder. One recommendation each way, two links. It is read last, because it resolves the question rather than opening it. Readers who are convinced add the machine to their basket while reading, then buy on payday.

Why format two pays better, in the language of the clause. It produces fewer clicks and more baskets. A basket filled inside the session pays for up to 89 days. A click that is followed by someone else's link pays for zero. Format one maximises the thing the rule does not reward and exposes every click to the thing that kills it.

What this does not mean. It does not mean roundups are worthless; they earn search traffic and accessory sales. It means judging them by clicks flatters them, and judging them by basket adds does not.

The bit Dev cannot fix. He cannot stop a reader clicking somebody else's link, and no article structure changes that. He can only decide whether his page is the one that ends the research or the one that starts it.

Disclosure is not optional. The Amazon agreement requires the statement As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases to appear clearly and prominently, and says a violation of that section is a material breach. Beyond Amazon's rules, UK consumer protection law requires that paid or commissioned promotion is made clear to readers.

The honest verdict

The 24 hour cookie is the most quoted and least read rule in affiliate marketing. The clause it comes from names three ending events and a second route to payment, and the summary keeps only one of the four.

The useful version is this: 24 hours is how long the reader has to act, adding to basket inside that window buys you up to 89 days, placing an order ends the session, and clicking another affiliate's link ends it immediately with no clock at all.

Practically, that argues for fewer links and better articles. Write the piece that resolves a decision rather than the one that opens it, put your recommendation where the reader has already made up their mind, and stop treating click counts as a measure of how you are doing.

And do not choose a programme on cookie length. On last click attribution, a 30 day window and a 24 hour window both end at the same event most of the time. The commission rate, whether sales get approved, and whether anyone actually adds the thing to a basket will decide your income long before the clock does.

Frequently asked

So is the Amazon cookie 24 hours or 89 days? Both, describing different things. The session lasts up to 24 hours and is the window in which the customer must act. If what they do inside it is add the product to their basket, Amazon's clause allows the order to be completed up to 89 days after the initial click through.

Does the window reset if the reader clicks my link again? A new click through your Special Link starts a new session. But if in between they clicked a different affiliate's link, your earlier session had already ended at that moment, which is why the order of clicks matters more than the number.

Is a 30 day cookie on another network better? Not necessarily. Most networks also operate last click, so a longer window still ends the moment another affiliate's link is clicked. Commission rate, approval rate and whether the product gets added to a basket usually matter more than window length.

What happens if the customer buys something else entirely? Under the session rules you can be paid on qualifying purchases made during the session, which is why a reader who clicks a link for a kettle and buys something unrelated in the same session can still earn you commission. But placing an order for a non digital product ends the session, so anything bought after that is outside it.
