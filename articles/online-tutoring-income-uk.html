The short version

  • Published UK guides put GCSE tutoring at about £30 to £55 an hour and A Level at £35 to £70. Listings data is lower: FindTutors puts typical online GCSE maths at £25.
  • MyTutor pays you £22.48 from a £40 lesson, by its own earnings table. Above a £48 price you keep 60%. Below it, the platform keeps more.
  • Tutorful adds its fee to the student's price and pays tutors their full rate, but its tutor applications are closed. PMT Education says it takes 18% per lesson.
  • The same £40 lesson can pay £22.48 or £40. The only difference is how the student found you.
  • Tax: the trading allowance is £1,000 until 5 April 2027, then £3,000. Past it, you register for Self Assessment.
  • The benchmark is the National Living Wage, £12.71 an hour now and £13.21 from April 2027. Tutoring clears it from the first lesson.

1. How much do online tutors charge in the UK?

The honest answer is a range, and it depends on who is counting.

Guides written for parents quote the higher figures. Listings data from tutor marketplaces, where many beginners advertise, comes in lower.

LevelPublished guide rangeTypical online listing
GCSE£30 to £55 an hour£20 an hour, general GCSE
GCSE mathsTop half of the GCSE range£25 an hour
A Level£35 to £70 an hour£20 an hour, general A Level
A Level mathsTop half of the A Level range£25 an hour

The guide ranges come from TutorCruncher, dated May 2026. The listing figures come from the FindTutors 2026 price report, built from 38,602 listings across 20 UK cities.

Read the gap as a warning, not a contradiction. The bottom of a marketplace is crowded with tutors charging little. The top of the range is where experienced tutors with results and referrals sit.

Online is not always cheaper than in person. In the FindTutors data, online GCSE listings were actually higher than in person ones. What online removes is travel, and an hour of unpaid driving costs you more than any small rate difference.

What raises your online tutor hourly rate, in rough order of effect:

  • The subject. Maths and the sciences pay more than most.
  • The level. A Level pays more than GCSE, and university level more again.
  • Examiner experience. The single most quotable credential.
  • Results you can point to. Not your degree class, which almost no parent asks about.

2. How much does MyTutor and each platform take?

This is where most of your money goes, or does not go. The models differ in ways that are easy to miss.

MyTutor does not print a single percentage on its help page. It prints an earnings table instead, and the table tells you everything.

So the cheaper the lesson, the bigger the platform's share. From £48 upwards, the tutor keeps 60%.

MyTutor's monthly membership bands follow the same pattern. Band 1 lessons cost the family £26 to £30 and pay the tutor £13 to £15.

Here is how the main models compare on a £40 lesson.

PlatformModelOn a £40 lesson
MyTutorKeeps a share of the lesson price, set by price bandYou receive £22.48
TutorfulService fee added on top, paid by the student. No commission from the tutorYou keep £40. Tutor applications are closed
PMT Education18% platform fee per lesson, its own published figureYou keep about £32.80
Direct studentNo platformYou keep £40, minus the time spent finding them

The two models are genuinely different, not just different numbers. A commission taken from the tutor lowers your pay for the same price.

A service fee added to the student raises the price without lowering your pay. You look more expensive, but your rate stays intact.

Neither is dishonest. But on a tutor commission model you must price with the deduction in mind. A £40 lesson on MyTutor is a £22.48 job.

On a £40 MyTutor lesson the platform keeps £17.52. Whether that partner earns its share depends on whether you could have found the student yourself.

3. What about the unpaid hours?

Tutoring looks like a pure hourly job, and it is not. A one hour lesson is rarely one hour of your time.

  • Preparation. Often 10 to 30 minutes for a new topic, less once you have taught it a few times. A reusable bank of materials is the biggest efficiency gain available.
  • Marking and feedback. If you set work between sessions, and you should, someone has to look at it.
  • Parent communication. Messages, progress updates, rescheduling. Small each time, significant in total.
  • Admin. Invoicing, chasing payment, keeping records for tax.

A fair planning figure is 20 to 30% on top of billed time in your first year. It falls towards 10% once your materials are built.

So £22.48 an hour on a platform is closer to £17 to £19 in year one. That is still above the £12.71 National Living Wage, but it is not £40.

Two policies protect you. A 24 hour cancellation policy, stated before the first lesson. And payment in advance for blocks of lessons.

Late cancellations are the largest hidden cost in private tutoring. Almost every experienced tutor has learned this the expensive way.

4. How do I find direct students, legally?

The obvious move is to take platform students off platform. Read your contract first. Most platforms prohibit it, can remove you, and in some cases can pursue the fee.

The legitimate routes to direct students:

  • Referrals from families you already teach directly. The strongest source by a distance. Parents talk to other parents.
  • Local school and community groups. Village and school Facebook groups, parish newsletters, notice boards. Unglamorous and effective.
  • Your own simple page. Subjects, levels, rate, DBS status, how to contact you. It needs to exist so people can check you are real.
  • Schools directly. Some pass tutor details to parents who ask, especially for shortage subjects.

The sensible plan is a platform for the first term, with direct students built alongside it. The platform solves the hardest problem, your first students and first testimonials.

5. What does a real academic year earn?

Tom, 29, maths graduate in Bristol. Tutoring evenings, term time only, starting September 2026.

Autumn term, 12 teaching weeks. Six hours a week on MyTutor at a £40 price, so he receives £22.48 a lesson. 72 lessons. £1,618.56. Preparation adds about 30%, so his real rate is about £17.29 an hour.

Spring term, 12 weeks. Two families find him through a school group at his full £40. Four platform hours plus three direct hours a week. Platform £1,079.04, direct £1,440, total £2,519.04. One extra hour a week, £900 more than autumn.

Summer term, 9 weeks before exams end. Demand peaks. Two platform hours plus seven direct, and he raises his direct A Level rate to £45. Platform £404.64, direct £2,835, total £3,239.64.

Academic year: about £7,377 from 237 teaching hours.

The comparison that matters. Had all 237 hours stayed on the platform at £22.48, he would have earned about £5,328. Moving hours direct earned him about £2,050 more for the same teaching. The only change was who introduced him to the family.

Tax. He passes the £1,000 trading allowance in his first term, so he must register for Self Assessment. From 6 April 2027 the allowance rises to £3,000, which helps a smaller tutor but not Tom.

Seasonality. Demand drops in July and August and is strongest from January to May. Budget for a quiet summer rather than being surprised by it.

6. Do I need a DBS check, insurance and tax registration?

  • A DBS check is not legally required for private tutoring, but almost every parent will ask, and most platforms require one. You can request a basic check yourself on GOV.UK. An enhanced check usually comes through a platform or an umbrella body.
  • Record your online lessons or keep the chat log, with the parent informed. This protects the student and it protects you.
  • Keep a parent in the loop. For under 18s, communicate through the parent rather than privately with the child.
  • Professional indemnity insurance is usually inexpensive and some platforms require it. Our guide to professional indemnity for UK freelancers explains what it covers.
  • The trading allowance is on gross income. It is £1,000 until 5 April 2027. At £30 a lesson you pass it in 34 lessons. Read how the £3,000 allowance works from April 2027.
  • Register for Self Assessment once you pass it. The side hustle tax rules explain when HMRC needs to know.
  • If you are a teacher, check your contract and your school's policy on private tuition, especially tutoring your own school's pupils.

The honest verdict

Online tutoring is one of the best side hustles in the UK. It clears the £12.71 National Living Wage from the first hour, with no equipment and no startup cost.

If you can teach GCSE maths or A Level sciences, you can earn more per hour than most part time jobs pay.

The mistake is staying on a high commission platform for years. It is the right way to start, because it solves your first students and reviews. It is a poor way to continue once you have both.

Plan one academic year. Platform in the autumn, referrals in the spring, and most of your hours direct by the summer exams. That shift is worth more than any rate rise you will ever negotiate.

Frequently asked questions

How much do online tutors make in the UK?

Published guides put GCSE at about £30 to £55 an hour and A Level at £35 to £70. Marketplace listings sit lower, around £20 to £25 online. What you take home depends on the platform: a £40 MyTutor lesson pays the tutor £22.48, while a direct student pays the full £40.

How much does MyTutor pay tutors?

MyTutor's own earnings table shows the tutor receiving £22.48 from a £40 lesson and £30 from a £50 lesson. From a £48 price upwards the tutor keeps 60%. On cheaper lessons the platform's share rises, to about 49% at £26, so low prices cost you twice.

Do I need to be a qualified teacher to tutor online?

No. Subject knowledge and the ability to explain it are what parents pay for. Qualified teacher status and examiner experience help you charge more, but neither is required. You do need a DBS check for most platforms, and many families will ask to see one before booking.

What should a beginner tutor charge in the UK?

Start a little below the published averages for your level, around £30 for GCSE and £35 to £40 for A Level, then raise it after three students and a few testimonials. Avoid starting at £15. It is very hard to climb from there, and it signals inexperience to parents.

Is online tutoring worth it as a side hustle?

Yes, for most people with a strong subject. Even at a platform's £22.48 a lesson, your real rate after preparation is about £17, above the £12.71 National Living Wage. Six to ten hours a week alongside a full time job is sustainable. Beyond that, tired tutoring loses students.
