Apprentice Wage 2026: £8 an Hour, No Student Debt, and the First Year Rule

Ruhul Amin · Published 26 August 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026 · 11 min read · Figures checked November 2026

The apprentice wage in 2026 is £8.00 an hour, rising to £8.31 in April 2027. If you are 19 or over, that low rate applies for your first year only. After that you must get the minimum wage for your age, which is £12.71 from age 21. So for an adult, an apprenticeship costs one lean year, pays for the training, and leaves no student debt.

The short version

  • The apprentice minimum wage is £8.00 an hour from April 2026, against a National Living Wage of £12.71 for workers aged 21 and over. From April 2027 the two rates are £8.31 and £13.21.
  • The age rule is the bit people miss. The apprentice rate applies if you are under 19, or 19 and over in the first year. After that first year, if you are 19 or over, you must be paid the normal rate for your age.
  • So for an adult career changer, the low pay lasts twelve months, not the whole apprenticeship. On a 37.5 hour week from April 2027, that year costs about £9,555 against staying on the National Living Wage.
  • There is no upper age limit in England. People aged 25 and over made up 52.4% of apprenticeship starts in the latest official figures.
  • One door has closed for adults. Since January 2026, master's level (Level 7) apprenticeships are government funded only for starters aged 16 to 21, with a few exceptions.
  • You pay nothing for the training and take on no student debt. You are an employee, with at least 20 days of paid holiday plus bank holidays.
  • Many employers pay above the minimum. The floor is not the offer, so read the advert.

1. What is an apprenticeship?

You are a paid employee doing a real job. A set part of your working week is spent training toward a recognised qualification.

Your employer and the government cover the training cost. You learn without paying tuition and without taking on debt. You come out with experience, a qualification, and often a permanent job with the same employer.

Three things follow that people underestimate:

  • The training happens in paid time, not on top of it. GOV.UK says at least 20% of your normal working hours go on training, and those hours are paid. If you are told to do the coursework at home in your evenings, that is a problem to raise, not the normal arrangement.
  • You are an employee. A contract, at least 20 days of paid holiday plus bank holidays, sick pay, and the same rights as anyone else in the building.
  • It has a defined length. GOV.UK gives the range as 8 months to 6 years, depending on the level. Older articles quote a 12 month minimum, which is out of date.

The system described here is England's. The minimum wage rates apply across the UK, but Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland run their own apprenticeship schemes.

2. What is the apprentice wage in 2026 and 2027?

Here are the minimum hourly rates now, and the rates the government has accepted for April 2027.

RateFrom April 2026From April 2027Who it applies to
National Living Wage£12.71£13.21Workers aged 21 and over
18 to 20 rate£10.85£11.27Workers aged 18 to 20
Under 18 rate£8.00£8.31Workers under 18
Apprentice rate£8.00£8.31See the rule below. This is the one that is misread

The apprentice rate does not apply to every apprentice for the whole apprenticeship. It applies if:

  • you are under 19, or
  • you are 19 or over and in the first year of your apprenticeship.

Once you are 19 or over and past your first year, you must be paid at least the normal minimum wage for your age.

For someone aged 21 or over, that is a step from £8.00 to £12.71 today. That is a rise of 59%, and it happens on the anniversary.

An adult who starts now will cross April 2027 in year one. Their floor moves from £8.00 to £8.31 in April, then to £13.21 at the anniversary. Our guide to the National Living Wage from April 2027 has the full set of new rates.

For an adult apprentice, the £8.00 is a twelve month toll, not the price of the whole journey. Almost every discussion of apprenticeship pay leaves that out, and it is the difference between a bad idea and a good one.

How much is that a month? At £8.00 an hour for 37.5 hours a week, the floor is £300 a week, or £1,300 a month before tax. At £8.31 it is about £1,350.

Do apprentices pay tax and National Insurance? Yes, on the same rules as any employee. You pay nothing on earnings inside the £12,570 personal allowance.

Two more things about the money. Employers pay no employer National Insurance for apprentices under 25 on earnings up to £50,270 a year. That is a real reason for them to take a younger apprentice on.

And many employers pay above these minimums. The legal floor is not the market rate, so do not assume the advertised salary will be £8.00 an hour.

3. What are the apprenticeship levels?

NameLevelRoughly equivalent toTypical use
Foundation and intermediate2GCSEsEntry into a trade or an operational role
Advanced3A levelsThe standard route into most skilled trades
Higher4 to 7Foundation degree and aboveTechnical and supervisory roles
Degree6 to 7Bachelor's or master's degreeProfessional entry: engineering, accountancy, nursing, software, management

That last row is the one worth pausing on. A degree apprenticeship gets you a full degree, awarded by a university, while employed and paid. There is no tuition fee and no student loan.

You finish with the degree, years of relevant work experience, and no tuition debt. A traditional graduate in England starts repaying with an average loan balance of £47,730, on the 2025 to 2026 official figures.

Set the two routes side by side on money, not on prestige. Our guide compares graduate scheme pay against apprenticeship pay, year by year.

The rule that changed for adults. Since January 2026 the government funds Level 7 apprenticeships, the master's level, only for people who start aged 16 to 21. Those aged 22 to 24 qualify if they have an education, health and care plan or are care experienced.

If you are 30 and hoped for a funded master's level apprenticeship, that route now depends on an employer paying the full cost itself. Level 6, the bachelor's level, is not affected.

The catch is competition. The deal is good, so expect a selective process. Apply early, apply to several, and treat each one as a job application, because that is what it is.

Not everyone finishes. The official achievement rate for 2024/25 was 65.4%, so roughly one apprentice in three did not complete. Ask the employer and the training provider how many of theirs do.

4. Can you do an apprenticeship at 30 or 40?

Yes. There is no upper age limit in England. GOV.UK says you need to be 16 or over. This is the most common misconception about apprenticeships, and it stops people who would benefit most.

The numbers back it up. In the Department for Education figures for August 2025 to April 2026, people aged 25 and over made up 161,670 of 308,770 starts. That is 52.4%.

Adults also no longer have to take a separate English and maths qualification as part of the apprenticeship, unless the employer chooses to include one. That applies to apprentices aged 19 and over.

For an adult changing career, the comparison is not against staying at school. It is against the other retraining routes, and it holds up well:

The row that kills most career change plans is the last one, and it is not the tuition fee. It is the lost salary, which is usually larger.

Our career change guide works through the payback sum properly. The conclusion is consistent: anything you can do while still being paid has a far shorter payback. An apprenticeship is the version of that with a qualification attached.

The honest counterweight for an adult: taking £8.00 an hour for twelve months is a real cut if you earn more now, and you need to be able to absorb it. That is the actual decision. It is a cash flow question, not a career one.

One smaller cost. For council tax, an apprentice is only disregarded if paid no more than £195 a week. At £8.00 for 30 hours you earn £240, so most apprentices still count as an adult in the household.

5. Worked example: the first year sum, and degree against apprenticeship

Dan, 34, on the National Living Wage in Derby

Dan works 37.5 hours a week on the National Living Wage. He is offered an engineering apprenticeship starting in April 2027 that pays only the legal floor.

If he stays put. £13.21 x 37.5 hours x 52 weeks = £25,759.50 for the year.

Year one as an apprentice. £8.31 x 37.5 x 52 = £16,204.50. The first year costs him £9,555 before tax.

Year two. He is over 19 and past his first year, so he must be paid at least the National Living Wage again. The cut is over, and he is now training for a better paid trade at no cost.

The same change through a full time course. One year at the £9,790 fee cap, with no salary, costs £9,790 plus £25,759.50 of lost pay. That is £35,549.50, nearly four times the apprenticeship route.

Two school leavers, same field

Route A, the traditional degree. Three years of full time study. Tuition fees of up to £9,790 a year on a student loan, living costs part funded by a maintenance loan, no salary. The graduate leaves with a degree, limited work experience, and a loan repaid out of income for years.

Route B, the degree apprenticeship. Employed from day one. Paid at least the apprentice rate for the first twelve months, then at least the minimum wage for their age. Tuition paid by the employer and the government. They finish with the same level of degree, years of relevant experience, references, and no tuition debt at all.

The gap is not really the debt. Route B has been paid the whole time and arrives with the experience that Route A now has to go and get.

Where Route A wins: if you do not know what you want to do. A degree keeps options open. An apprenticeship commits you to one employer and one field for several years. That is a real reason to choose university, and a better one than the debt comparison.

6. How do you find and apply for an apprenticeship?

  1. Search the official service. GOV.UK's Find an Apprenticeship lists vacancies by field, level and area. Employers are not required to advertise there, so check company careers pages too.
  2. Apply early and apply widely. Degree and higher apprenticeships in popular fields close quickly.
  3. Treat it as a job application, because it is. A tailored CV and a specific cover letter matter as much here as anywhere.
  4. Be specific about why that trade and that employer. Employers are committing years of training to you. Commitment is what they screen for, more than qualifications.
  5. Prepare properly for the interview. Our interview guide applies directly. Know the employer before you walk in.
  6. Ask your current employer. Many organisations will fund an apprenticeship for existing staff. You keep your job and usually your pay. This is the cheapest, fastest version of the whole plan, and almost nobody asks.

It helps to know what is in it for the employer. The government pays the full training cost for apprentices aged 16 to 24 at smaller employers that do not pay the levy. For older apprentices those employers pay 5%.

Our guide to what small employers pay toward an apprenticeship is useful reading before you make the case to your boss.

7. Who should not do an apprenticeship?

  • If you cannot absorb twelve months on the apprentice rate and the employer will not pay above it. Check the advertised salary before you get invested. Many pay well above the floor and some do not.
  • If you do not know what field you want. An apprenticeship is a commitment to one path. Doubt is a good reason to choose a broader route.
  • If your existing employer would fund a qualification anyway. Ask first. It is usually faster and you keep your salary.
  • If a Skills Bootcamp would get you to the same place. They last up to 16 weeks, are open to people aged 19 or over, and you keep your current job while doing one.
  • If you are 22 or over and want a master's level qualification. Level 7 funding now stops at 21 for most people.
  • If you want the university experience itself. That is a fair thing to want, and an apprenticeship does not provide it. Be honest with yourself about it.

The honest verdict

An apprenticeship is one of the best routes into skilled work in the UK, and the reason is not the wage. You are paid something while you train, you pay nothing for the training, and you arrive at the end with experience, not only a certificate.

The price is twelve months at £8.00 an hour, or £8.31 from April 2027, if your employer only pays the floor. After that you are legally back on the rate for your age.

So treat the low rate as a first year toll, not a permanent condition. Check what the specific employer pays. And ask your current employer whether they would fund one before you look elsewhere.

Ignore anyone who tells you it is for school leavers. More than half of new apprentices are 25 or over. The payback sum is often better at 35 than at 18, because you are comparing it with a full time course you would have had to fund yourself.

Frequently asked

How much do apprentices get paid a month? At the legal minimum of £8.00 an hour on a 37.5 hour week, about £1,300 a month before tax. From April 2027 the rate is £8.31, about £1,350. If you are 19 or over, your pay must rise to your age rate after the first year. Many employers pay more from the start.

Am I too old for an apprenticeship? No. There is no upper age limit in England, and people aged 25 and over made up 52.4% of starts in the latest figures. The one age rule to know is that government funding for Level 7, the master's level, is now limited to starters aged 16 to 21.

Do apprentices pay tax and National Insurance? Yes, in the same way as any employee, through the payslip. You pay no income tax on earnings within the £12,570 personal allowance. A full time first year at the apprentice rate comes to about £16,200 from April 2027, so some tax is due on the part above the allowance.

Can I do an apprenticeship if I already have a degree? Yes. GOV.UK says you can have a previous qualification, including a degree. The funding rules need the apprenticeship to give you substantial new skills, with content that is materially different from what you already hold. A different field usually passes that test.

Do I get holiday and sick pay, and a job at the end? You are an employee, so you get at least 20 days of paid holiday plus bank holidays, and sick pay. A job at the end is common but not guaranteed. Ask what share of their apprentices the employer kept on. A vague answer is an answer.
