1. What should you do before you spend anything?

Is a smart thermostat worth it? Only after you have made the free changes. They often save more than the gadget.

The Energy Saving Trust is blunt. Turning the thermostat down by one degree, from 22°C to 21°C, could save about £120 a year in Great Britain. It costs nothing and needs no app.

It says most people are comfortable somewhere between 18°C and 21°C. Set the lowest temperature that feels fine for your household.

Three more free changes come next. Match the heating schedule to when you are really home. Turn radiator valves down in rooms you rarely use. Check the hot water is not heating all day.

One more dial is worth a look. Lowering the boiler flow temperature can cut gas use without touching comfort, as our flow temperature guide explains.

Do these first. Then you judge any gadget against a sensible baseline, not against years of waste.

A smart thermostat does not save energy. It makes it easier to do things that save energy. If you were already doing them, it saves you very little.

2. Do smart thermostats really save money?

Yes, often, but rarely as much as the box says. The real saving depends on how you control your heating today.

The Energy Saving Trust says installing and using heating controls well could save about £100 a year in Great Britain. It adds that whether smart controls save you money depends on how you control your heating now.

Manufacturers aim higher. A Which? investigation looked at the claims made by three big brands.

  • Tado claimed savings of "up to 31%". Which? found this added together two separate study results, 24% from turning heating off when you are out and 7% from weather based control.
  • Hive claimed "up to £110" a year. That figure assumed gas use of 16,489 kWh a year, far above a typical home.
  • Google said UK Nest users saved 8.4% to 16.5%. Which? found that assumed heating ran at least 17 hours a day.

Which? published those findings in December 2021, and brands may have changed their wording since. The lesson still holds: check the assumption behind any saving claim.

Compare that with Ofgem's own typical figure. Since 1 July 2026 Ofgem has used 9,500 kWh of gas a year as typical use for price cap bills.

Why the gap? Because a headline figure compares a smart system with a badly managed home. If your heating runs on a timer you set years ago, you will do well. If you already turn things down and off, there is little left to find.

3. How long does a smart thermostat take to pay for itself?

Start with the price. In November 2026 Which? listed the Habi Wireless at £62, the Tado Smart Thermostat X at £131 and the Google Nest Learning Thermostat at £219.

Wired models usually need an installer. Which? recommends professional installation for many of them, so get a quote before you count the payback.

That is still a good return next to most things you buy for the home. It is simply not the miracle the packaging suggests.

Two things push payback the right way: a larger house, and irregular hours where an empty home gets heated today. Two push it the wrong way: a small, well insulated flat, and a household that is already careful.

Energy prices move the sums too. From 1 October to 31 December 2026, the Ofgem price cap puts gas at 7.97p per kWh with a 29.68p daily standing charge, by Direct Debit. Our October 2026 price cap guide has the detail.

4. Are smart radiator valves worth it?

A thermostat measures one room and controls the whole house. If the hallway is warm enough, the spare bedroom and the empty dining room are still heated, all winter.

Smart thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs) fix that. Each one controls a single radiator, so you heat only the rooms you use.

The catch is cost. Hive's own shop listed its smart radiator valve at £59 each on 23 November 2026. Valves usually need to match your thermostat's brand.

Ordinary TRVs are much cheaper. The Energy Saving Trust puts the cost of adding standard TRVs to every radiator at about £370, if you already have a programmer and thermostat.

The sensible path is to fit smart valves only where the waste is. That is usually a spare bedroom, a dining room or a hallway. Three valves where they matter beat eight valves everywhere.

One warning from the Energy Saving Trust: it generally does not recommend radiator covers, because they can stop a TRV sensing the room properly. Our radiator covers guide explains why.

Worked example: the Ahmed family, three bed semi

The Ahmeds' meter shows about 12,000 kWh of gas a year. At the October to December 2026 cap rates, that is 12,000 × 7.97p = £956.40, plus 365 × 29.68p = £108.33 standing charge. Call it £1,065 a year.

Both adults work out of the house three days a week. The heating runs on a fixed timer whatever happens, at 22°C. Two rooms are rarely used.

Step one, free: thermostat down from 22°C to 21°C, and the timer matched to the days they are home. The Energy Saving Trust puts the one degree alone at about £120 a year. Cost: nothing.

Step two, £131: a wireless Tado Smart Thermostat X, which Which? lists as a DIY fit. If it reaches the Energy Saving Trust figure of about £100 a year, it pays back in about 16 months. At half that, about two years seven months.

Step three, about £177: three smart valves for the two empty rooms and the hallway, using Hive's £59 price as a guide. They must match the thermostat brand, so the Ahmeds check the Tado price first. There is no reliable independent figure for the saving, so they treat this step as optional.

The lesson: the free step was worth more than either purchase. The hardware earns its place, but only after the habit change, and only where there is real waste to remove.

5. What smart home kit is genuinely worth buying?

  1. A smart thermostat, if your current control is a basic timer and your week is irregular. This is the one with a real payback.
  2. Smart TRVs in underused rooms only. Targeted, not every room, unless money is no object.
  3. A smart plug with energy monitoring. It saves little by itself, but it shows what is really costing you, which beats a guess.
  4. Draught proofing. Not smart and not exciting, but often the best return in the house. Covered in our energy bills guide.

6. Which smart gadgets will you stop using?

Colour changing bulbs everywhere. Fun for a fortnight, then you set them to white and forget the app. Standard LED bulbs already cut lighting costs. The smart part adds cost, not saving.

Voice assistants as an energy tool. Handy, but asking a speaker to turn the heating down saves the same as walking to the dial.

Smart kettles and smart fridges. The appliance uses the same electricity either way. You are paying for an app.

Whole house sensor kits bought before insulation. Measuring a draughty house very precisely does not make it warmer.

Subscriptions you did not plan for. Which? notes some Tado features, such as weather response and location tracking, need a subscription. Add that to the payback sum.

7. Who should not bother?

If you rent. Ask your landlord before changing anything mains wired. Smart TRVs and plugs can usually move with you, a thermostat generally cannot. Our renter's energy saving kit lists what travels.

If you live in a small, well insulated flat. There is often too little heating cost to save on. Payback can stretch past the life of the gadget.

If you are already careful. If your heating runs to a sensible schedule at 18 to 19°C, a smart thermostat buys convenience, not savings. That is a fine reason, just be honest that it is the reason.

If your home is draughty or poorly insulated. Fix that first. Precise control of heat that escapes through the loft is expensive precision.

8. The honest verdict

A smart thermostat is worth it for a specific household: a medium or large home, an irregular week, basic controls today, and rooms that go unused.

There, a smart thermostat and a few targeted valves can pay back in one to three years and keep paying.

It is not worth it as a first move. The free one degree change and a sensible schedule will save more than the thermostat, and cost nothing.

Buy the control, not the gadget. Then ignore every advert that promises 31%.

Frequently asked questions

Is a smart thermostat worth it in the UK?

For many homes, yes, but mainly where heating is badly controlled today. The Energy Saving Trust puts the saving from using heating controls well at about £100 a year in Great Britain. With units from about £62 to £219, payback runs from about seven months to four and a half years. Careful households will see less.

Do smart thermostats work with any boiler?

Most modern combi and system boilers work with them, but compatibility varies, especially with older or heat only systems and hot water cylinders. Check the maker's compatibility tool before you buy. Which? recommends professional installation for many wired models, so add any installer's fee to the payback sum.

What temperature should I set my thermostat to?

The Energy Saving Trust suggests the lowest comfortable temperature, which for most people is 18°C to 21°C. Turning it down from 22°C to 21°C could save about £120 a year in Great Britain. Keep it warmer if someone at home is older, unwell or at home all day.

Do smart thermostats need a subscription?

Most work without one, but some features can cost extra. Which? notes that on Tado's Smart Thermostat X, weather response, location tracking and learning features need a subscription. Check what is included before buying, and add any yearly fee to your payback sum, or the saving can vanish.

Are smart radiator valves better than a smart thermostat?

They do a different job. A thermostat controls when the whole house heats, and valves control each room. Valves help most where rooms sit empty. Hive listed its valve at £59 each in November 2026, so fit them only in the rooms where you waste the most heat.
