Dividend Tax UK: What You Owe Above The £500 Allowance

Ruhul Amin · Published 13 November 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked November 2026

The first £500 of dividends a year is tax free, and above that a basic rate taxpayer pays 10.75% in 2026 to 2027. The rate jumps to 35.75% if your dividends land in the higher band, so where they sit matters more than how much you get.

The short version

  • The first £500 of dividends a year is tax free. This is the dividend allowance, and it is £500 in both 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028.
  • Above that, the rates for 2026 to 2027 are 10.75% (basic), 35.75% (higher) and 39.35% (additional). The Budget on 28 October 2026 left them unchanged for 2027 to 2028.
  • Dividends fall inside your Personal Allowance first. If your other income does not use up the £12,570, dividends can use the rest of it before any tax applies.
  • £5,000 of dividends costs a basic rate taxpayer £483.75 and £10,000 costs £1,021.25 (our own arithmetic, shown below).
  • Dividends inside an ISA are outside all of this. The ISA limit is £20,000 a year in both tax years, and you do not declare ISA income.
  • Over £10,000 of dividends means Self Assessment. Below that you can file a return or ask HMRC to change your tax code, by 5 October after the tax year ends.

1. The allowance and the three rates

Dividends are the payments a company makes to its shareholders. You might get them from shares you hold outside an ISA, from a share dealing account, or from your own limited company.

The first £500 you receive in a tax year is covered by the dividend allowance. You pay nothing on it. Only the part above £500 is taxed.

The rate depends on your Income Tax band. GOV.UK gives these rates for 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027:

Income Tax bandDividend tax rateTaxable income, England
Basic rate10.75%Up to £50,270
Higher rate35.75%£50,271 to £125,140
Additional rate39.35%Over £125,140

These are rates on dividends only. Your wages and pension are still taxed at 20%, 40% and 45%. The band limits are the ones GOV.UK publishes for England. Scottish bands differ for earnings, and our guide to Scottish Income Tax covers that.

The Budget on 28 October 2026 did not move any of this. HMRC's rates and allowances annex lists the same three rates and the same £500 allowance for 2027 to 2028. What does rise from April 2027 is the tax on savings interest, to 24%, 44% and 49%. That is a separate change, and our savings interest tax guide covers it.

If you have been following the Budget coverage, the full list of what is already law is in our Budget 2026 guide.

2. How dividends stack on top of your other income

This is the part most explainers skip. HMRC adds your dividends to your other income to decide which band they sit in. Dividends are treated as the top slice of your income.

The order works like this, following GOV.UK's own method:

  1. Add your dividends to your wages, pension and other income.
  2. Take off your Personal Allowance of £12,570. Any dividends that fall inside it are not taxed.
  3. Of the dividends above that, the first £500 is covered by the dividend allowance.
  4. The rest is taxed at the dividend rate for the band it lands in.

Two consequences follow. A person with little other income can receive a lot of dividends tax free, because the unused Personal Allowance does the work. And a person whose wages already fill the basic rate band pays 35.75% on every pound above the allowance, because their dividends land straight in the higher band.

The dividend allowance still takes up room in the band. It is taxed at 0%, but it counts towards where your income sits. That matters if you are close to the £50,270 line. Our guide to frozen tax thresholds explains why more people are close to it each year.

GOV.UK shows the method with a worked example. A person with £29,570 of wages and £3,000 of dividends has £20,000 of taxable income after the Personal Allowance. They pay 20% on £17,000 of wages, nothing on £500 of dividends, and 10.75% on £2,500 of dividends. That last part comes to £268.75, our own arithmetic from GOV.UK's figures.

3. What each level of dividends costs

The table below shows the dividend tax for a basic rate taxpayer whose wages already use the whole Personal Allowance, and whose total income stays below £50,270. We assume wages of £28,000. Every figure is our own arithmetic: the dividend minus £500, multiplied by 10.75%.

Dividends in the yearTaxed at 10.75%Dividend tax
£500£0£0
£1,000£500£53.75
£2,000£1,500£161.25
£5,000£4,500£483.75
£10,000£9,500£1,021.25
£20,000£19,500£2,096.25

The effective rate stays under 10.75% at every level, because the first £500 is free. On £5,000 of dividends you pay about 9.7%.

What does the allowance protect in terms of shares? That depends on the yield, which is the dividend as a share of the price. At an illustrative 4% yield, £500 of dividends comes from £12,500 of shares. At the same yield, £10,000 of dividends needs £250,000 of shares. The 4% is our own assumption and is not a claim about any real company. Yields move.

So for most ordinary investors, dividend tax is a small number. It becomes a real one for people who run their own limited company and pay themselves mainly in dividends. We cover that choice in our guide to a limited company for affiliate income and sole trader versus limited company.

4. Worked example: two households

Priya in Leeds, and Tom in Reading

Priya earns £28,000 as a school administrator. She also holds shares outside an ISA that paid her £6,000 in dividends this year.

Her total income is £34,000. After the £12,570 Personal Allowance, £21,430 is taxable, all in the basic rate band. Her wages use the whole Personal Allowance, so every pound of dividend is taxable except the first £500.

The taxable dividends are £5,500. At 10.75% the tax is £591.25. On top of that she pays the usual 20% on her wages above the Personal Allowance.

Tom earns £48,000 and received £4,000 in dividends. His total income is £52,000, so £39,430 is taxable after the Personal Allowance. The basic rate band is £37,700 wide.

His wages take up £35,430 of that band, leaving £2,270. So £2,270 of his dividends sit in the basic band and £1,730 spill into the higher band.

Basic band part: £2,270 less the £500 allowance leaves £1,770, taxed at 10.75%, which is £190.28. Higher band part: £1,730 at 35.75%, which is £618.48. His dividend tax is £808.75 in total, and every figure here is our own arithmetic.

Tom pays 20.2% on his dividends. Priya pays 9.9% on hers. The difference comes entirely from where the dividends land in the bands.

If Tom had held the same shares in a stocks and shares ISA, his dividend tax on them would have been nil. The ISA limit is £20,000 a year, and you do not need to declare ISA income on a tax return.

The fix for Tom is not clever. It is to use the ISA allowance each year so that new money goes in sheltered. Moving existing shares into an ISA means selling them and rebuying, and that can trigger Capital Gains Tax on any gain, so check that first. The cash ISA changes for 2027 affect cash, not shares, and the overall £20,000 limit is unchanged.

5. When you have to tell HMRC

The rule on GOV.UK is that you must tell HMRC each year if you receive dividends you have tax to pay on. If your dividends are within the £500 allowance, you do not need to tell them.

Above that, it depends on the size:

Your dividendsWhat GOV.UK says to do
Within the £500 allowanceYou do not need to tell HMRC
Over £500, up to £10,000Report it on a Self Assessment return, or tell HMRC after 5 April and before 5 October and ask for your tax code to be updated
Over £10,000You must file a Self Assessment return

If you do not usually file a return and you need to, you must register by 5 October after the end of the tax year. For the 2025 to 2026 tax year, that date was 5 October 2026. The online return and the payment for that year are both due by 11:59pm on 31 January 2027.

The figures in this guide are for 2026 to 2027. A return due in January 2027 covers the earlier 2025 to 2026 tax year, so it uses that year's own rates and allowances. Check GOV.UK for that year before you file.

For the January deadline itself, including payments on account, see what is due on 31 January 2027. If you missed the October registration date, our guide to registering for Self Assessment covers what to do next. Other income can also trigger a return, as set out in our side hustle tax guide.

6. The honest verdict

For most people with a modest share portfolio, dividend tax is small and easy to avoid. Hold shares in an ISA first, and the problem mostly disappears. Use your £20,000 allowance before you buy anything outside it.

Outside an ISA, the first £500 a year is free, and the rest costs 10.75% if your other income leaves you in the basic band. It costs 35.75% if it does not. Work out which band your dividends land in before you decide how much to hold outside an ISA.

If you take dividends from your own company, the rates and the £10,000 reporting trigger matter more, and an accountant is worth the fee. This guide is general information, not tax advice.

Frequently asked

How much dividend income is tax free in the UK? The first £500 a year is covered by the dividend allowance. Dividends that fall inside your unused Personal Allowance of £12,570 are also untaxed. Dividends in an ISA are outside Income Tax altogether. The allowance is £500 in both 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028.

What is the dividend tax rate in 2026 to 2027? The rates are 10.75% for basic rate taxpayers, 35.75% for higher rate and 39.35% for additional rate. They apply only to dividends above the £500 allowance. HMRC's annex to Budget 2026 shows the same rates for 2027 to 2028, so no further rise is announced.

Do I have to declare dividends from shares in an ISA? No. GOV.UK says that if you complete a tax return, you do not need to declare any ISA interest, income or capital gains on it. The overall ISA limit is £20,000 a year for both 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028.

How do I pay tax on dividends if I do not file a tax return? If your dividends are over £500 and up to £10,000, tell HMRC after 5 April and before 5 October. You can ask them to update your tax code so the tax comes out of your wages or pension. Over £10,000 you must file a return.
