Fuel duty rise September 2026: it was scrapped, and the next 5p rise is now due on 1 July 2027

  • The January 2027 rise has been scrapped too. The Budget on 28 October 2026 extended the cut again, to 1 July 2027, and deferred the RPI increase to the same date.

1. What happened to the fuel duty rise in September 2026?

It was cancelled before it arrived. When this guide was first written, HMRC's rates table still showed a 1p rise on 1 September 2026. That table, published on 26 February 2026, had been overtaken.

On 20 May 2026 the Chancellor announced that the 5p cut would run for the rest of the year. HMRC published amended rates on 22 May 2026. The Budget costing note confirms the extension took effect on 1 September 2026.

Under that May plan the main rate for petrol and diesel was set to move like this:

To 31 December 2026: 52.95p a litre.

From 1 January 2027: 55.95p a litre, a 3p step.

From 1 March 2027: 57.95p a litre, the rate that applied before 23 March 2022.

The government's official weekly price series records the duty rate alongside the pump price. It shows 52.95p in every week from August to the week of 2 November 2026. Petrol and diesel duty did not move in September.

Red diesel went the other way. From 15 June 2026 the rate for marked gas oil was cut from 10.18p to 6.48p a litre, a further 3.7p on top of the existing cut.

2. What did the 28 October 2026 Budget do to fuel duty?

It scrapped the January step and pushed the whole return back six months. The Budget document says the government is cancelling plans to add 5p back onto fuel duty from January 2027 and extending the cut to 1 July 2027.

It also deferred the planned RPI increase to the same date. Our guide to what the 28 October Budget actually did covers the rest of the package.

The Budget policy costings set out what happens next:

Until 1 July 2027: 52.95p a litre for petrol and diesel, unchanged.

On 1 July 2027: rates rise by 5p a litre and are uprated in line with RPI, in one go.

From 1 April 2028: rates are due to rise with RPI every year.

The extra 3.7p red diesel cut was extended to 1 July 2027 as well. The Treasury puts the cost of the main fuel duty extension at £575m in 2026/27 and £780m in 2027/28.

The Budget says fuel duty is now as low as at any point in over 16 years. It also says the government's fuel duty decisions in this Parliament will have saved the average car driver £230 compared with the previous government's plans.

A note of caution on 1 July 2027. Duty has not gone up since 2011. The cut has been extended six times since 2022. Plan for the rise, but do not be surprised if it moves again.

3. Why is a 5p fuel duty rise really 6p at the pump?

Fuel duty is a fixed amount per litre. VAT at 20% is then charged on the whole pump price, including the duty. So every penny of duty becomes 1.2p at the pump.

Duty is due to rise 5p in July 2027. Your pump price rises 6p, because VAT is charged on the duty.

That matters for the tank figure you will see quoted. A 55 litre fill multiplied by 5p gives £2.75. Multiply by 6p and you get £3.30. The RPI uprating on the same day will add a little more, and nobody knows that figure yet.

Here is where the money goes today. In the week commencing 2 November 2026 the government's weekly series recorded average UK pump prices of 172.78p for petrol and 198.58p for diesel. Break the petrol price down:

Petrol at 172.78p a litre: duty 52.95p, VAT 28.80p, everything else (crude, refining, delivery, retailer margin) 91.03p.

Tax is 81.75p, or 47.3% of what you hand over.

There is a simple test for when tax passes half the pump price. It happens whenever the pump price is below three times the duty rate. At 52.95p duty that line is 158.85p, and petrol is well above it today.

Diesel, at 198.58p, carries tax of 43.3%. Diesel passed £2 a litre in the week of 12 October 2026, at 200.04p, which the Budget itself mentioned.

4. Worked example: the Ahmed household in Stevenage

Farhan Ahmed drives 9,000 miles a year in a petrol car averaging 42mpg. That is 974 litres a year, or about £1,683 at today's 172.78p.

Under the February plan he would have paid about £41 more over September 2026 to August 2027, as the three steps landed. Here is what he actually pays in extra duty now:

September 2026 to June 2027 at no extra duty: 812 litres, £0.

July and August 2027 at plus 6p: 162 litres, £9.74, plus the RPI uprating.

From July 2027 a full year at plus 6p: £58.44, before RPI.

So the delay saves a typical commuter around £31 over this twelve months. The steady state cost, once the 5p is back, is the same £58 a year it always was, plus inflation.

The bigger story for Farhan is not tax. With duty unchanged, the average petrol price rose about 11p a litre between late August and early November. On 974 litres that is roughly £108 a year, nearly double the whole duty rise.

If he drives for work in his own car, it is worth checking what the HMRC mileage rate now pays back per mile. If he is weighing up his next car, the electric car versus petrol cost per mile is the comparison to run.

5. Is the gap between forecourts bigger than the tax rise?

Usually, yes. This is the part almost nobody puts next to the duty story.

The Competition and Markets Authority's August 2026 report found the average retailer margin was 11.2p a litre in June 2026, above the 2025 average of 10.7p. Supermarkets averaged 10.4p. Other retailers averaged 12.0p. The whole duty return in July 2027 is 6p at the pump.

The CMA also found that non supermarket retailers are consistently more expensive than supermarkets. It does not give one fixed gap, so use your own local prices. As an illustration only, a 4p a litre saving across Farhan's 974 litres is £38.96 a year. That is two thirds of the full duty rise, from one choice of forecourt.

Since 2 February 2026 there has been a legal way to see that gap. Under the Fuel Finder scheme every station selling petrol or diesel must register and report any price change within 30 minutes. The CMA held off enforcement until May 2026 to let retailers adjust.

The CMA estimates about 97% of UK petrol stations are now registered, covering around 99% of fuel sold. That is why the free price apps now show live forecourt prices rather than guesses. It costs nothing to check before you drive.

6. Why will the pump price not change at midnight on 1 July 2027?

Fuel duty is charged at the duty point. That is when fuel leaves the refinery or bonded storage terminal, not when it leaves the pump. Every litre already in a forecourt's underground tanks at midnight was duty paid at the old rate.

A busy site turns its stock over in a few days. A quiet rural one can take a fortnight or more. So the 6p filters through over one to two weeks rather than appearing everywhere at once.

That is not a conspiracy, it is just how the tax works. A forecourt that raises its price on 1 July is raising it on fuel bought at the old rate. A site that has not moved by the weekend is not doing you a favour, it simply has not taken a delivery yet.

The practical version: filling up the day before saves about £3.30 on a 55 litre tank. Worth doing if you were going to fill up that week anyway. Not worth a special journey.

The honest verdict

The rise this article was written about did not happen, and the next one has been pushed to 1 July 2027. For a typical petrol commuter that is around £31 saved over the coming year. It is real money, and it is small next to what wholesale prices have done since August.

Two things are worth carrying away. First, quote the pump number, not the duty number: 5p of duty is 6p at the pump, plus RPI on the day. Second, the gap between forecourts is now published in near real time, and checking it takes about fifteen seconds on a phone.

Watch the Spring Statement and any update before July 2027. This cut has been extended six times since 2022. Plan for the 6p rise, and check again before you rely on it.

Frequently asked

Did fuel duty go up in September 2026? No. The 1p rise planned for 1 September 2026 was cancelled on 20 May 2026, when the 5p cut was extended to 31 December 2026. HMRC's published rate stayed at 52.95p a litre for petrol and diesel. The government's weekly pump price series shows the same 52.95p duty rate every week since.

When will fuel duty go up next? On 1 July 2027, under the plan set in the 28 October 2026 Budget. Duty is due to rise by 5p a litre and be uprated by RPI on that day. From 1 April 2028 it is due to rise with RPI every year. The government says it will keep fuel prices under review.

Is diesel rising by more than petrol? No. The duty rate and the planned rise are identical for both, at 52.95p now. Diesel costs more at the pump because of refining costs and demand, not tax. In fact diesel carries a lower share of tax than petrol, 43.3% against 47.3%, because the untaxed part of its price is bigger.

Does this affect red diesel or heating oil? Red diesel, or marked gas oil, fell to 6.48p a litre from 15 June 2026, and the Budget kept that extra 3.7p cut until 1 July 2027. Domestic heating kerosene sits outside the road fuel rates. Kerosene users have a bigger bill to plan, as replacing an oil tank carries its own rules and costs.

Why are pump prices higher if duty did not rise? Duty is only one part of the price. Between the weeks of 24 August and 2 November 2026, average petrol rose 11.15p and diesel 15.76p a litre, with duty unchanged. The CMA links the 2026 rises to the conflict in the Middle East pushing up wholesale costs, especially for diesel.
