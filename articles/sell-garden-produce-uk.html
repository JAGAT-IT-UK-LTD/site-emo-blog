Selling Garden Produce From Home in the UK: The Rules and What It Earns

Ruhul Amin · Published 26 August 2026 · Updated 25 November 2026 · 10 min read · Figures checked November 2026

Selling garden produce from home is legal in the UK, and for loose veg and fruit the rules are light. They tighten at two points. Eggs mean registering with the council and, since October 2024, registering every bird you keep. Anything you cook or bottle makes you a food business. Our worked year for one plot and six hens earns about £436, so do it for the glut, not the income.

The short version

  • One distinction decides everything: primary products straight from the ground or the bird, against processed products you have made. Cooking, bottling or preserving moves you across the line.
  • Veg and fruit are the easy end. A gate stall with an honesty box is normal practice. Regular selling still means registering as a food business, which is free.
  • Allotments are for surplus only. The Allotments Act 1922 defines an allotment garden as one cultivated mainly to feed you and your family.
  • Eggs have their own rules. Under 50 birds you can sell ungraded eggs direct to consumers. Best before is no more than 28 days from laying.
  • Every bird keeper must register, even with one hen. That has applied in England and Wales since 1 October 2024. And a bird flu housing order is in force in November 2026 for anyone who sells or gives away eggs.
  • Jam, chutney and cakes make you a food business. Register with your council at least 28 days before trading. It is free. Allergen labelling matters most.
  • The trading allowance covers £1,000 of gross sales, rising to £3,000 from 6 April 2027. Most gate sellers never need to tell HMRC anything.

1. Is it legal to sell garden produce from home?

Yes. What you have to do depends on what you sell.

UK food rules split what you grow into two groups. Almost every question people ask is really a question about which side of this line they are on.

A word on registration. It is free, it is done with your local council, and it cannot be refused. It is not a licence.

Official guidance says a council may decide registration is not needed where the food activity is very limited and infrequent. A few bags of beans in August can fall there. A stall that is out every week does not. If in doubt, ring the council.

Selling a courgette is one thing. Turning it into chutney and putting a lid on it is a different legal activity, and the change happens the moment the pan goes on.

Know which group you are in before you do anything else. Most problems start with someone who thought they were still in the first column.

2. Can you sell vegetables and plants from your garden or allotment?

From your garden, yes. This is the easy end. A table at the gate with a few boxes of runner beans and an honesty box is normal practice across the country.

Keep it sensible. Sell it clean and fresh, and do not make health claims.

Three small rules apply even to loose veg. Tell buyers the name of the food. If you sell by weight, use metric. And "organic" is a legally protected term that needs certification, so do not use it unless you are certified.

Allotments are different. In England and Wales the Allotments Act 1922 defines an allotment garden as one cultivated wholly or mainly to feed the plot holder and their family.

So selling a true surplus is one thing. Running the plot as a business is not allowed. Your tenancy agreement may be stricter still, and some councils forbid any sales. Read it first.

Plants and seedlings. You do not need a plant passport for in person sales to the public, such as a gate table or a plant stall. You do need one for online or distance sales, even to private buyers. That makes posting plants a different job from selling them at the gate.

The money. It is pocket money, not a business. In our worked year below the summer glut brings in £210. The real return is that you stop buying vegetables yourself.

3. Do you need a licence to sell eggs?

No licence, but there are three registrations and rules to know. People get caught out here more than anywhere else.

  • Small flock, under 50 birds. You can sell ungraded, unstamped eggs direct to consumers. That covers the gate, door to door and a local public market. You cannot supply shops or cafes this way.
  • Register as a food business with your council. It is free. Trading standards guidance says egg producers selling direct should register.
  • Register your birds. Since 1 October 2024, everyone who keeps poultry in England and Wales must register within one month, even with a single hen. Scotland has its own kept bird register. Older advice about a 50 bird threshold is out of date.
  • Two dates matter. The best before date must be no more than 28 days from laying. Retailers must sell eggs within 21 days of laying, which is a sensible limit for a gate stall too.
  • At a market, display your name and address, the best before date, and advice to keep the eggs chilled after purchase.
  • Sell only clean, uncracked eggs.

Bird flu, November 2026. A housing order is in force in England and Wales. If you keep fewer than 50 birds for your own use only, you do not have to house them.

If you sell or give away their eggs, you must house them. That catches every gate egg seller, so check the current position on GOV.UK before you put a box out. Eggs can keep a free range label for 16 weeks from the date the birds were housed.

Hens on an allotment. The Allotments Act 1950 lets a plot holder keep hens, but not by way of trade or business. Hens kept to sell eggs belong in your own garden.

The money. Feed is the cost that decides it. In our worked year six hens bring in £144 of egg sales against about £110 of feed. This is a hobby that partly pays for itself, and it drops in winter when laying slows.

4. What are the rules for selling homemade jam?

Here the bar rises. This is where the real duties start.

  1. Register as a food business with your local council. It is free, and it must be done at least 28 days before you start trading. It is a legal requirement.
  2. Label properly. A prepacked jar needs the name of the food, the ingredients, allergens emphasised in that list, a date mark, the weight, and your business name and address. Getting allergens wrong can harm someone.
  3. Use the right name. "Jam" and "marmalade" are reserved descriptions, with minimum fruit and sugar levels set in the Jam and Similar Products Regulations. Honey has its own reserved descriptions and must show its country of origin.
  4. Your kitchen can usually be your home kitchen. The council may inspect it. They would sooner help you get it right than shut you down, so ring them before you start, not after.
  5. Get adequate training. A food hygiene certificate is not compulsory, but you must be trained well enough for what you do.
  6. Keep records. Batch dates, ingredients used, and where they came from. If something goes wrong, this is what limits the problem to one batch.

Giving it away does not always get you out of this. Official guidance treats regular supply of food to the public as a food business even when it is free. A jar for a neighbour is not regular supply. A weekly table of free jam is.

The money. It becomes real only at volume, which means market stalls, and stall fees change the sums quickly.

Say you sell twelve jars at £5, the jars and ingredients cost you £1.50 each, and the pitch costs £25. You take £60, spend £43, and have made £17 for a day of your life plus the cooking. Ask the organiser for the pitch fee before you cook anything.

6. A worked year, with the numbers

Asha in Shropshire: one allotment plot, and six hens in the back garden

The prices and yields here are an example to show the sums. Yours will differ.

Spring. She sells surplus seedlings, tomato and courgette plants raised from seed, at £1 each on a gate table. £85 from seed costing about £8, so £77 net. The best margin of the year, and it happens in April before a single vegetable exists.

Summer. The glut. Beans, courgettes and tomatoes on the gate table with an honesty box. £210 in the box across the season. Perhaps £15 more went unpaid, because that is what an honesty box costs you.

Eggs, year round. Six hens at home, registered as a keeper and as a food business. About four dozen a month sold at £3 a dozen. £144 a year against about £110 of feed. Net £34, and the hens were going to be there anyway.

Autumn preserves. Registered 28 days ahead and labelled properly. 40 jars at £5 is £200. Ingredients and jars cost about £60, and one market pitch £25. £115 net for roughly two full days of cooking, labelling and standing behind a table.

Year total: £436 net, on gross sales of £639. Against perhaps 200 hours across the year of growing, picking, cooking and selling. That is a little over £2 an hour.

The lesson in the numbers. The seedlings took the least time and made the best return per hour. The preserves took the most time, needed the most compliance, and made £115. Value added beats volume, and early beats late.

7. Do you pay tax on selling garden produce?

Usually not, at this scale. Money from selling produce counts as trading income.

The first £1,000 of total sales in a tax year is covered by the trading allowance. So most gate sellers never need to tell HMRC anything.

Past £1,000 in gross sales, not profit, you need to register for Self Assessment. In the worked year above, gross sales were £639, comfortably inside. Add a few more market days and it would not be.

The limit is rising. The Budget of 28 October 2026 announced a trading allowance of £3,000 from 6 April 2027. It still has to pass into law. Our guide to the £3,000 trading allowance explains it.

The allowance covers all your side income added together, not each activity. The full explanation, including the gross against profit trap, is in our side hustle tax rules guide.

Is it worth doing?

As an income, no. Our worked year earns a little over £2 an hour, far below the £12.71 benchmark in our guide to side hustles that beat minimum wage.

As a way to stop wasting a glut, cover your seed and feed costs, and meet your neighbours, it is one of the better things you can do with a garden.

If you want the growing to pay, the money is in the next step up. Seedlings and plants in spring sell far better than vegetables in summer, and preserves sell better than the fruit they came from. Value added beats volume, which is the same lesson our print on demand guide reaches from a completely different direction.

Before anything else, read your allotment tenancy, ring your council, and if you keep hens, check the bird flu rules. All three are free and quick. They prevent the only problems in this article that cost real money.

Frequently asked

Do I need a licence to sell vegetables from my garden? No licence. For regular sales you register as a food business with your council, which is free and cannot be refused. Official guidance says a council may decide registration is not needed where the activity is very limited and infrequent, so a few bags of surplus may not count.

Can you sell allotment produce? Only a surplus, in England and Wales. The Allotments Act 1922 defines an allotment garden as one cultivated wholly or mainly for you and your family to eat. Your tenancy agreement may ban sales altogether, and hens kept on an allotment cannot be kept for trade. Read the agreement first.

Do I need a licence to sell eggs from my garden? No licence, but you must register twice. Register as a food business with your council, and register as a bird keeper, which is compulsory even for one hen. With under 50 birds you can sell ungraded eggs direct to consumers. During a housing order, birds whose eggs you sell must be housed.

Is it legal to sell plants from home in the UK? Yes. In person sales to the public, at a gate table or a plant stall, need no plant passport. Online and distance sales do need one, even when the buyer is a private gardener. So selling seedlings at the gate is simple, and posting them is not.

Do I need a food hygiene certificate or insurance? A certificate is not compulsory, but your training must be adequate for what you make. A short online food safety course is the usual way to show that. Insurance is not a legal duty for a gate stall, though most market organisers ask for public liability cover.
