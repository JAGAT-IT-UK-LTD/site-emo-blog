Your First Home scheme: is a 2.5% deposit worth the 20% equity loan?

Ruhul Amin · Published 20 November 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked November 2026

Your First Home lets a first-time buyer in England buy a new-build with a 2.5% deposit and a government equity loan of 20%, interest free for 5 years. On GOV.UK's own example it cuts the monthly bill by about £282 at first. The catch is the loan you still owe, and interest on it from year 6.

The short version

  • You put down at least 2.5% of the price. The government adds an equity loan worth 20%, and a repayment mortgage covers the rest. On a £300,000 home that is £7,500, £60,000 and £232,500. Source: GOV.UK, read 20 November 2026.
  • The loan is interest free for 5 years, then costs 1.5% in year 6 and 3% from year 7. On £60,000 that is £75 and then £150 a month. Source: GOV.UK.
  • Only new-build homes in England qualify, inside a local price cap, with household income of £110,000 or less outside London and £140,000 or less in London. Source: GOV.UK.
  • Dates: pre-registration by the end of December 2026, first applications in February 2027 and first sales in April 2027. Source: GOV.UK.
  • The loan is repaid at 20% of the home's value at the time, so you repay more if the price rises and less if it falls. Source: GOV.UK.
  • Funding is limited: £4.7 billion over 5 years, with £700 million in 2027 to 2028. Source: GOV.UK.

1. What is Your First Home, and when does it start?

It is a new government scheme in England for first-time buyers who want a new-build home with a smaller deposit and a smaller mortgage. GOV.UK published the guidance on 28 October 2026 and last updated it on 3 November 2026.

You use three pots of money. You pay a deposit of at least 2.5% of the price. A participating lender gives you a repayment mortgage. The government adds an equity loan worth 20% of the price.

You own the home from the day you complete. GOV.UK says the government will not be a joint owner or your landlord. Lenders still run affordability and credit checks, as with any mortgage.

The timeline is short. GOV.UK says customer pre-registration opens before the end of 2026. First customer applications follow in February 2027, and the first property sales are due in April 2027.

Pre-registration does not commit you to buy. It gives you more information on your eligibility and the price cap in your area. Full terms and conditions are due before the scheme opens, so some detail below may still change.

If you are comparing it with other routes, see our guides to the Lifetime ISA and to stamp duty for first-time buyers.

2. Who can use it, and which homes?

GOV.UK says buyers will normally need to meet five tests:

  • be a first-time buyer
  • buy a new-build home in England, within the local price cap
  • use it as their main home
  • have total annual household income of £110,000 or less outside London, or £140,000 or less in London
  • be able to get a mortgage from a participating lender

That is not a scheme for existing homes. The home must be a new-build from a developer that has signed up. There is no way to use it on a resale flat or a second-hand house.

Each local authority has its own price cap. GOV.UK says the caps keep the support focused on modest homes. There are also separate caps for inner and outer London. Here is a sample from the published list, read on 20 November 2026.

3. What does it cost each month?

GOV.UK gives a worked comparison. It assumes a £300,000 home, a 30 year repayment mortgage at 5% and no repayment of the equity loan. It compares the scheme with a standard 95% mortgage.

PeriodStandard 95% mortgageYour First Home mortgageEquity loan interestTotal under Your First Home
Years 1 to 5£1,530£1,248£0£1,248
Year 6£1,530£1,248£75£1,323
From year 7£1,530£1,248£150£1,398

The deposit is lower too. GOV.UK says the same home would normally need a £15,000 deposit and a £285,000 mortgage on a standard 95% deal. Under the scheme the deposit is £7,500 and the mortgage is £232,500.

Here is our own arithmetic on those figures. The monthly saving is £282 in years 1 to 5, £207 in year 6 and £132 from year 7. Over the first 10 years that adds up to £25,740 (60 months at £282, 12 at £207 and 48 at £132).

Now the other side. Interest on the equity loan does not reduce what you owe. On £60,000, our arithmetic is £900 in year 6 and £1,800 a year after that. If you never repay any, that is £8,100 in interest by the end of year 10.

GOV.UK says its figures are an illustration only. They leave out mortgage fees, insurance, council tax, service charges, estate charges and maintenance. They also assume the same 5% rate for both mortgages, and a lender may price the two differently.

4. What happens to the 20% loan when prices move?

The loan is linked to the value of your home, not to the £60,000 you first received. When you repay, you normally pay back 20% of the home's value, based on an independent valuation.

GOV.UK gives two examples. If a £300,000 home is later valued at £320,000, you repay £64,000, which is £4,000 more. If it is valued at £280,000, you repay £56,000, which is £4,000 less. The government shares both gains and losses.

You normally repay the loan when you sell. You can also repay some or all of it earlier. Rules on minimum repayments, valuation costs and any fees are due before applications open, so we cannot cost them yet.

Worked example: Priya and Tom Hussain in Leeds

These two are made up for illustration. The household income and house price are invented, and the arithmetic apart from GOV.UK's own examples is ours.

Priya and Tom earn £78,000 between them, well under the £110,000 limit outside London. They find a £300,000 new-build in Leeds, under the £305,000 cap. They have £9,000 saved.

With Your First Home they put down £7,500, borrow £60,000 from the government and take a £232,500 repayment mortgage. They keep £1,500 spare. Using GOV.UK's assumptions, they pay about £1,248 a month for the first 5 years, against about £1,530 on a standard 95% mortgage.

A standard 95% deal would need £15,000. They only have £9,000, so without the scheme they could not buy this home. That is exactly the gap the scheme is meant to fill.

Ten years on, the home is valued at £340,000. They sell and owe 20% of that, which is £68,000. That is £8,000 more than they received. They have also paid £8,100 in equity loan interest across years 6 to 10, on our arithmetic.

Had the price fallen to £260,000, they would owe £52,000. In both cases the interest comes on top of that.

5. What to do before February

Pre-register when it opens. It is free to look and does not commit you. It will confirm your eligibility and show the price cap for your area.

Test the monthly cost at a higher rate. GOV.UK itself says to consider whether you could afford higher mortgage rates in future. Our guide to what rising rates mean for your mortgage is a useful starting point.

Plan for year 6. From then your monthly cost goes up by £75, then by £150. Decide now whether you will pay that from income or save to repay part of the loan. Our guide on whether to overpay a mortgage or save covers a similar choice.

Ask the developer what the price includes. Developers pay a participation fee of 1%, 3% or 4% of the sale price, depending on how many homes they sell through the scheme in a year. GOV.UK does not say whether that affects what buyers pay, so compare the price with similar homes nearby.

Get independent advice. GOV.UK strongly suggests independent financial and legal advice before committing.

Do not count on a place. The scheme has £4.7 billion over 5 years, with £700 million in 2027 to 2028 and £1 billion in each later year. On a £60,000 loan, our arithmetic puts the first year at roughly 11,700 homes. Developers manage the allocation, so you may need to ask.

6. The honest verdict

If a 5% deposit is the thing stopping you, this is a real help. A £7,500 deposit instead of £15,000 on a £300,000 home, and a lower mortgage, are worth having.

But it is a loan, not a gift. You will owe 20% of the home's value, and from year 6 you pay interest on it too. Your monthly saving shrinks from £282 to £132 on GOV.UK's example, and it does not count the £60,000 or more you still owe.

It is worth it if you want a new-build, you could not otherwise buy, and you can still afford the payments if rates rise. It is not worth it if you can already reach 5%, if you prefer an older home, or if the new-build is priced above similar homes nearby.

Wait for the full terms before deciding. Several of the costs that matter, including fees and repayment rules, are still to be published.

Frequently asked

Is Your First Home only for new-build homes? Yes. GOV.UK says it supports eligible new-build homes from developers taking part in the scheme. It will not help you buy an existing house or flat. The home must also be in England, within the local price cap, and used as your main home.

When can I apply for Your First Home? GOV.UK says customer pre-registration opens before the end of 2026. First customer applications are expected in February 2027, and the first sales in April 2027. Pre-registration does not commit you to buy. Full guidance and terms will be published before applications open.

Do I pay interest on the government equity loan? Not for the first 5 years. After that GOV.UK says the rate is 1.5% in year 6 and 3% from year 7. Interest is paid separately from your mortgage and does not reduce the loan. How it will be collected is still to be published.

Can I use Your First Home in London? Yes, in England including London, with a higher income limit of £140,000 or less. GOV.UK says there will be separate price caps for inner and outer London. Some boroughs on the published list, such as Camden, have higher caps than most areas.

Is the scheme available in Scotland or Wales? The GOV.UK guidance says the scheme applies to England. Buyers must purchase a new-build home in England, and the home must be their main residence. If you live elsewhere in the UK, check what help your own nation offers. The guidance mentions no version for Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.
