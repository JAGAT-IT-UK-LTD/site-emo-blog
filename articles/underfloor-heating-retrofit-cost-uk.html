Underfloor Heating Does Not Save You Money. A Lower Flow Temperature Does, And That Is The Bit To Buy

Ruhul Amin · Published 14 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Retrofit underfloor heating is sold on comfort and bought on a promise of lower bills. The comfort is real. The saving depends entirely on one number that most installers never mention, and on whether your doors still shut afterwards.

The short version

  • Low profile overlay systems add 15 to 25mm to the existing floor, which is the number that decides whether doors, stair bottoms and thresholds still work.
  • Fitted cost for a retrofit overlay runs roughly 100 to 140 pounds per square metre, against 100 to 190 for wet systems generally including dig outs.
  • UK building regulations now require new or substantially upgraded water underfloor systems to be designed to a maximum flow temperature of 55 degrees. That constraint is also where the saving comes from.
  • The saving is not from the pipes, it is from running the boiler cooler. A condensing boiler at 45 degrees recovers more latent heat than one at 70, which is the same free change we recommend to everyone with radiators.
  • At the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, gas is 7.97p per kWh and a typical 11,500 kWh gas household spends about 916 pounds a year on gas alone.
  • The payback: on a 20 square metre kitchen at 120 pounds per square metre, 2,400 pounds against a realistic 8 per cent whole house heating saving of 73 pounds a year is 33 years. Buy it for the comfort and the floor space, not the bill.

1. The 25 millimetres that decide the job

Before anybody quotes you a price, measure the gap under your internal doors and the height of the bottom stair. A retrofit underfloor system is not primarily a heating decision. It is a floor build up decision, and the heating follows.

A low profile overlay system adds 15 to 25mm to whatever is there now, sitting on top of the existing subfloor without excavation. Add the floor covering on top of that and you are commonly looking at 30 to 40mm of extra height. That is enough to stop a door closing, to make the bottom stair a trip hazard by being shorter than the others, and to bury a skirting board.

The alternative is a full dig out and new screed, which puts the pipes at the proper depth and adds nothing to the finished level, but which means excavating the floor, relaying insulation and waiting for a screed to cure. It costs considerably more and it is a building site for weeks.

Overlay systems reduce disruption and suit retrofit work where door heights, stairs and existing levels limit what you can add. They are also less forgiving of a poorly prepared subfloor, because there is no screed thickness to absorb an uneven base.

2. Where the saving actually comes from

This is the part that gets sold badly, so here it is plainly.

Underfloor heating emits from a very large surface at a low temperature. A radiator emits from a small surface at a high temperature. To deliver the same heat into the same room, the underfloor circuit runs at roughly 35 to 45 degrees where a radiator system is typically set at 65 to 75.

UK building regulations now require new or substantially upgraded water underfloor heating systems to be designed with a maximum flow temperature of 55 degrees. That is not an arbitrary cap. It is the design point at which a condensing boiler actually condenses.

The efficiency gain belongs to the low flow temperature, not to the pipes in the floor. You can have most of it tomorrow, for nothing, by turning the boiler down.

A condensing boiler recovers latent heat from the flue gases only when the return water is cool enough to condense them, which in practice means a return below about 55 degrees. Run it at 70 and it spends most of the winter not condensing, which is why the single biggest free saving in a British house is the flow temperature dial, covered in turning your boiler flow temperature down to 60 degrees.

Underfloor heating forces that low flow temperature by design. That is a genuine advantage. It is not worth 2,400 pounds on its own, because the dial is free.

4. Worked example: the Rahman household in Ilford

A 1930s semi, kitchen and dining area knocked through, 20 square metres, solid concrete floor, tiles going down anyway as part of a refit. Gas combi, currently set at 72 degrees.

The quote. Low profile overlay wet system, 20 square metres at 120 pounds per square metre fitted, manifold, zone control and commissioning. 2,400 pounds. Build up 18mm, plus 10mm tiles and adhesive, so 28mm total. The back door needed easing and the utility door needed 30mm off the bottom.

The saving, honestly stated. The kitchen is about 22 per cent of the heated floor area. Moving that zone to a 40 degree flow, and the rest of the house to 55 rather than 72, gets the boiler condensing for most of the season. On 11,500 kWh of gas at 7.97p, the annual gas cost is 916 pounds plus 108 pounds of standing charge. A realistic whole house efficiency gain of 8 per cent on the gas is 73 pounds a year.

Payback: 2,400 divided by 73 = 32.9 years. The system will not last that long.

What they could have had for nothing. Turning the existing boiler from 72 down to 60 degrees would have captured a large share of that same 73 pounds, this week, for no money at all.

The Rahmans fitted it anyway, and that was a reasonable decision, because the floor was coming up regardless, the room is where the family actually lives, and a warm tiled floor in January is worth something that does not appear on a bill. What would have been unreasonable is fitting it because of the bill.

5. When it genuinely is the right call

Three situations where the arithmetic changes and underfloor heating stops being a comfort purchase.

You are fitting a heat pump. A heat pump's efficiency falls sharply as flow temperature rises, far more steeply than a gas boiler's. Underfloor heating is close to a requirement for a well performing heat pump retrofit, and in that context the cost belongs on the heat pump project rather than on a saving calculation of its own. The same logic applies to the comparison in oil boiler against heat pump running costs.

The floor is already coming up. If you are digging out, re screeding or replacing a failed floor anyway, the marginal cost of adding pipes is a fraction of the standalone price, because the expensive part is the floor work, not the heating.

You want the wall space. Removing radiators from a small room genuinely changes what you can do with it. That is a floor plan benefit, and it is fair to value it, as long as you value it as a floor plan benefit and not as energy.

Frequently asked

Will my existing boiler work with it? Almost certainly yes as a heat source, through a mixing manifold that blends the hot flow down to the underfloor temperature. What matters is whether the rest of the house can also run cool, because a single boiler cannot serve 40 degree underfloor and 75 degree radiators efficiently at the same time without effectively giving up the condensing gain elsewhere.

Is it slower to heat up? Yes, and that is a feature rather than a fault. Underfloor heating works best on long steady run times rather than short bursts, which is a different way of operating a heating system and does not suit every household pattern.

Can I put it under a wooden floor? Yes, with the right build up, but engineered board is far safer than solid timber, and the floor covering manufacturer will state a maximum surface temperature. Exceed it and you will cup or gap the boards.

Does it need building regulations approval? The heating work itself usually falls under the controlled services provisions, and the flow temperature design limit above is a regulatory requirement. Your installer should either be competent person scheme registered or you need a building control application. Ask which, and get the certificate.

The honest verdict

Turn your boiler flow temperature down first. It is free, it captures most of the same efficiency gain, and it tells you within one winter whether your house can actually run on lower water temperatures.

If the floor is already coming up, or you are fitting a heat pump, put the pipes in. In those two cases the marginal cost is small and the benefit is real.

Otherwise, do not buy retrofit underfloor heating to save money. At roughly 33 years on the Rahmans' numbers it does not pay back, and any quote that claims otherwise is comparing it against a boiler nobody should be running at 72 degrees in the first place.
