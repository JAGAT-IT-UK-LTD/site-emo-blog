Low Standing Charge Tariff UK: Who Actually Saves, and Who Pays More

Ofgem's one year lower standing charge trial cuts the daily charge by about £150 a year for a home on gas and electricity, but it raises the price of every unit you use. On our own arithmetic it only pays if you use very little energy, and most trial places have already gone. Here is how to work out which side of the line your home sits on before you sign anything.

The short version

  • Ofgem is running a voluntary one year trial of lower standing charge tariffs from June 2026, with British Gas, EDF, E.ON and Octopus. It is not a rule that every supplier must offer one.
  • The headline cut is about £150 a year in standing charges for a home using both gas and electricity. Single fuel homes get less.
  • The catch is the unit rate. MoneySavingExpert reports E.ON's trial adds around 3.5p per kWh to electricity and 1p to gas against its baseline fixed deal.
  • Our own arithmetic: at those rates it pays below roughly 1,800 kWh of electricity and 7,500 kWh of gas a year, and loses money above that.
  • Octopus says its trial is full. Its page of 7 July 2026 says it was offered to 33,000 homes and can no longer be joined.
  • At the price cap a home pays about £308 a year in standing charges (our arithmetic from Ofgem's 54.83p and 29.68p a day), so £150 is roughly half of it.
  • Verdict: worth a look only for a small, low use home. For most families a standard fixed deal beats it.

1. What is a low standing charge tariff?

Your energy bill has two parts. The unit rate is what you pay for each kWh you use. The standing charge is a fixed daily amount. Ofgem describes it as a cost you pay every day, even if you use no energy that day.

A low standing charge tariff shrinks the daily amount and raises the price per unit to make up the difference. The supplier still needs to recover its costs, so the money moves from one part of the bill to the other.

Ofgem first proposed in September 2025 that every major supplier must offer one. It has since changed course. Its policy page says it is running a one year pilot instead, starting from June 2026, with a limited number of customers in England, Scotland and Wales.

The four suppliers in the pilot are British Gas, EDF, E.ON and Octopus. Each sets its own terms and eligibility. Ofgem says dual fuel customers could expect to pay about £150 a year less in standing charges than on the price cap, and that the tariffs may not be cheaper overall.

That last point matters most. A lower standing charge is not the same as a lower bill. If you want the wider picture first, our guide to the October 2026 price cap shows where the current rates come from.

2. How much standing charge do you pay now?

On the price cap from 1 October to 31 December 2026, Ofgem's published nationwide averages for direct debit are 54.83p a day for electricity and 29.68p a day for gas. Unit rates are 26.32p and 7.97p per kWh.

Multiply the daily figures by 365 and you get the yearly standing charge. This is our own arithmetic, using Ofgem's rates.

So the trial's £150 cut is about 49% of the standing charge a dual fuel home pays at the cap. That sounds large. It is large, but only against the standing charge, not the whole bill.

Gas has VAT at 5% in these rates. Electricity has none from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, so the electricity VAT cut is already built into the 26.32p.

These are national averages. Your region has its own rates, and Ofgem's page lists them by region. Check yours before doing any sums.

3. When does a lower standing charge save money?

The sum is simple. The tariff saves you the standing charge cut, then charges you extra on every unit. You come out ahead only while the extra unit cost is smaller than the cut.

MoneySavingExpert reports figures for E.ON's trial: standing charges fall by £150 a year for dual fuel users, while unit rates rise by around 3.5p per kWh for electricity and 1p per kWh for gas, compared with E.ON's baseline fix. MoneySavingExpert also says it pays off mainly for homes using under 1,800 kWh of electricity and 7,500 kWh of gas a year.

We checked that with our own arithmetic. The extra cost is 0.035 x electricity kWh plus 0.01 x gas kWh. The saving is £150 minus that.

Two warnings. First, the rise is quoted against E.ON's own baseline fix, not the price cap, so it tells you the shape of the trade, not the exact bill. Second, the rates are "around" figures from a news report, not a tariff sheet. Always get the real rates for your postcode before you decide.

4. Worked example: two households, one tariff

Take two homes offered the same trial tariff. We use the E.ON style figures above and our own arithmetic. These are illustrations, not real customers.

Priya Shah, one bed flat in Leeds. She works from the office three days a week and uses 1,700 kWh of electricity and 5,500 kWh of gas a year. Her standing charge falls by £150. Her extra unit cost is 0.035 x 1,700 = £59.50 plus 0.01 x 5,500 = £55.00, or £114.50. She is £35.50 better off, about £3 a month.

The Okafor family, four bed house in Luton. Two adults and two children use 3,400 kWh of electricity and 12,000 kWh of gas. Their extra unit cost is 0.035 x 3,400 = £119.00 plus 0.01 x 12,000 = £120.00, or £239.00. Against a £150 cut, they are £89.00 worse off.

The same tariff is a small win for one and a clear loss for the other. Nothing about the tariff changed. Only the usage did.

There is a second cost for the Okafors. MoneySavingExpert reports E.ON's trial is a 24 month fix with £100 per fuel exit fees. Leaving early would cost them £200 on top. Priya would face the same fee.

Cutting use is a surer way to cut a bill than moving charges around. Standby power alone can cost around £45 a year, and our energy bills guide covers the bigger items.

EDF's version is worth a note. MoneySavingExpert says it cuts the standing charge by £6.25 a month per fuel, which is £150 a year for both. Unit rates stay at its FreePhase rates, which vary by time of day. That suits a home that can move use to cheap hours. It also needs a smart meter, so our smart meter guide is a useful read first.

Ofgem says the pilot runs for one year from June 2026, so it should end around June 2027. Ofgem is also encouraging more suppliers to join. It has not said that any will.

6. What to do instead

You do not need a trial place to act on this. Here is a short order of work for a low use home.

  1. Find your real usage. Use twelve months of kWh from your bill or app, not an estimate.
  2. Run the sum above with the unit rate rise and standing charge cut on the tariff sheet you are offered.
  3. Compare against a plain fixed deal. MoneySavingExpert says the cheapest fixes are on average 15% or more below the current cap. A standard fix with normal rates may beat a trial tariff for you.
  4. Check exit fees and the contract length before you sign. A 24 month fix is a long time to be wrong.
  5. Wait for 25 November. Ofgem announces the next cap then, and our fix now or wait guide explains how to use it.

If your supply bills are the problem, switching and saving on UK bills covers the wider options. A low standing charge tariff is one small tool, not a fix for a high bill.

The honest verdict

For most households this is not worth chasing. The unit rate rise eats the saving once you use a normal amount of energy, and the family home in our example lost £89 a year.

It can work for a small flat with low use, a second home, or someone away a lot. Even then the gain in our sums is under £50 a year, and a long contract with exit fees can wipe that out.

Do the sum with your own kWh. If the extra unit cost is less than £150, it may help. If it is more, skip it and compare ordinary fixed deals instead.

Frequently asked questions about low standing charge tariffs

What is a standing charge on an energy bill? It is a fixed daily amount you pay to stay connected, whether or not you use any energy. Ofgem describes it as a cost you pay every day, even on days you use nothing. Suppliers set it for each tariff, and the price cap limits it along with the unit rate.

Is there a zero standing charge tariff in the UK? Not as a rule. Ofgem looked at a zero standing charge option in February 2025, then moved to a voluntary pilot of lower standing charge tariffs. The trial tariffs cut the charge by about £150 a year but do not remove it, and unit rates are higher.

Will a lower standing charge cut my energy bill? Only if you use little energy. Ofgem says the tariffs may not be cheaper overall because unit rates are higher. On our sums with E.ON style rates, a home using more than about 1,800 kWh of electricity and 7,500 kWh of gas a year pays more, not less.

Who offers a low standing charge tariff? Ofgem names British Gas, EDF, E.ON and Octopus as the pilot suppliers, and says it wants more to join. Each sets its own rules. Octopus says its trial is full, so check each supplier's own page for current availability and eligibility.
