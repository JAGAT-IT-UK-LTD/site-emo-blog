Maternity Allowance: £27 a week, or £194.32, and the £47.45 in between

Ruhul Amin · Published 6 September 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Maternity Allowance is what you claim when you do not qualify for Statutory Maternity Pay. For an employee it is a flat comparison. For a self-employed woman it is a sliding scale, and the gap between the bottom of it and the top is over six thousand pounds.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • £194.32 a week or 90% of your average weekly earnings, whichever is less, for up to 39 weeks, if you are employed or have recently stopped working (GOV.UK, Maternity Allowance, page updated 4 September 2026).
  • Self-employed, it is between £27 and £194.32 a week, decided by how many Class 2 National Insurance contributions you have paid in the 66 weeks before the baby is due.
  • Class 2 costs £3.65 a week and you need 13 weeks of it to reach the maximum. That is £47.45 to move from the bottom rate to the top one, and HMRC will tell you exactly how many weeks you are short.
  • 39 weeks is not 52. If you take the full Statutory Maternity Leave, the last 13 weeks are unpaid.
  • If you get Universal Credit, your UC payment is reduced by the amount of your Maternity Allowance, pound for pound. Jobseeker's Allowance stops entirely.

1. When Maternity Allowance is the answer

Statutory Maternity Pay is the default for employees, and it has an employment test attached: continuous service with the same employer over a qualifying period, and earnings above a weekly threshold. Fail either half of that test and SMP is simply not available to you, whatever your circumstances.

Maternity Allowance is the fallback. It is paid by DWP rather than by an employer, and it exists precisely for the people SMP misses:

  • the self-employed, who have no employer to pay SMP
  • anyone who changed jobs too recently to build up the continuous service SMP requires
  • anyone who has recently stopped working
  • people who do unpaid work for a spouse or civil partner's business

This guide is about that fallback. If you are an employee with settled service, SMP is your route and it is a different calculation entirely.

One structural point before the numbers. Maternity Allowance runs for up to 39 weeks. Statutory Maternity Leave runs for up to 52. GOV.UK spells out the consequence: "if you take the full 52 weeks Statutory Maternity Leave, your final 13 weeks will be unpaid." Three months of a year with a new baby and nothing coming in is worth planning for in month two, not month ten.

2. The employed and recently stopped working rate

If you are employed or have recently stopped working, the calculation is short.

£194.32 a week, or 90% of your average weekly earnings, whichever is less, for up to 39 weeks.

The tipping point is the earnings level at which 90% of your pay equals £194.32, which is about £215.91 a week. Below that, you get 90% of what you earned. At or above it, you get the flat £194.32 and nothing more, however much you earned.

Two things follow.

  • Higher earnings do not buy a higher rate. Someone on £600 a week and someone on £220 a week get the identical £194.32.
  • Across 39 weeks the maximum is £7,578.48. That is the whole of the money side, and it is the number to budget against.

3. The self-employed sliding scale

This is where the guides stop being useful, because it is not one rate. GOV.UK: "You can get between £27 to £194.32 a week for up to 39 weeks if you're self-employed."

What decides where you land is how many Class 2 National Insurance contributions you have made in the 66 weeks before your baby is due.

To get the maximum you must have:

  • been registered with HMRC for at least 26 weeks in the 66 weeks before the baby is due
  • paid Class 2 National Insurance for at least 13 of those 66 weeks

Miss the 13 week test and GOV.UK says your Maternity Allowance is calculated on how many weeks of Class 2 you have made. Pay none at all and you get £27 a week.

Now the sentence that turns this from a description into an action:

"You can top-up your contributions to increase your Maternity Allowance after you apply."

And the mechanism, in GOV.UK's words: after you apply, HMRC will contact you if you have paid less than 13 weeks of Class 2, and "they will tell you how many additional contributions you need to get the maximum amount of Maternity Allowance that you're entitled to". Once those contributions are linked to your application, "your payments will be increased and backdated if necessary".

Class 2 National Insurance contributions are £3.65 per week.

So the entire cost of moving from nothing to the full 13 weeks is 13 × £3.65 = £47.45.

Two operational notes worth having in advance.

  • Do not wait for perfect contributions before applying. The mechanism only works after you apply, because HMRC contacts you off the back of the application. Applying first is the correct order.
  • Expect £27 for a while. GOV.UK warns you may get £27 for a few weeks while your contributions are linked to the application, that payments are increased and backdated if necessary, and that this "can take several weeks". A low first payment is not a refusal.

There is one more, much smaller, self-employed adjacent case. If you do unpaid work for your spouse or civil partner's business, you can get £27 a week for up to 14 weeks. Not 39, and not means tested up. It is a narrow provision but almost nobody knows it exists.

4. Nia in Caernarfon: £47.45 that was worth £6,525.48

Nia is a self-employed hairdresser in Caernarfon. She registered with HMRC 40 weeks before her due date, which clears the 26 week registration test comfortably. She has not paid any Class 2 National Insurance in that period, because her profits have been low and she assumed there was nothing to pay.

Her position on the day she applies: zero Class 2 weeks, so £27 a week.

Over 39 weeks that is £1,053.

She applies. HMRC contacts her, as GOV.UK says it will, and tells her she needs 13 weeks of Class 2 to reach the maximum she is entitled to.

The cost: 13 weeks at £3.65, which is £47.45.

Her position afterwards: £194.32 a week for 39 weeks, which is £7,578.48, with earlier payments increased and backdated.

Forty seven pounds and forty five pence, for six and a half thousand pounds. There are not many decisions in personal finance with that shape, and this one is written on a government web page that almost nobody reads to the bottom.

Two honest caveats on the example.

  • It assumes Nia clears the 26 week registration test, which she does at 40 weeks. Somebody registered for only 20 of the 66 weeks does not, and paying Class 2 will not fix that limb.
  • It assumes she has paid no Class 2 at all, which is the case GOV.UK states produces the £27 rate. If you have paid some but fewer than 13 weeks, your rate is calculated on the weeks you have, so your own gap and your own top-up will be smaller.

The action either way is the same: apply, wait for HMRC to tell you the number, and pay it.

5. What it does to your other benefits

Maternity Allowance is not neutral, and GOV.UK is clear about which benefits move. The list of those affected is: Universal Credit, bereavement benefits, Carer's Allowance, Carer Support Payment, Council Tax Reduction, Employment and Support Allowance, Housing Benefit, and Jobseeker's Allowance.

The reassuring framing on the page is that "your total benefit payments will usually either go up or stay the same". The detail underneath is less comfortable in one case.

Universal Credit is reduced pound for pound. GOV.UK: "Your Universal Credit payment will be reduced by an amount equal to your Maternity Allowance payment." So for a household on Universal Credit and nothing else, Maternity Allowance may add nothing at all to the money coming in.

Which raises the obvious question: why bother claiming it?

Three reasons, all on the same page.

  1. National Insurance credits. "You'll get Class 1 National Insurance credits automatically with Maternity Allowance, which help you qualify for some other benefits and the State Pension." Those credits protect your State Pension record for the period, and they are worth real money decades later.
  2. Childcare costs. Universal Credit may pay some of your childcare costs if you get both Maternity Allowance and Universal Credit.
  3. The child element. You may get an extra amount of Universal Credit for your children whether you get Maternity Allowance or not, so the two are not alternatives.

Jobseeker's Allowance stops if you get Maternity Allowance. That is a stop, not a reduction.

The benefit cap may also apply, since it limits total benefits for most people aged 16 or over who have not reached State Pension age.

Practical points on payment and reporting. Maternity Allowance is paid every 2 or 4 weeks, into your bank, building society or credit union account. If you are on Universal Credit you must report the change on your Universal Credit account when you start getting it. And if you are overpaid because you did not report a change straight away, gave wrong information, or were overpaid by mistake, you may have to repay it.

The verdict

If you are employed and failed the SMP test, Maternity Allowance is £194.32 a week or 90% of your earnings for 39 weeks, and there is nothing to optimise: claim it and plan for the last 13 weeks of leave being unpaid. If you are self-employed, there is one thing to get right and it is worth more than anything else in this article. Apply, let HMRC tell you how many weeks of Class 2 National Insurance you are short, and pay them. Thirteen weeks at £3.65 is £47.45 and it can move you from £1,053 across the 39 weeks to £7,578.48. If you are on Universal Credit, claim it anyway for the Class 1 National Insurance credits, even though the payment itself is offset pound for pound.

Frequently asked

Can I get Maternity Allowance and Statutory Maternity Pay? No. They are alternatives. Maternity Allowance is what you claim when you do not qualify for SMP, which is usually because you are self-employed, changed jobs recently, or recently stopped working.

How late can I pay the Class 2 contributions? GOV.UK describes the process as happening after you apply: HMRC contacts you, tells you how many extra contributions you need, and once they are linked to your application your payments are increased and backdated if necessary. It warns that linking can take several weeks, so apply as early as you can rather than waiting.

Why is my first payment only £27? That is normal if your Class 2 contributions have not been linked to your claim yet. GOV.UK says you may get £27 for a few weeks while that happens, and that payments will be increased and backdated if necessary. It is not a final decision.

Is it worth claiming if I am on Universal Credit? The cash may be neutral, because Universal Credit is reduced by an amount equal to the Maternity Allowance. It is still usually worth claiming, because you get Class 1 National Insurance credits automatically, which protect your State Pension record, and because Universal Credit may pay some childcare costs if you get both.
