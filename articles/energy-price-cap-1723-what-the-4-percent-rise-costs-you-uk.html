1. What £1,723 actually is, and what it is not

Ofgem set the energy price cap at £1,723 a year for the period 1 October to 31 December 2026. That figure is for a household with typical consumption of both gas and electricity, paying by direct debit, if the cap level were sustained for a full year.

It is worth being precise about what that number is, because it is the most misreported figure in British consumer journalism.

It is not a cap on your bill. It is a cap on the unit rate and the standing charge. If you use more energy than the typical household, you will pay more than £1,723. If you use less, you will pay less. Ofgem says this in plain terms on its own explainer page: the cap does not limit the cost of your total bill.

It is not an annual figure. It is a quarterly cap expressed in annual terms, which is a genuinely confusing way to publish a number. The cap is reset every three months.

2. The 4% average hides an 8% gas rise

The headline is a 4% rise, or £60 a year, from £1,663 to £1,723. That works out at about £5 a month.

Underneath the average, the picture is lopsided. Most of the increase is driven by higher gas costs, with gas bills rising by around 8%. Households that do not use gas at all see an increase of less than 1%.

That is a large difference, and it means the "£5 a month" figure is close to meaningless for any specific household. If you heat with gas and cook with gas, you are at the sharp end of that 8%. If you are all electric, whether on a heat pump, storage heaters or simply a flat with no gas connection, this cap change is close to a non event for you.

3. The VAT change that most coverage skipped

Here is the detail that gets lost, and it explains the split above.

The government removed VAT from domestic electricity bills, and that change is reflected in this cap update. Because the VAT reduction pushes electricity costs down while wholesale prices push them up, electricity ended up broadly stable.

Gas did not get the same treatment. Ofgem's own price cap explainer still lists VAT at 5% on gas for 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027 among the costs included in the cap.

So the widely repeated shorthand "the VAT cut" is not quite right. It was an electricity VAT removal. If your home runs on gas for heating and hot water, which most British homes do, you received the wholesale price rise without the offsetting VAT relief. That is the whole reason a 4% average contains an 8% component.

There is a second reason the headline rarely matches a real bill, and it is the standing charge. The cap sets a maximum on the unit rate and the daily standing charge together, and both vary by region, by payment method and by meter type. Two households burning identical amounts of gas and electricity in different parts of the country pay different amounts, entirely legally, under the same national cap. Ofgem publishes the unit rates and standing charges by region, and that page, not the headline, is where your own numbers live.

4. Who the cap does not touch

The cap only protects people on standard variable tariffs. Ofgem states that you are not protected by the price cap if you have agreed a fixed tariff, if you are on a business energy contract, or if you get heat from a heat network or use heating oil.

The scale of that exclusion is larger than most people assume. Around 35% of households, roughly 11 million, are on fixed tariffs and were not affected by the October rise.

If you are one of them, every article you have read this month about your bill going up £60 was not about you. Your rate is whatever you agreed, until your fix ends.

5. A worked example: two households, same cap, different outcome

Two households in Doncaster, both on standard variable tariffs, both affected by the cap change on the same day.

The Hargreaves household live in a three bedroom semi with a gas boiler, gas hob and typical dual fuel consumption. The cap change lands on them almost exactly as advertised. Their annualised cap level moves from £1,663 to £1,723, a rise of £60 a year, which is £5 a month. Because they use gas for both heating and cooking, and gas rose about 8%, the gas side of their bill carries essentially the whole increase.

Their neighbour Fern rents a one bedroom flat with no gas connection. Everything runs on electricity. For her, the increase is less than 1%. On a smaller all electric bill, that is a change measured in single pounds across a quarter. She spent an evening worrying about a £60 rise that was never going to reach her.

The useful takeaway is unglamorous. Before you react to a cap announcement, check two things on your own bill: whether you are actually on a standard variable tariff, and what proportion of your usage is gas. Those two answers turn the national headline into your number. Without them, the headline is just a national average that fits almost nobody exactly.

And if you are on a fix that has months left to run, the honest answer is that this announcement does not help you and does not hurt you. There is nothing for you to do.

Frequently asked

Is £1,723 what I will pay this year? No. It is the level of the cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, expressed as an annual figure for a typical household. Your bill depends on how much energy you actually use.

Why did my electricity bill barely change? VAT was removed from domestic electricity, which offset the wholesale price rise. Gas did not get that relief and still carries 5% VAT, which is why gas bills rose around 8%.

Does the cap apply if I am on a fixed tariff? No. Ofgem is explicit that the price cap does not protect you if you have agreed a fixed tariff. Around 35% of households are in that position.

When does the cap change again? Ofgem sets the cap every three months, so the next level covers 1 January to 31 March 2027.
