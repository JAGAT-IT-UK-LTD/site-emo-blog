Coffee Cart Side Hustle in the UK: What It Takes and What It Pays

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Updated 18 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026

A coffee cart side hustle in the UK can work, but the coffee is the easy part. You must register with your council at least 28 days before you trade, and most public pitches also need a street trading licence. A busy three session week can leave roughly £180 to £240 before your time, insurance and kit, which works out below the £12.71 National Living Wage once you count the hours.

That matters because most coffee cart business guides lead with an 80% margin. This one starts with the rules, then the real numbers, then the cheaper routes in.

The short version
  • The coffee is the easy part. The barrier is licensing, pitches and insurance, and that is where most people quit.
  • You must register as a food business with your council at least 28 days before you trade. Councils do not charge a fee, and trading unregistered is an offence.
  • Nearly every public pitch also needs street trading consent from that specific council. Trading without it can mean a fine of up to £1,000.
  • Ingredient cost per cup is low, roughly 35p to 55p. Pitch fees, hours and footfall decide whether this works.
  • The trading allowance is £1,000 for 2026/27 and rises to £3,000 from 6 April 2027. A real cart passes either figure quickly.
  • Selling roasted beans from home is a far cheaper start than a cart, with a far smaller ceiling.

What licences do you need for a coffee cart business in the UK?

Everyone starts by researching grinders. That is the wrong end.

Here is the part that decides whether you are allowed to trade at all.

  • Register as a food business with your local council. It is done online, and it must be at least 28 days before you start trading. GOV.UK lists mobile units, stalls and vans among the businesses that must register. There is no fee: Babergh council, for example, says plainly there is no fee, and warns that running a food business unregistered can bring a fine or prison. You register through the FSA registration service, with GOV.UK guidance setting out the 28 day rule. A mobile unit registers at the place where it is normally stored overnight, not where it trades.
  • Street trading consent or licence. If you are on a street, a car park, a verge or other public land, you almost certainly need permission from that council. Each council sets its own fee, its own designated streets and its own waiting list. The council can refuse, or grant fewer days than you asked for, and trading without a licence can bring a fine of up to £1,000. Fees vary a great deal from one council to the next, so ask yours directly.
  • A late night licence, if you trade late. Selling hot food or hot drinks between 11pm and 5am counts as late night refreshment, a licensable activity. A daytime cart never meets this. A cart at a late event or outside a club might.
  • Food hygiene training. Level 2 Food Safety and Hygiene for Catering is not strictly a legal requirement. You must, though, be trained appropriately for what you do, and councils treat Level 2 as the working standard. It is a short online course.
  • Public liability insurance. Markets, events and councils will ask for it before they let you set up, and the organiser sets the level of cover. Our guide to public liability insurance for a side hustle explains what it covers.
  • Gas Safe certification if you use LPG for hot water or heating, and PAT testing on electrical equipment. Event organisers ask for the paperwork, not your word.
  • Allergen information. Milk is an allergen. Oat milk contains gluten unless it is certified gluten free. You need this written down, not remembered.

One correction worth making, because many coffee guides get it wrong. A drink made to order is not prepacked for direct sale, so the full PPDS ingredients label under Natasha's Law does not apply to each cup.

You still have to give allergen information accurately, and signpost clearly that customers can ask. The allergen guidance for food businesses now sits on GOV.UK.

None of this is unmanageable. It is simply several weeks of admin before you sell a single cup, and it is why most "start a coffee cart this weekend" content is useless.

The food side is the same ground any home food business has to clear before it takes a pound off anybody.

What does a cup of coffee actually cost you?

The ingredient maths is the encouraging part, so enjoy it while it lasts.

Coffee, 18g double shot25p to 40pDepends heavily on whether you buy speciality or commodity beans, and in what quantity.
Milk, 200ml10p to 15pOat and other alternatives cost more, which is why most sellers charge a surcharge.
Cup, lid, sleeve12p to 25pCompostable costs more. Some sites and events now require it.
Sugar, stirrer, napkin, waste3p to 8pSmall, but it is not zero.

Indicative UK trade price ranges, not an official figure. Check current prices with your own suppliers, because green coffee prices have been volatile and dairy moves with the season. For comparison, what the same cup costs you at home is the number your customers are quietly measuring you against.

So a flat white sold at £3.50 carries maybe 60p to 80p of ingredients and packaging. That looks like an 80% margin, and it is the number every guide leads with.

It has almost nothing to do with whether you make money. Your pitch fee, equipment repayment, fuel, time and cups sold per session decide that.

A brilliant margin on 25 cups a day is a hobby.

Buy used. Search "mobile coffee business for sale" and you will find people who discovered the licensing after they bought the equipment.

That is unfortunate for them and useful for you.

One piece of equipment advice that actually matters: spend on the grinder before the machine. A mediocre machine with a good grinder makes acceptable coffee. A brilliant machine with a poor grinder does not.

Where should a coffee cart trade?

Two identical setups, one making a living and one making nothing, differ almost entirely by where they park.

What works: business parks with poor on site catering, park and playground entrances at weekends, railway station approaches, park runs and car boot sales.

Farmers' markets, sports clubs and events with a captive audience and no competing cafe also work well.

What does not: a high street with four established coffee shops. You will be the fifth choice with the worst seating and no toilet.

The strongest position is a private site with permission rather than a public street. Private land normally sidesteps street trading consent, and the landowner wants you to do well.

Think of a business park manager, a garden centre, a large church, a gym or a builders' merchant. Knock on doors. That conversation is worth more than any equipment upgrade.

Is a coffee cart profitable? A worked UK week

Meet Dan, a warehouse worker in Bristol who runs a market stall three sessions a week. He sells 45 cups a session at an average of £3.20.

  • Revenue: 135 cups a week, £432.
  • Ingredients and packaging at roughly 70p a cup: about £95.
  • Pitch fees at three sessions: often £60 to £120 depending on the market.
  • Fuel, gas, waste and card fees: call it £40.
  • Left over: roughly £180 to £240 a week before he pays himself, insures anything or repays the setup.

Now count the hours. Three sessions of five hours is fifteen hours. Setup, breakdown, cleaning, stock runs and admin realistically add another eight.

That is 23 hours for £180 to £240, or about £7.80 to £10.40 an hour. The National Living Wage is £12.71 an hour from April 2026, and it rises to £13.21 from April 2027.

So Dan has a real side income, but it pays less per hour than a minimum wage job until his volume goes up.

The coffee businesses that work push past this in one of three ways. More cups per session at a better site. A second stream like pastries with a much higher markup. Or events and weddings, where a day rate replaces cup by cup selling.

Can you sell coffee from home instead?

If the licensing wall is what stops you, the alternative is selling coffee rather than serving it.

A coffee roasting side hustle still means registering as a food business and dealing with labelling law. But there is no pitch, no gazebo and no early start in the rain.

The equipment cost is a fraction of a cart. A small sample roaster is affordable. The output is small.

Be clear eyed about the ceiling. You are competing with well run UK micro roasters who have years of head start and better green bean buying power.

Speciality coffee buyers are knowledgeable and loyal to roasters they already trust. Selling beans works best alongside something you already have, a cafe, a newsletter or a local following, rather than as a cold start.

How is a coffee side hustle taxed?

For the 2026/27 tax year, the first £1,000 of trading income is covered by the trading allowance and does not need reporting. Above that, you register as self employed with HMRC and file a Self Assessment return.

From 6 April 2027 the trading allowance rises to £3,000, and the Self Assessment reporting threshold rises with it. Our guide to the £3,000 trading allowance from April 2027 walks through who gains.

Dan's stall shows why this barely matters for a real cart. At £432 a week he passes £1,000 of sales in his third week, and £3,000 in his seventh.

Two things people get wrong. First, the allowance is on income, not profit, so £1,200 of sales with £900 of costs still crosses the line.

Second, if you claim the allowance you cannot also deduct expenses. Once your costs are meaningful, which happens quickly with equipment, you are usually better off deducting real costs.

The wider position, including what happens when this sits on top of a PAYE job, is in our guide to side hustle tax rules. This is general information and not tax advice. If the numbers get real, an hour with an accountant costs less than the mistake.

The part most guides leave out. Claiming the trading allowance changes what counts as your income for some things and not for others.

HMRC's own guidance says that if you deduct the allowance instead of real expenses, your income is reduced for tax credits, the high income child benefit charge, student loan repayments and married couple's allowance. Income for Universal Credit purposes is not affected by the allowances.

So a home barista on Universal Credit who claims the £1,000 allowance still has the full sales figure counted against the award. If you are on Universal Credit, report real income and real costs every assessment period, and treat the allowance as a tax shortcut only. Checked against HMRC guidance on 18 November 2026.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a licence to sell coffee from a cart in the UK? Usually yes. Every coffee cart must register as a food business with the council at least 28 days before trading. On public land you will normally also need street trading consent from that council. Selling hot drinks between 11pm and 5am needs a late night refreshment licence too. Private land with the owner's permission often avoids the street licence.

How much does it cost to start a coffee cart? A market stall with a gazebo, a single group machine, a good grinder and a generator is the cheapest real setup, usually low thousands if you buy second hand. Trailers and vans cost far more. Add insurance, hygiene training, pitch fees and stock before you count on any profit from your first month.

Is a coffee cart business profitable? It can be, but slowly. Our worked week of 135 cups at £3.20 leaves about £180 to £240 for 23 hours of work. That is below the £12.71 National Living Wage. Carts that do well find a busier private site, add pastries with a higher markup, or move into events and weddings on a day rate.

Can I just turn up at a market and sell coffee? No. Markets allocate pitches, and the good ones have waiting lists that can run for months. Ring the market operator rather than the council and ask what the process is. Have your food registration, hygiene certificate and public liability insurance ready, because most operators ask for all three before offering a pitch.

Do I need a food hygiene rating for a coffee cart? Yes, in practice. Mobile caterers are inspected and rated like any other food business, by the council where the unit is stored overnight. A poor rating is public and event organisers check it. Clean water, hand washing, safe milk storage and written allergen information are what the inspector will look at first.

The verdict

A coffee cart side hustle is a real income for someone who enjoys early mornings, physical work and talking to strangers. It is a poor investment for anyone chasing the 80% margin figure.

Secure a pitch first, buy used equipment second, and treat the first six months as paying for your own education. The most common failure is buying the kit before securing a pitch. If the licensing puts you off, that is useful information rather than a failure.
