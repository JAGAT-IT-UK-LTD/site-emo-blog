Turning Good Coffee Into a Side Hustle in the UK

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Checked August 2026
The short version
  • The coffee is the easy part. The barrier is licensing, pitches and insurance, and that is where most people quit.
  • You must register as a food business with your council at least 28 days before you trade. It is free, and trading without it is an offence.
  • Nearly every public pitch also needs street trading consent from that specific council, and the rules differ from borough to borough.
  • Ingredient cost per cup is genuinely low, roughly 35p to 55p. Everything else is what decides whether this works.
  • Selling roasted beans from home is a far cheaper start than a cart, and a far smaller ceiling.

What you legally have to do first

Everyone starts this by researching grinders. That is the wrong end. Here is the part that decides whether you are allowed to trade at all.

  • Register as a food business with your local council. Free, done online, and it must be at least 28 days before you start trading. This applies even if you are a one-person stall selling nothing but coffee. Trading unregistered is an offence, and it is the first thing an environmental health officer checks. Registration is free, cannot be refused, and is done through the FSA registration service, with GOV.UK setting out the 28 day rule. A mobile setup registers at the address where it is stored overnight, not where it trades.
  • Street trading consent or licence. If you are on a street, a car park, a verge or any public land, you almost certainly need permission from that council, and each council sets its own fee, its own designated streets and its own waiting list. The council can also refuse consent outright, or grant fewer days than you asked for, and trading without it carries a fine of up to £1,000. Some London boroughs charge several hundred pounds a year. Some rural districts charge very little. Do not assume what you read about one area applies to yours.
  • Food hygiene training. Level 2 Food Safety and Hygiene for Catering is not, strictly, a legal requirement, but you must be trained appropriately for what you do, and councils treat Level 2 as the working standard. It is a cheap online course.
  • Public liability insurance. Markets, events and councils will ask for it before they let you set up, usually five million pounds of cover. Nobody will let you trade without it.
  • Gas Safe certification if you use LPG for hot water or heating, and PAT testing on electrical equipment. Event organisers ask for the paperwork, not your word.
  • Allergen information under Natasha's Law and general food law. Milk is an allergen. Oat milk contains gluten unless it is certified gluten free. You need this written down, not remembered. One correction worth making, because a lot of coffee guides get it wrong: a drink made to order is not prepacked for direct sale, so the FSA rules for non-prepacked food apply instead of the PPDS label. You still have to give allergen information accurately, and you must signpost clearly that customers should ask, but you are not required to print an ingredients label for every cup.

None of this is unmanageable. It is simply four to eight weeks of admin that must happen before you sell a single cup, and it is the reason most "start a coffee cart this weekend" content is useless. The food side of it is the same ground any home food business has to clear before it takes a pound off anybody.

What a cup actually costs you

The ingredient maths is the encouraging part, so enjoy it while it lasts.

ItemRoughlyNote
Coffee, 18g double shot25p to 40pDepends heavily on whether you buy speciality or commodity beans, and in what quantity.
Milk, 200ml10p to 15pOat and other alternatives are roughly double, which is why most sellers charge a surcharge.
Cup, lid, sleeve12p to 25pCompostable costs more. Some sites and events now require it.
Sugar, stirrer, napkin, waste3p to 8pSmall, but it is not zero.

Indicative UK trade prices as at August 2026. Verify current prices with your own suppliers, because coffee green-bean prices have been unusually volatile and dairy moves with the season. For comparison, what the same cup costs you at home is the number your customers are quietly measuring you against.

So a flat white sold at £3.50 carries maybe 60p to 80p of ingredients and packaging. That looks like an 80% margin, and it is the number every guide leads with.

It is also the number that has almost nothing to do with whether you make money. What decides that is your pitch fee, your equipment repayment, your fuel, your time, and how many cups you actually sell in a session. A brilliant margin on 25 cups a day is a hobby.

Three setups, three price tags

SetupTypical outlayHonest assessment
Market stall, gazebo, single-group machine and a generatorLow thousandsThe realistic entry point. You can build it second hand. Weather decides your income, and British weather is not on your side.
Converted trailer or three-wheelerMid five figures new, far less usedLooks the part, tows behind a normal car, opens up events and weddings. Storage and towing become real problems.
Converted vanFive figures and upWeatherproof and self-contained. Now you also have an MOT, insurance, servicing and a vehicle that depreciates.

Buy used. The second-hand market for coffee carts and trailers is full of people who discovered the licensing after they bought the equipment. That is unfortunate for them and useful for you.

One piece of equipment advice that actually matters: spend on the grinder before the machine. A mediocre machine with a good grinder makes acceptable coffee. A brilliant machine with a poor grinder does not.

The pitch is the whole business

Two identical setups, one making a living and one making nothing, differ almost entirely by where they park.

What works: business parks with poor on-site catering, park and playground entrances at weekends, railway station approaches, park runs, car boot sales, farmers' markets, sports clubs, and events with a captive audience and no competing cafe.

What does not: a high street with four established coffee shops. You will be the fifth choice with the worst seating and no toilet, and you will spend the day watching people walk past.

The strongest position of all is a private site with permission rather than a public street, because private land normally sidesteps the street trading consent problem entirely and the landowner has an interest in you doing well. A business park manager, a garden centre, a large church, a gym, a builders' merchant. Knock on doors. That conversation is worth more than any equipment upgrade.

A realistic week

Take a market stall doing three sessions a week. Say 45 cups a session at an average of £3.20.

  • Revenue: 135 cups a week, about £432.
  • Ingredients and packaging at roughly 70p a cup: about £95.
  • Pitch fees at three sessions: often £60 to £120 depending on the market.
  • Fuel, gas, waste, card fees: call it £40.
  • Left over: roughly £180 to £240 a week before you have paid yourself, insured anything, or repaid the setup.

Now count the hours. Three sessions of five hours is fifteen hours, plus setup, breakdown, cleaning, stock runs and admin, which realistically adds another eight. That is 23 hours for around £200. It is a real side income and it is roughly minimum wage until volume goes up.

The businesses that work push past this in one of three ways: more cups per session at a better site, a second stream like pastries with a much higher markup, or events and weddings, where a day rate replaces cup-by-cup selling entirely.

The cheaper route: selling beans

If the licensing wall is what stops you, the alternative is selling coffee rather than serving it.

Roasting at home to sell still means registering as a food business and dealing with labelling law, but there is no pitch, no gazebo, no early start in the rain, and the equipment cost is a fraction. A small sample roaster is affordable. The output is small.

Be clear-eyed about the ceiling. You are competing with well-run UK micro-roasters who have five years of head start and better green-bean buying power. Speciality coffee buyers are knowledgeable and loyal to roasters they already trust. Selling beans works best as a companion to something else you already do, a cafe, a newsletter, a local following, rather than as a cold start.

Tax and the trading allowance

The first £1,000 of trading income in a tax year is covered by the trading allowance and does not need reporting. Above that, you register as self-employed with HMRC and file a Self Assessment return. The wider position, including what happens when this sits on top of a PAYE job, is in our guide to side hustle tax rules.

Two things people get wrong. First, the allowance is on income, not profit, so £1,200 of sales with £900 of costs still crosses the line. Second, if you claim the allowance you cannot also deduct expenses, so once your costs are meaningful, which happens quickly with equipment, you are better off deducting properly.

This is general information and not tax advice. If the numbers get real, an hour with an accountant costs less than the mistake.

The part most guides leave out. Claiming the trading allowance changes what counts as your income for some things and not for others. HMRC's own guidance says that if you deduct the allowance instead of real expenses, your income is reduced for tax credits, the high income child benefit charge, student loan repayments and married couple's allowance, but that income for Universal Credit purposes is not affected by the allowances. So a home barista on Universal Credit who claims the 1,000 pound allowance still has the full sales figure counted against the award, while the same person would see a lower figure used for a student loan deduction. If you are on Universal Credit, report real income and real costs every assessment period and treat the allowance as a tax shortcut only. Checked against HMRC guidance on 10 October 2026.

Questions people ask

Can I just turn up at a market? No. Markets allocate pitches, and the good ones have waiting lists measured in months. Ring the market operator, not the council, and ask what the process is.

Do I need a food hygiene rating? Mobile caterers are inspected and rated like any other food business. A poor rating is publicly visible and event organisers check it.

Is speciality coffee worth it or should I buy cheap beans? Somewhere in between. At £3.50 a cup people notice bad coffee but they do not pay extra for a single-origin natural process. Buy a solid, consistent, freshly roasted blend from a UK roaster and put the money you saved into your grinder.

What is the single most common reason these fail? Buying the equipment before securing a pitch. Order it in the other direction and the whole thing becomes much less risky.

The verdict

This is a real side income for someone who enjoys early mornings, physical work and talking to strangers, and it is a poor investment for anyone chasing the 80% margin figure. Secure a pitch first, buy used equipment second, and treat the first six months as paying for your own education. If the licensing puts you off, that is useful information rather than a failure.
