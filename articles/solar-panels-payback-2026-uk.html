Solar Panels Pay Back In About Ten Years, But Only If You Sign Up To Export. Without That, Add Twenty.

Solar is the biggest home energy purchase most people will ever make, and the payback depends less on the roof than on a piece of paperwork. The Energy Saving Trust's figures, updated 27 August 2026, put a typical 4.5 kilowatt peak system at about £7,600 and the annual saving anywhere from £190 to £930 depending on where you live, whether you are home in the day, and above all whether you have an export tariff. We turned every combination into a payback and set it against the 25-year life of the panels and the 12-year life of the inverter.

The short version

  • A typical 4.5 kWp home system costs around £7,600 installed, on Energy Saving Trust figures updated 27 August 2026, using about 12 panels over 20 to 30 square metres of roof.
  • With a Smart Export Guarantee tariff, the Energy Saving Trust puts the saving at £740 to £930 a year across London and Manchester, and the payback at 9 to 12 years across London, Manchester, Aberystwyth and Stirling. Being out all day adds one year at most.
  • Without an export tariff the same panels save £190 to £330 a year and take 23 to 40 years to pay back, longer than the panels last. The export payment is two thirds of the return.
  • The inverter needs replacing after about 12 years and panels last 25 or more, so a 9 to 12 year payback leaves 13 or more years of near-free electricity; a 23 year payback leaves almost none.
  • A battery adds £5,000 to £8,000 and shifts the sum rather than shortening it; it is a separate decision and this article does not assume one.
  • Three things decide whether to buy: an unshaded roof facing south, east or west; a Smart Export Guarantee application on the day the system is registered; and the intention to stay in the house past the payback year.

1. The cost and saving figures, and what they assume

The Energy Saving Trust's solar panels page, last updated 27 August 2026 and read again on 4 September 2026, is the most complete public set of solar figures for Great Britain and it states its basis: a 4.5 kilowatt peak system, around 12 panels, on a suitable roof, at fuel prices from July 2026. The installed cost is around £7,600.

Its annual savings for two of the cities it models, in pounds, are:

City and occupancySaving with a Smart Export Guarantee tariffSaving with no export tariff
London, home all day£930£330
London, home half the day£920£270
London, out all day£900£200
Manchester, home all day£810£310
Manchester, home half the day£800£250
Manchester, out all day£740£190

Its published payback periods with export payments, for four cities, are London 9 years in all three occupancy cases, Manchester 10, 10 and 11, Aberystwyth 9, 9 and 10, and Stirling 11, 11 and 12. Northern Ireland figures are due in its next update and are not included here.

Two things about the assumptions matter. The saving includes electricity you use yourself, valued at the price you would have paid for it, plus electricity you export, valued at the export rate, which the Energy Saving Trust puts at typically around 12p a unit. And the July 2026 fuel prices are a little below the October to December 2026 cap of 26.32p, so the self-use part of the saving is slightly understated for the coming quarter. We have used the Energy Saving Trust's figures as published.

The Energy Saving Trust's published paybacks are about a year longer than ours, which is consistent with rounding up and with allowing for an inverter replacement over the life of the system; treat its column as the answer and ours as the check. Either way the shape is the same. With an export tariff, every combination pays back inside 12 years and leaves 13 to 16 years of the panels' life producing electricity for nothing. Without one, no combination pays back inside 23 years, and the out-all-day cases run past the panels' own life.

Notice how little occupancy matters once you export. London home all day and London out all day are both 9 years. That is because a household that is out exports most of what it makes and is paid for it. It is the export payment, not your working pattern, that carries the return.

3. Why the export tariff is two thirds of the return

Take Manchester, home half the day. With export, £800 a year. Without, £250. The difference, £550 a year, is what the grid pays you for the electricity you did not use, and it is 69 per cent of the total saving. The panels are the same. The roof is the same. The only difference is a tariff.

The Smart Export Guarantee is the scheme, in England, Scotland and Wales, under which licensed suppliers with 150,000 or more customers must offer to pay for exported electricity. The rate is set by each supplier, not by Ofgem; the Energy Saving Trust's "typically around 12p" is a working figure, and rates in 2026 range from a few pence to well above that for suppliers who want your import business too. You do not have to export to the same supplier you buy from.

Three things go wrong in practice. Some households never apply, because the installer did not say so and the paperwork looks optional. Some apply to their existing supplier without checking whether another pays double. And some are on an old tariff that pays a flat few pence when a better one exists. The remedy for all three is the same: on the day the installer registers the system with your network operator and gives you the MCS certificate, compare export tariffs and apply, and check the rate again once a year.

A battery changes the split but not the total. It lets you use more of your own generation at 26.32p instead of exporting it at around 12p, so the self-use figure rises and the export figure falls. On the Energy Saving Trust's costing of £5,000 to £8,000 for a battery, the extra saving rarely covers the extra cost inside the battery's own life, and it is a separate decision that depends on your tariff. This article prices panels alone.

4. Inverter, panels, roof and the things that shorten or lengthen it

Panels should last 25 years or more; the Energy Saving Trust says so and manufacturers' performance warranties commonly run that long. The inverter, which turns the panels' direct current into mains, needs replacing after around 12 years. Inverter warranties are five years as a minimum and can be extended to 15, but the Energy Saving Trust notes a 15-year warranty can cost almost as much as a replacement inverter, so price both. Budget for one inverter replacement in the life of the system; it is the main reason to add a year to the simple payback.

Orientation and shade. South-facing and unshaded is ideal. East or west facing loses around 15 to 20 per cent of the output, which pushes a 9 year payback towards 11. North-facing is not recommended. Shade from a chimney or a tree matters more than people expect, and optimisers help only where there is shade.

The roof itself. Panels last 25 years; a roof covering that will not is a cost you incur twice, once to take the panels off and once to put them back. If the roof needs renewing, do it first or at the same time, and if scaffolding is already up for other work, the installation is cheaper.

Moving house. The payback assumes you are there to collect it. Solar does add to a home's value and its EPC, but not by £7,600 in year two. If you expect to move inside the payback period, the sum is weaker than the table says.

Permissions. Roof panels are usually permitted development; listed buildings, conservation areas and national parks are the exceptions, and the installer must register the system with the Distribution Network Operator. Use an MCS-certified installer, because MCS certification is what the Smart Export Guarantee requires.

5. Worked example: the Nowak family in Norwich

Four-bed detached, south-east facing rear roof, no shade, two adults working from home two days a week each, children at school

Norwich is not one of the Energy Saving Trust's four cities, so the Nowaks use the London figures, which is a slight overstatement for East Anglia, and take the south-east orientation as a 10 per cent haircut. Their occupancy is closest to "home half the day".

Saving with export, our arithmetic. London half-day figure £920, less 10 per cent for orientation, about £830 a year.

Payback. £7,600 divided by £830 is 9.2 years; call it 10 with one inverter replacement, which matches the Energy Saving Trust's London figure. Panels then produce for a further 15 years.

If they forget the export application. London half-day without export is £270, less 10 per cent, about £245. Payback 31 years. The house will be sold long before.

The battery question. They price a battery at the bottom of the Energy Saving Trust's range, £5,000. To pay back inside its life it would need to save them over £400 a year on top of the panels, which on their tariff it does not. They buy panels only, apply for an export tariff the day the certificate arrives, and put a note in the diary to compare rates every September.

6. The honest verdict

Buy solar panels if three things are true: you have an unshaded roof facing south, east or west; you will apply for a Smart Export Guarantee tariff the day the system is registered and check the rate yearly; and you expect to be in the house for ten years. On the Energy Saving Trust's August 2026 figures that is a 9 to 12 year payback on £7,600, followed by 13 to 16 years of electricity that costs nothing.

Do not buy them if you will not export, or cannot, because the same panels then take 23 to 40 years to pay back and the inverter will need replacing before they do. And treat a battery as a second decision with its own sum, not as part of this one. Adding storage changes the payback again, and whether a solar battery pays for itself does that calculation separately.

Frequently asked

How long do solar panels take to pay for themselves in the UK? On Energy Saving Trust figures updated 27 August 2026, 9 to 12 years for a 4.5 kWp system costing about £7,600, with a Smart Export Guarantee tariff, across London, Manchester, Aberystwyth and Stirling. Without an export tariff, our arithmetic on the same figures gives 23 to 40 years.

How much do solar panels save per year? £740 to £930 a year with export payments for London and Manchester, and £190 to £330 without, on the Energy Saving Trust's July 2026 fuel prices. Being home all day adds only £30 to £70 once you export.

Is a solar battery worth it? It is a separate sum. The Energy Saving Trust prices batteries at £5,000 to £8,000; a battery raises the share of your own electricity you use at 26.32p instead of exporting at around 12p, and whether that covers the cost depends on your tariff and usage.
