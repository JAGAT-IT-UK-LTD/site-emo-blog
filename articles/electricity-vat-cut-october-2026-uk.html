Electricity VAT Cut: 0% Until 30 June 2027, and Why Your Bill Still Rose

Ruhul Amin · Published 24 August 2026 · Updated 9 November 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked November 2026

The electricity VAT cut took VAT on household electricity from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026, and the 28 October Budget extended it to 30 June 2027. Ofgem says it takes about £45 off the yearly price cap. You will not see it as a line on your bill, because it was already inside the October cap, and a rise in gas prices swallowed it.

The short version

  • VAT on household electricity went from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026. It covers England, Scotland and Wales.
  • The Budget on 28 October 2026 extended it. The zero rate now runs to 30 June 2027, three months longer than first planned. 5% returns on 1 July 2027 unless it is extended again.
  • Gas is not included. Gas stays at 5% VAT. On dual fuel, only the electricity half changed.
  • Ofgem set the October cap at £1,723, up 4% from £1,663. Ofgem says the rise would have been about £45 higher without the VAT change.
  • There is nothing to claim and no refund. It arrived as lower electricity unit rates, already built into the cap.
  • The pre-tax price of electricity rose 5.8%. The VAT cut absorbed all of it. That matters on the day 5% comes back.
  • Northern Ireland is excluded. Electricity there stays at the 5% reduced rate.
  • The January 2027 cap is not out yet. Ofgem announces it on 25 November 2026.

1. What did the electricity VAT cut change on 1 October?

The government announced on 21 July 2026 that VAT on domestic electricity would be temporarily zero rated. HMRC published the detail on 8 September 2026.

The rate went from 5% to 0% on 1 October 2026. It applies to electricity only. HMRC says the rate stays at 5% on every other type of domestic fuel, so gas, heating oil and LPG are unchanged.

It covers more than homes. The Budget costing lists domestic use, charities' non-business use, residential accommodation such as care homes, and businesses that use so little power they fall under the de minimis limit. HMRC sets that limit at an average of 33 kWh a day, or 1,000 kWh a month.

Northern Ireland is excluded. HMRC says the order does not apply there, and qualifying electricity in Northern Ireland stays at the reduced 5% rate. If you run a small business from home and are VAT registered, the rate you charge matters more than the rate you are charged, which is the whole question in whether the VAT flat rate scheme is worth joining.

2. How long does the electricity VAT cut last now?

When it was announced, the zero rate was due to end on 31 March 2027. That changed at the Budget on 28 October 2026.

The Treasury's Budget policy costings now describe the measure as a cut from 5% to 0% from 1 October 2026 to 1 July 2027. HMRC's VAT on fuel and power notice now gives the end date as 30 June 2027. Both say the same thing: 0% for nine months, then 5% from 1 July 2027.

The Treasury puts the cost at £755 million in 2026 to 2027 and £305 million in 2027 to 2028. That second figure is the three month extension.

One caution. Some Ofgem pages written before the Budget still say 31 March 2027. The HMRC notice and the Budget documents are the newer source, so plan around 30 June 2027. Our older guide on the original 31 March 2027 end date for electricity VAT explains what happens to unit rates when the 5% comes back, and the same sums now apply three months later.

3. Why did my bill not go down?

This is the part that drives the angry calls to suppliers, so it is worth reading twice.

Ofgem's price cap is quoted including VAT. The October cap, which Ofgem confirmed on 26 August 2026 for 1 October to 31 December, was worked out with electricity VAT already at 0%.

So you did not get a £45 credit, a rebate or a VAT line going to zero. The saving was spent before the number reached the news.

There was never a second saving waiting for you. The £45 was not added on top of the cap. It is one of the reasons the cap is not higher than it is.

And it still was not enough. Ofgem set the cap at £1,723 a year for a typical direct debit household, up 4% from £1,663. That is £60 a year, or £5 a month.

Ofgem blamed wholesale gas prices, up 11% over the previous three months because of the conflict in the Middle East. A tax cut on electricity does nothing about the price of gas.

Ofgem's own note says the rise would have been about £45 higher without the VAT change. That matches the government's figure. It also shows the money went into absorbing a rise, not into your pocket.

One footnote helps when you compare figures across the year. Ofgem changed its typical household on 1 July 2026 to 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year, down from 2,700 and 11,500. On the old yardstick, the same October rates come to £1,935. Your usage did not change. Only the yardstick did.

4. Is the VAT cut hiding an electricity price rise?

Yes. Here are Ofgem's direct debit rates, the averages for England, Scotland and Wales. Your region will differ.

RateJuly to September 20261 October to 31 December 2026Change
Electricity unit rate26.11p per kWh26.32p per kWh+0.8%
Electricity standing charge57.19p a day54.83p a day−4.1%
Gas unit rate7.33p per kWh7.97p per kWh+8.7%
Gas standing charge29.04p a day29.68p a day+2.2%

Look at the electricity unit rate. 26.11p to 26.32p is a rise of less than one percent. Most coverage called electricity broadly stable.

It is stable for you, this quarter. It is not stable underneath.

The 26.11p you paid in July included 5% VAT. The 26.32p you pay now includes none. So the like for like comparison is 24.87p against 26.32p.

The pre-tax price of electricity went up 5.8%. The VAT cut absorbed all of it and a little more.

The standing charge did the same. It seems to fall from 57.19p to 54.83p a day. Strip the VAT out of July's figure and it was 54.47p, so the underlying charge went up by 0.7%.

Here is why that matters. When 5% VAT returns on 1 July 2027, today's 26.32p becomes about 27.64p and the standing charge about 57.57p, before any other change. Nothing in the wholesale market has to move for your bill to jump. The tax break simply stops covering a rise that already happened.

None of this is hidden or improper. But "electricity bills stay broadly stable" and "the price of electricity did not rise" are two different statements. Only the first one is true.

5. How much is the electricity VAT cut worth to you?

Forget £45. It is an average for a household that may look nothing like yours. VAT is proportional, so your saving scales with what you spend.

Your bills from 1 October already carry no VAT on electricity. The VAT you would otherwise pay is 5% of that, or one twentieth.

The sum

Add up what you expect to spend on electricity from 1 October 2026 to 30 June 2027, then divide by 20. That is roughly what the electricity VAT cut is worth to you over the nine months it runs.

If you are working from an old bill that still had VAT on it, divide by 21 instead, because 5% VAT is one twenty-first of a VAT inclusive price.

Two things follow. If you have gas central heating and modest electricity use, you keep well under £45 a year. If you heat with electricity, run a heat pump, charge an electric car at home, or use a lot of power for a medical need, you keep more.

The cut is worth most to the households with the biggest electricity bills. Because VAT is a flat percentage, the largest cash gains go to the largest users, whatever their income. If you already have solar panels, the same logic makes self use more valuable, which is the case for sending surplus generation to the immersion heater instead of the grid.

One check on Ofgem's £45. A typical home on October rates spends about £858 a year on electricity: 2,500 kWh at 26.32p plus 365 days at 54.83p. One twentieth of that is about £43. So £45 is a fair round figure for a full year at typical use.

6. Worked example: the Rahman household

Three bedroom terrace, Watford

Two adults and two children, gas central heating, slightly above typical electricity use. From their own past bills, roughly 3,000 kWh of electricity and 8,900 kWh of gas a year. About £555 of their electricity spend falls between October and March, and about £215 between April and June.

What the VAT cut is worth to them. £555 + £215 = £770. £770 ÷ 20 = £38.50 across the nine months. Without the Budget extension it would have been £555 ÷ 20, about £28.

Their electricity, year on year. At July rates, 3,000 × 26.11p plus 365 × 57.19p is £992. At October rates, 3,000 × 26.32p plus 365 × 54.83p is £990. Essentially flat, and that is the VAT cut doing its job.

Their gas, year on year. At July rates, 8,900 × 7.33p plus 365 × 29.04p is £758. At October rates, 8,900 × 7.97p plus 365 × 29.68p is £818. That is £59 more, and all of the increase is gas.

Net position: about £57 a year worse off. The VAT relief never shows as a credit. It sits inside lower electricity unit rates they will not notice. With 5% VAT on that £990 of electricity, they would be about £106 a year worse off instead.

The kWh figures are this household's own usage, used to show the method. The April to June figure assumes October rates hold, which nobody knows yet because Ofgem sets the April cap on 23 February 2027. Run the same sums on your own last twelve months rather than the headline.

If you want to know where those units go, the two cheapest changes in most kitchens are dropping the wash from 40 to 30 degrees and using the microwave instead of the oven for small portions.

This is not a reason to be cynical about the policy. It is a reason to stop waiting for it to fix anything.

7. What should you do before the January 2027 cap?

Know the date. Ofgem announces the cap for 1 January to 31 March 2027 on 25 November 2026. As of 9 November it has not been published, so any figure you see now is a forecast, not a cap. Our guide on whether to fix now or wait for the 25 November announcement walks through that choice.

Check your opening meter reading. If your supplier estimated across 30 September, an estimate can apply October rates to units you used in September. If the figure looks wrong, send a photo of the meter.

Check your payment method. Ofgem's October levels are £1,723 on direct debit, £1,678 on prepayment and £1,861 on standard credit, which means paying when the bill arrives. That is £138 a year between direct debit and standard credit for the same energy. It is worth about three times the VAT cut and it is free to fix.

Do not fix a tariff purely because of the VAT news. Any fixed deal already prices in 0% VAT for as long as it lasts, and it prices in 5% coming back on 1 July 2027. You are not getting ahead of anything.

Put 1 July 2027 in your diary. That is the day your electricity gets about 5% dearer unless the zero rate is extended again. Our October 2026 price cap guide covers the fix or stay decision with the full rates.

Frequently asked questions about the electricity VAT cut

Do I need to apply for the electricity VAT cut? No. There is no form, no claim and no deadline. Your supplier applies the zero rate to electricity supplied from 1 October 2026 to 30 June 2027. Fixed tariff customers get it too, Ofgem says. If anyone offers to claim it for you, treat it as a scam and do not share bank details.

Is VAT on electricity 5% or 20%? For homes it is normally 5%, and from 1 October 2026 to 30 June 2027 it is 0% in Great Britain. Businesses pay the standard 20% rate unless their use is under HMRC's de minimis limit of about 1,000 kWh a month, or they are a charity using power for non-business purposes.

Did my bill go down on 1 October? For most dual fuel homes, no. The VAT cut held the electricity rate almost flat, but the gas unit rate rose 8.7% and pushed the overall cap up 4% to £1,723. All electric homes using under about 4,100 kWh a year did pay slightly less than in July.

Does the electricity VAT cut apply in Northern Ireland? No. HMRC says the zero rate covers England, Scotland and Wales only. Qualifying electricity in Northern Ireland stays at the reduced 5% rate. Northern Ireland also has its own energy regulator, so the Ofgem cap figures in this guide do not apply there either.

Two more dates sit around this one. September CPI came out on 21 October at 3.5%, and that figure sets most benefit rates for April. A week later came the 28 October Budget and what it actually changed, including this extension.
