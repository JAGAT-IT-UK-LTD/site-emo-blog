1. What Google's dashboard actually shows for 2026

Google runs a public Search Status Dashboard. Its ranking history page lists every confirmed ranking update with a start date and how long it took to roll out. It is the only primary source for whether an update happened, and it says this for 2026:

Two core updates, three spam updates, one Discover update. The May core update started on 21 May and ran for just under 12 days, so it completed on or about 2 June. Since then Google has confirmed two spam updates and nothing else. For comparison, 2025 had core updates in March, June and December, so a gap from June to September is not unusual; the December 2025 core update ran for 18 days.

That is the whole of what is confirmed. Everything else written about August is inference from third party tracking tools, which measure volatility in results but cannot tell you what caused it.

2. Core update versus spam update, and why the response differs

Google describes the two differently and the difference is not academic.

A core update is a broad reassessment of how Google's systems judge content overall. Sites move up or down without having done anything wrong, because the yardstick moved. Google's published guidance for a core update is to keep making the content more helpful and to expect that recovery, if it comes, may take until the next core update. There is no fix to apply, because nothing is broken.

A spam update improves the systems that detect violations of Google's spam policies: scaled content abuse, site reputation abuse, expired domain abuse, cloaking, link schemes and the rest. A site hit by a spam update has, in Google's assessment, broken a specific published rule. Recovery means finding the violation, fixing it, and waiting for the systems to recrawl. Rewriting your honest reviews does nothing, because they were never the problem. The clearest example is the rule Google tightened in August 2026, and how the site reputation abuse policy now reads for affiliate pages is the version that matters.

So a site whose traffic fell around 18 to 21 August faces a fork. If it fell because of the spam update, the question is which spam policy it is tripping, and the usual suspects for affiliate sites are scaled content produced without oversight and site reputation abuse, meaning third party content hosted on a domain to borrow its authority. If it fell for some other reason, a technical fault, a competitor's new page, a seasonal dip, a change in AI Overviews, then no update is involved and the fix is specific to the cause. Neither branch is "rewrite everything".

3. So what moved in August?

Three things happened in August that can move an affiliate site's traffic, and only one of them is on the dashboard.

The spam update, 18 to 21 August, is confirmed. Sites running large volumes of near-identical product pages, or hosting third party coupon or review content on their domain, are the ones Google's spam policies name. If that is you, the dashboard has told you what to look at.

Tracking tools reported elevated volatility in the first half of August, before the spam update started. Google confirmed nothing for that period. The SEO trade press debated whether it was an unconfirmed core update. It was not confirmed, which for practical purposes means the same as it not being a core update: there is no announced yardstick change to respond to.

And AI Overviews and AI Mode continued to take clicks from informational queries throughout the summer, independently of any update. We measured that effect on affiliate traffic in the guide linked below, and for many UK sites it is a larger and steadier drain than any single algorithm change.

4. A worked example: a Hampshire garden tools site

Take a small UK affiliate site reviewing garden tools, run from Hampshire, with organic traffic of 20,000 visits a month in July. In the last ten days of August it falls to a run rate of 14,000. The owner reads that a core update has hit affiliate sites and starts rewriting the ten best-performing reviews.

Stop there and open the dashboard. The only confirmed update in that window is the spam update. So the first question is not "is my content helpful enough" but "am I doing anything on Google's spam policy list". The owner has 40 hand-written reviews with original photos. She also, since June, has been hosting a coupon section supplied by a third party network, on her own domain, with 600 auto-generated pages. That is the shape Google's site reputation abuse policy describes.

The right response is to remove or noindex the coupon section and wait, not to touch the reviews. Rewriting the ten best pages would have destroyed the ranking history of the pages that were never the problem, to fix a problem they did not cause. Had the dashboard shown a core update instead, the response would have been different again: leave the site alone, improve what is genuinely thin, and expect no movement until the next core update.

The 30% fall is real either way. The dashboard does not make it smaller. It stops the owner making it permanent.

5. The five checks before you change a word

First, the dashboard: was there a confirmed update in the window when traffic moved, and what kind? Second, Search Console: did clicks fall across the whole site, or on a handful of pages, and did impressions fall with them? A fall in clicks with steady impressions is a click-through problem, often AI Overviews, not a ranking problem. Third, Search Console's manual actions and security reports: a spam issue serious enough may show there. Fourth, the spam policies page, read against your own site with an honest eye, especially anything scaled, syndicated or hosted for a third party. Fifth, the calendar: August is a soft month for many UK niches regardless, and the year-on-year figure tells you more than the month-on-month one.

Only when all five are done does rewriting content become a candidate, and even then it should be the thin pages, not the strong ones.

6. The honest verdict

There has been no confirmed Google core update since early June. If your August traffic moved, the confirmed cause on offer is a spam update, and that points at policy violations, not at the quality of your honest pages.

Do not rewrite your best content in response to an unconfirmed update. That is how a temporary dip becomes a permanent one.

Bookmark the dashboard and read it before you read anyone's analysis. It is free, it is Google's, and there is nothing to buy here. We have no tool to sell you and would say the same if we did.
