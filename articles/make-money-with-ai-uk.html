Selling AI-written blog posts stopped working, because AI destroyed the price of exactly that. Meanwhile UK businesses are paying £75 to £150 an hour for someone to set AI up properly. Here is where the money actually moved in 2026, four honest routes to a first £1,000, and how long each really takes.

The short version

  • The obvious route is the dead one. Rates for basic blog posts, simple logos and data entry have collapsed, because AI does a passable version in seconds. Selling that is competing with a free tool.
  • The money moved to implementation. UK freelancers who set up repeatable AI workflows for businesses charge roughly £75 to £150 an hour, or package it as a £500 to £2,000 setup sprint.
  • Day rates are real. AI consulting sits around £500 to £1,200 a day, and SEO or UX specialists who use AI well are on £400 to £800 a day.

1. Why the obvious AI money disappeared

The instinct is reasonable: AI writes fast, so sell writing. The problem is that everybody had the same instinct, including your prospective client.

Rates for the work AI does easily, generic blog posts, simple logos, basic data entry, transcription cleanup, have fallen hard. A business owner with a free chatbot can produce something adequate in ninety seconds. They will not pay you £40 for it, because they know what it took.

This is the part the "make money with AI" videos skip. AI did not create a market for selling AI output. It destroyed the market for anything AI can do unsupervised.

If a client can get 80% of your service for free in under a minute, you do not have a business. You have a demonstration.

2. Where the money actually went

It moved one level up: from producing output to making the tools work inside a business. That is the gap, because most small firms know AI exists, have tried it once, and have no idea how to make it reliable or repeatable.

3. Four routes to a first £1,000, with real timelines

Route A: the small business AI audit and setup

Find one local business drowning in repetitive admin, quotes, invoices, enquiry replies, social posts. Spend a day mapping what they repeat, then build three or four templates and processes that use AI properly, and write it down so their staff can follow it. If that is the hustle you want, the sums are already worked through in what it costs to start teaching AI tools to local businesses.

Charge £400 to £800 for the first one while you have no track record. Two of those is your £1,000. Realistic timeline: 4 to 8 weeks, most of it spent finding the first client, not doing the work.

Route B: AI-assisted service in a field you already know

If you already have a skill, bookkeeping, recruitment, property, teaching, sell that skill delivered faster with AI, rather than selling AI. Your domain knowledge is the moat. AI just doubles your output. There is a worked version of exactly that route in a 30-day AI-assisted freelance writing plan built for under 50 pounds.

Timeline: 3 to 6 weeks, and the fastest route if you already have any professional background.

Route C: fixing what AI got wrong

A growing, unglamorous market. Businesses published AI content that is generic, inaccurate or damaging their search visibility, and now need a human to audit and repair it. Same for AI-generated code and spreadsheets nobody understands.

Timeline: 4 to 8 weeks. Easier to sell than it sounds, because the client already knows they have a problem.

Route D: productised templates or tooling

Build one genuinely useful thing, a prompt system, a spreadsheet, a small automation, and sell it repeatedly. Lower price, no client calls, but it needs an audience, which is the slow part.

Timeline: 3 to 6 months to £1,000. Be honest with yourself about this one.

Worked example: Nadia's first £1,000 in seven weeks

Nadia works in admin and has no design or coding background. She picks Route A.

Weeks 1 to 2. She practises on her own workplace's repetitive tasks and documents what she built: an enquiry reply system, a quote template, a weekly report. No client yet, but she now has a real before-and-after to show.

Week 3. She approaches five local businesses she already buys from. Not a cold email blast, five people who recognise her name. One says yes to a paid trial.

Weeks 4 to 5. A dog groomer, drowning in enquiry messages. She builds a reply system, a booking confirmation flow and a simple monthly social plan, then writes a two page guide so the owner can run it without her. £450 invoiced.

Weeks 6 to 7. The groomer refers a nearby beautician. Same work, now faster because she has done it once, and she charges more. £600 invoiced.

Total: £1,050 in seven weeks. Roughly 40 hours of actual work, so about £26 an hour once you include the unpaid learning and selling. Her third client, priced properly, is where it starts looking like a real rate.

4. Getting the first client with no portfolio

  1. Solve it for yourself or your employer first. A documented before-and-after from a real business, even your own workplace, beats any certificate.
  2. Start with people who already know you. The gym, the barber, the takeaway, the accountant. Recognition removes most of the risk in their mind.
  3. Lead with their problem, not the technology. "You are answering the same five questions all day" lands. "I do AI automation" does not.
  4. Offer a small paid pilot, not a free trial. Free work attracts people who will never pay. £150 for one process, then more if it works.
  5. Leave them something they own. A written process document turns a one-off into a referral.

5. What to charge, and how to say the number

Price the outcome, not your hours. "Two hours of my time" invites haggling. "This saves you around six hours a week, the setup is £600" is a business case.

Starting out, sit at the bottom of the market range and move up every second client. Going from £400 to £600 to £800 across three jobs is normal and expected. Our freelance rate guide covers the maths of what you actually need to charge to make self-employment worth it.

One rule: never discount to win the first client. Reduce the scope instead. A cheap client sets a cheap anchor and tells everyone they refer you what you cost.

6. The tax and disclosure bit

Two things to handle from your first invoice.

Tax. Once your total trading income passes £1,000 in a tax year you need to register for Self Assessment, which is exactly the threshold this article is about. Full detail in our side hustle tax guide. Set aside a share of every payment from day one rather than finding the bill in January.

Honesty with clients. Tell them AI is part of your process. Most do not mind at all, they are hiring your judgement. Hiding it and being found out ends the relationship and the referrals. And check whatever you produce, because you are professionally responsible for it, not the tool.

7. What to avoid entirely

Courses promising £10,000 a month. The person selling the course found a more reliable business model than the one they are teaching: selling the course.

Mass-produced AI content sites. Search engines have got noticeably better at spotting bulk low-value pages. It is a lot of work for a coin flip.

Anything requiring upfront payment for the privilege of working. Legitimate clients pay you. Reversed, it is a scam.

Reselling raw AI output as your own service. Not because it is immoral, though tell your client, but because it is commercially fragile. That price only goes one way.

8. The honest verdict

A first £1,000 from AI in the UK is realistic. It is not fast, it is not passive, and it will not come from selling AI-generated text.

It comes from picking one repetitive problem in one real business, solving it properly, documenting it, and charging £400 to £800 for the outcome. Do that twice and the £1,000 is done. Do it five times and you have a rate, referrals and something that compounds.

The skill that pays is not prompting. It is understanding a business well enough to know what is worth automating, and being accountable for the result.

Frequently asked

Do I need to be technical? No. The most sellable version of this is process understanding plus clear documentation. If you can map how a business repeats itself and write instructions people follow, that is the job.

Which AI tools should I learn? One general assistant well, rather than twelve badly. Clients are not buying a tool list, and the tools change every few months anyway.

Can I do this alongside a full-time job? Yes, and most people should start that way. Two clients at 4 to 6 hours each is an evening-and-weekend workload. Just check your employment contract for any restriction on outside work.

Is it too late? No, but the easy version has gone. Generic AI services are saturated. Specific, local, accountable implementation is not, because it does not scale for the big players.
