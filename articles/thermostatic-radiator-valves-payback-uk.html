Eight Valves, One Cold Spare Room, and the Sum Nobody Does

Thermostatic radiator valves are the cheapest heating control in the house and the most often mis-sold. The saving is real, but it comes from the rooms you stop heating, not from the valve itself. Here is the arithmetic at the October 2026 price cap, and the three very different prices you can pay for the same idea.

The short version

  • A TRV does not save gas. Closing down a room you were heating for no reason saves gas. The valve is just what makes that easy to do and easy to keep doing.
  • Two little-used rooms taken down to a holding temperature is worth roughly 880 kWh a year on typical gas use, which is £70 a year at 7.97p per kWh.
  • Swapping heads onto existing valve bodies costs about £120 for eight and pays back in under two years. This is the version almost nobody is told about.
  • A full valve replacement on all eight radiators runs around £370 fitted and pays back in about five and a quarter years. Still worth doing, just not fast.
  • Smart TRVs at £400 to £500 for the house take eight years or more unless your heating pattern is genuinely irregular. For a house where everyone leaves at the same time, they are a gadget.

1. What a TRV actually does, and does not do

A thermostatic radiator valve is a temperature-sensitive tap. A wax or liquid capsule inside the head expands as the room warms, pushing a pin down and throttling the flow of hot water through that one radiator. When the room cools, the capsule contracts and the valve opens again.

Note what is missing from that description. The valve has no idea what the boiler is doing, what time it is, or whether anyone is home. It cannot turn the heating on or off. It cannot make the boiler run more efficiently on its own. It controls one radiator, by temperature, and nothing else.

That matters because of how TRVs are usually sold. The claim is that fitting them saves you money. The truthful version is that fitting them gives you the ability to stop heating rooms you were heating pointlessly, and that saves money. If you fit eight TRVs and set them all to 5, you have spent a few hundred pounds and saved nothing at all.

The Energy Saving Trust figure people quote, around £100 a year, is for a programmer, a room thermostat and TRVs working together in a typical three-bedroom semi on gas. It is not a TRV-only figure, and quoting it as one is the most common piece of sloppiness in this corner of the internet. If you already have a working programmer and room thermostat, the extra from TRVs alone is a fraction of that hundred pounds, and where the fraction lands depends entirely on how many rooms you genuinely stop heating.

2. The saving, calculated at the October 2026 cap

Here is the arithmetic in full, so you can substitute your own numbers.

Every figure below uses the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: gas at 7.97p per kWh including VAT at 5% and electricity at 26.32p per kWh with no VAT, because the zero rate on electricity runs from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. State that rate if you quote these numbers anywhere, because the next cap is announced on 25 November 2026 and the arithmetic moves with it.

Ofgem's typical gas household uses 11,500 kWh a year. Hot water and cooking take a slice of that, so call space heating roughly 85%, which is 9,775 kWh.

Now the honest part. Suppose you have eight radiators and two of them sit in rooms you barely use: a spare bedroom and a dining room that has become a place where laundry goes to think. Those two rooms are perhaps 15% of the house's heat demand, so about 1,466 kWh a year. Taking them from fully heated down to a frost-holding setting cuts roughly 60% of that, which is 880 kWh.

At 7.97p per kWh, 880 kWh is £70.13 a year.

That is the number. It is not spectacular and it is not nothing. It is also honest, which is more than can be said for most of the claims made about these valves. If you have four rarely used rooms rather than two, double it. If you live in a two-bed flat where every room is occupied every evening, the saving is close to zero and you should not buy TRVs at all.

3. Three ways to fit them, three prices

Option one, heads only. If your radiators already have TRV bodies fitted, and most UK homes built or refurbished since the mid-1990s do, you can unscrew the old head and screw a new one on without draining anything. Look at the valve: if it has a numbered dial that turns, it is a TRV body. Replacement heads are a low-cost item and the job takes about ten minutes per radiator with no tools beyond a pair of hands. Budget around £120 for eight.

Option two, full valve replacement. If your radiators have plain on-off wheel valves, the valve body itself has to be changed, which means draining the system down, cutting in, and refilling and rebalancing afterwards. The Energy Saving Trust puts adding TRVs to all radiators at around £370 where a programmer and thermostat are already in place. That is a plumber for most of a day.

Option three, smart TRVs. These add a motor, a radio, a schedule and usually a hub. Per radiator they cost several times a manual head, so a whole house lands somewhere around £400 to £500. They are genuinely useful in one situation: a household with irregular occupancy, where different rooms need heat at different and unpredictable times. In a house where everyone leaves at eight and comes back at six, a manual valve set once does the same job for a tenth of the money.

Worked example: the Whitfield household, Dewsbury

Three-bedroom semi, gas combi, eight radiators, two adults and a teenager. Gas use is a little above typical at 12,400 kWh a year, so space heating is roughly 10,540 kWh.

The box room has been a box room since 2019 and the front room gets used at Christmas. Between them they are about 18% of the heat demand, so 1,897 kWh. Dropping both to a holding setting cuts around 65% of that: 1,233 kWh.

At the October 2026 capped gas rate of 7.97p per kWh, that is £98.27 a year.

Their radiators already have TRV bodies, the dials just seized years ago. Eight new heads cost them about £120. Payback: £120 divided by £98.27, or 1.2 years, one winter and a bit.

They also looked at smart valves at roughly £450. Same saving, payback 4.6 years, on hardware with batteries in it. They bought the heads and spent the difference on loft insulation, which was the right call.

5. The four mistakes that cancel the saving

Fitting a TRV in the same room as the main thermostat. The two controls fight each other. The TRV closes the radiator, the room cools, the thermostat calls for heat, and the boiler runs to warm a room whose radiator is shut. Leave that one radiator on a plain valve, fully open. This is in every installation guide and ignored in roughly half of installations.

Treating the numbers as temperatures. The dial is not degrees. Setting 3 does not mean three degrees. Most manufacturers put 3 at roughly 20°C, but the only reliable method is to set it, wait a day, and adjust.

Setting unused rooms to zero. Take them down, do not shut them off. A completely unheated room in a British winter grows condensation on the cold wall it shares with a heated room, and condensation becomes mould, and mould costs more than the gas you saved. The frost setting exists for exactly this.

Covering the head. A TRV senses the air immediately around it. Behind a long curtain, under a shelf, or tucked behind a sofa, it reads a pocket of warm trapped air, decides the room is hot, and shuts the radiator while you sit there cold. If the valve is boxed in, a remote-sensor version exists and is worth the extra.

6. The honest verdict

If your radiators already have TRV bodies and the dials are stiff or broken, replace the heads. At roughly £120 for a house and a payback inside two winters, it is one of the few home energy jobs where the sum is not close.

If you have plain valves and would need a plumber, it is still worth doing at around five years payback, but do it when the system is being drained for something else, such as a new radiator or a powerflush, and the labour cost largely disappears.

If you live somewhere every room is used every day, do not buy them. There is no saving to capture and no amount of clever hardware creates one.

Frequently asked

Do TRVs make the boiler work harder? No. Closing radiators reduces the flow through the system, and on a modern condensing boiler that generally lowers the return temperature, which improves efficiency. On an older system with no bypass it can cause the pump to labour, which is why you leave one radiator without a TRV.

Can I fit TRV heads myself? Heads onto existing bodies, yes, with no tools and no draining. Valve bodies, no, unless you are comfortable draining and refilling a sealed heating system and rebalancing it afterwards.

What setting should unused rooms be on? The frost or star setting, or 1. That keeps the room above roughly 8°C, which prevents pipes freezing and keeps condensation off the shared walls without heating the room properly.

Are smart TRVs worth it for a rented flat? Rarely. They are expensive, they need a hub, and you would want to take them with you. Manual heads are cheap, reversible and you can refit the originals when you leave.
