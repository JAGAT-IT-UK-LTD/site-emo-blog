Mortgage rates after Bank Rate rose to 4%: waiting did not pay, and the 7.13% SVR still settles it

1. Where rates stand, from the primary source

Moneyfacts gave its latest averages on 5 November 2026, the day Bank Rate rose to 4%. The average two year fix rose from 5.59% at the start of September to 5.97% at the start of November. The average five year fix rose from 5.63% to 6.00%. The Moneyfacts Average New Mortgage Rate reached 5.89%, which Moneyfacts calls a three year high.

Two other numbers from the same article matter more than the averages. The average standard variable rate, the rate you revert to when a fix ends and nothing replaces it, was 7.13% on 1 November. And the average two year tracker was 4.68%, more than a point below the average two year fix.

Lenders kept repricing fast. Moneyfacts' Treasury Report for October found mortgage deals stayed on the market for 13 days on average, down from 14 in September. The record low was eight days in April 2026.

The Bank of England sees the same move in its own data. Its November Monetary Policy Report says quoted rates on two year fixes at 75% and 90% loan to value rose by around 60 basis points on average since the July Report. Mortgage approvals for house purchase fell by 8% in the three months to September.

When this guide was first published on 4 September, the latest Moneyfacts report was from 10 August. It showed a two year fix at 5.63%, a five year at 5.66% and the SVR already at 7.13%. It also quoted the Bank of England's estimate that nearly 750,000 households on fixes under 3% reach the end of their deal this year, facing rises of about £170 a month on average.

2. The three choices, priced

Take a £180,000 repayment mortgage with 25 years to run. Our arithmetic, standard annuity formula, no fees, using Moneyfacts averages:

4.68%, average two year tracker, 1 November£1,019£137 less

Choice one, secure a rate now. You pay about £1,156 from the day the old deal ends, at the average. Most lenders let you apply up to six months ahead and, crucially, let you drop the offer for a cheaper one before completion. So securing a rate is not a bet against rates falling. It is insurance against them rising, at no cost.

Choice two, wait for 17 December and fix afterwards. This is what waiting for 17 September looked like, and it did not pay. Borrowers who held off from early September to November saw the average two year fix rise by 0.38 points, about £41 a month on this loan. The gain from waiting is capped. The loss is not.

Choice three, let it roll onto the SVR while you think. This is the one people do by accident. Every month at 7.13% instead of 5.97% costs about £131 on this loan. Lenders can also pass the November rise on to their SVR, which would make it worse.

A fourth option is now worth a look: a tracker. At 4.68% the average two year tracker is £137 a month cheaper on this loan today. Moneyfacts notes many trackers have no early repayment charges. The catch is plain: it moves with Bank Rate, and the market curve in the Bank's Report rises to around 4.9% at the end of 2027.

3. What the September and November decisions did to your rate

On 17 September the Bank held Bank Rate at 3.75% by 6 to 3. On 5 November it raised it to 4% by 8 to 1, with one member wanting 4.25%. We cover both decisions in what the rise to 4% on 5 November means.

Fixed mortgage rates are priced off swap rates, which reflect where the market expects Bank Rate to be over two or five years, not where it is today. That is why the September hold did not bring fixes down. Swaps were already pricing a rise. Moneyfacts says many lenders pre-empted the November decision when setting their fixed prices.

A tracker is different: it follows Bank Rate directly, so trackers rose by 0.25 points after 5 November. On a standard variable rate your lender decides whether to pass the rise on. If you are sitting on the SVR waiting, a further rise would push 7.13% higher, not lower.

4. A worked example in Slough

The Okoro family in Slough had £180,000 left on a mortgage fixed at 2.49% until 31 October 2026, 25 years remaining. In early September their lender's SVR was 7.13% and its best two year fix for their loan to value was 5.63%. What the Committee signals matters as much as what it decides, and how a hawkish vote split feeds through to fixed rates is the part that moves pricing before any announcement does.

Here is how each route has played out, now that both decisions are known.

Route A. They applied on 5 September for the 5.63% deal to start on 1 November. Their payment went from £807 to £1,119, a £312 a month rise, which is the real story and no decision changed it. Against today's average two year fix of 5.97%, that early lock is worth about £37 a month, or £889 over the two years. Cost of holding the option: nothing.

Route B. They waited for the September decision, then applied on 20 September. Bank Rate was held, but fixed rates kept rising. Their deal still ended on 31 October, and a remortgage typically needs four to six weeks, so they cut it fine. If the application slipped into November they paid £1,287 for a month, £168 more than Route A.

Route C. They did nothing until the SVR bill landed. That is £480 more a month than they were used to. Every month of indecision now costs about £131 against an average fix of 5.97%, and £168 against the deal they could have had in September.

The Okoros' honest risk was never the Bank. It was the calendar.

5. Who should wait, who should not

Do not wait if your deal ends inside three months. Secure a rate, keep the right to switch, and let the Bank do what it does on 17 December.

Waiting is defensible only if your deal ends after May 2027, because you cannot secure a rate more than about six months out anyway, and you lose nothing by watching.

Never wait on the SVR. At 7.13% against 5.97%, the revert rate is the most expensive place to be undecided. A further rise in Bank Rate makes it worse, not better.

And the number to hold onto: on this loan a quarter point is worth about £27 a month. The gap between a 2.49% deal that ends and a 5.97% deal that replaces it is £350. Shopping across lenders and using a broker moves the second number. Nothing you do moves the first.

6. The honest verdict

If your fix ends in the next six months, apply now and keep the right to switch. It is free, and a month on the 7.13% SVR costs more than any plausible fall in rates would save.

Waiting for the Bank did not pay this autumn. The average two year fix rose from 5.59% to 5.97% between early September and 1 November, about £41 a month on £180,000. Budget for the jump from your old deal. Do not gamble on the next vote.

We do not know the 17 December vote and neither does anyone selling you a prediction. The figures here are Moneyfacts' published averages, the Bank of England's own documents and our own arithmetic, nothing else.

Frequently asked

Can I lock a mortgage rate before my current deal ends? Most lenders let existing customers secure a product transfer rate up to six months before the deal ends, and most allow you to switch to a cheaper deal if one appears before completion. Terms vary, so check your own lender's rules in writing before you rely on the switch.

Did fixed rates fall after the 17 September decision? No. The Bank held at 3.75%, but Moneyfacts records the average two year fix rising from 5.59% at the start of September to 5.97% on 1 November. Swap rates were pricing a rise, which came on 5 November. Borrowers who waited for September paid more, not less.

What is the standard variable rate? The rate your lender charges when a fixed or tracker deal ends and you have not moved to another. Moneyfacts put the average at 7.13% on 1 November 2026, the same level as in August. Your own lender's SVR may differ, and it can change when Bank Rate moves.

Two year or five year fix? On 1 November 2026 the Moneyfacts averages were 5.97% for two years and 6.00% for five, so almost identical. That is a personal choice about certainty against flexibility, and we are not advisers. A broker can compare your actual quotes and fees.

Is a tracker mortgage worth it now? Moneyfacts put the average two year tracker at 4.68% on 1 November, more than a point below the average two year fix, and many have no early repayment charges. The risk is that it rises with Bank Rate. The market curve in the Bank's November Report rises to around 4.9% by the end of 2027.
