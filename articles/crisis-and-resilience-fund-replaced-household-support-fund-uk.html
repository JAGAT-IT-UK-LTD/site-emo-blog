If Someone Told You the Household Support Fund Was Finished, They Were Half Right

1. What actually changed on 1 April

The Household Support Fund ran from October 2021 in a series of short extensions, six months here, twelve months there. Its final round covered 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026, with £742 million for local authorities in England. Then it stopped.

What replaced it is the Crisis and Resilience Fund, launched in April 2026 and split across local authorities throughout England. DWP describes it as the first time a multi-year settlement has been in place for crisis support, replacing what it calls the annual cliff-edge funding cycle. Funding is confirmed to 31 March 2029.

The headline figure is £1 billion, including Barnett consequential, announced in the Spending Review. Councils had to submit a delivery plan covering the full period by 1 July 2026, so your council has already decided broadly how it is spending the money.

The practical difference for a household is not the total. It is the certainty. A council running a one-year pot tends to hold money back and hand out supermarket vouchers in the last quarter. A council with a three-year settlement can commission an advice service that is still there next winter.

2. The four things the money can be spent on

The guidance sets out four components. Councils decide the proportions themselves, and those proportions can vary between years.

ElementWhat it is for
Crisis PaymentSupport for people facing a financial crisis now. This is the closest equivalent to the old Household Support Fund payment or voucher.
Housing PaymentFinancial support towards housing costs. This is where Discretionary Housing Payments now sit.
Resilience ServicesFunding for services that improve financial resilience, for example debt advice or help to claim what you are entitled to.
Community CoordinationInvestment in joining up the local support landscape, so the food bank, the advice service and the council are not three separate journeys.

That third and fourth element are the real change of direction. The old fund was overwhelmingly cash and vouchers after the fact. This one is explicitly meant to spend money before the crisis as well as during it. Whether it does that in your area depends entirely on the delivery plan your council filed in July.

A fund with four names and no national application form is a fund most people will never claim. The money is real. The signposting is not.

3. The Discretionary Housing Payment change nobody announced loudly

This is the part worth knowing if you have ever had help with a rent shortfall.

Discretionary Housing Payments were a separate thing. You applied to your council when Housing Benefit or the housing element of Universal Credit did not cover your rent, and they had their own grant. From April 2026, in England, that funding is folded into the Crisis and Resilience Fund as the Housing Payment element.

DWP has said that in the years ending March 2027 and March 2028, councils are expected to maintain existing spending on Housing Payments, using their Discretionary Housing Payment allocations for the year ending March 2026 as the guide. So the money is protected in the short term. It is not guaranteed forever: from year three the split between the four elements is a local decision. If you rely on a rent top up, watch that rather than assume it.

In Wales, Discretionary Housing Payments continue as before. Local welfare provision is devolved, so Scotland and Northern Ireland run their own arrangements.

Worked example: the Ashworth household in Barnsley

Two adults, one working part time, two children at primary school. In February 2026 they received a £150 supermarket voucher from the Household Support Fund after their fridge freezer failed. They assumed that pot was gone for good and did not look again.

What they missed: a Crisis Payment is the direct successor to that voucher, and it is available for the same kinds of need. Nothing about their circumstances stopped in April. Only the name did.

What is new for them: the Resilience Services element. Their council may now fund a benefits check or debt advice service. On a part time wage with two children, a proper entitlement check is often worth more per year than any one off payment, because it finds things like council tax reduction that keep paying every month.

What it costs them to find out: one search for their council's name and the words crisis support, and one phone call. That is the whole barrier.

4. How to actually ask

Start at GOV.UK's cost of living page, which routes you to your own council. There is no national form and no national helpline, because the fund is administered locally.

Search your council's site for more than one phrase. Councils are not consistent. Try Crisis and Resilience Fund, crisis support, local welfare assistance, household support and discretionary housing payment. Some councils have not renamed their public pages at all.

Ask even if you are working. The old fund was never limited to people on benefits, and the new one is aimed at preventing people falling into crisis, not only at those already in it. Councils set their own criteria.

Ask about the advice service, not just the payment. If your council funds a Resilience Services partner, a full benefits check through them is free and often finds recurring money rather than a one off. And if you are refused, ask what else is available: Council Tax Reduction, free school meals and the Warm Home Discount are separate schemes with separate rules.

5. The honest verdict

This is a better structure than what it replaced. Three years of certainty lets councils commission something that lasts, and folding Discretionary Housing Payments in removes one separate application from a system that had far too many.

The weakness is visibility. The Household Support Fund took four years to become a name people recognised, and it has been replaced by a name almost nobody knows, with no national application, no national branding, and 150 local variations. A household that needs help in November 2026 will search for the old name and find a page telling them it closed.

So the useful action is small and specific. Look up your own council, use several search terms, and ask. The money exists until at least March 2029. Whether it reaches you depends mostly on whether you ask for it.

Frequently asked

Is this available in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland? No. The Crisis and Resilience Fund applies to England only. Local welfare provision is devolved, and Discretionary Housing Payments continue to exist in Wales.

Do I have to be on benefits? Not necessarily. Councils set their own eligibility criteria within the guidance, and the fund is explicitly meant to prevent households falling into crisis as well as respond to one. Ask rather than assume.

What happened to my Discretionary Housing Payment? In England it is now the Housing Payment element of this fund. Councils were expected to maintain existing spending levels in the years ending March 2027 and March 2028, using their previous allocations as a guide.

How much can I get? There is no national amount, and any figure quoted as a national one is wrong. Awards are set locally and vary by council and by circumstance.
