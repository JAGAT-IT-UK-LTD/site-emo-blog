Black Friday 2026 Fake Discounts: How To Check A Real Deal

1. Are Black Friday deals actually cheaper?

Usually not by as much as the sticker says. Which?, the consumer group, published its latest research on 25 November 2025. It tracked 175 home, tech and health appliances at Amazon, AO, Argos, Boots, Currys, John Lewis, Richer Sounds and Very.

It followed prices for six months before and six months after the 2024 sales period, from May 2024 to May 2025. It then compared each product with its price during the sales, which Which? set as 15 November to 12 December 2024.

The result: 83% of products were cheaper or the same price on at least one occasion outside those four weeks. On Black Friday itself, Which? found no deals at all at their cheapest price of the year.

That does not mean every deal is fake. Some prices do drop, and some products only dip in November. It means the word "deal" tells you nothing. The price history tells you everything.

2. What does UK law say about "was" prices?

The rules changed on 6 April 2025. The Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 were replaced by the unfair commercial practices provisions in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024. The CMA's guidance, CMA207, confirms they apply to practices from that date.

Misleading actions. Section 226 bans false or misleading information. It also covers information that is true but presented in a misleading way. A "was £899" that was only charged for a fortnight can be true and still mislead.

False urgency. Paragraph 7 of Schedule 20 bans outright any false claim that a product or price is only available for a limited time, made to rush your decision. The CMA's own example is a countdown clock that runs out and then quietly restarts.

Drip pricing. Section 230 says an invitation to buy must show the total price, including any fees, taxes or charges you will necessarily pay. Optional delivery charges must be stated too. The CMA's guidance says showing a headline price and adding mandatory charges later is prohibited.

The penalties have teeth now. CMA207 says breaches can bring fines of up to the higher of £300,000 or 10% of worldwide turnover.

For the deeper history of hidden fees, see our guide to drip pricing and the CMA's fines.

3. Which pricing tricks should I watch for?

The CMA spelled these out in an open letter to online businesses on 29 March 2023, with worked examples. The CMA still points to that letter in its current guidance. In a footnote it says the higher price "must be a genuine and realistic selling price", and that a business should be able to prove it.

TrickThe CMA's exampleWhy it misleads
Stale launch price£120 at launch in April, then £90 in May, June and JulyThe lower price is now the usual price
Hardly anyone paid it5 sold at £150, then 200 sold at £100The higher price was never a realistic selling price
Flip flopping£15 and £11 in alternate monthsThe higher price is no more usual than the lower one
Short spike£130 for months, one month at £160, back to £130The hike was temporary
Ancient priceA £30 price charged on one date a year earlierOne day's price is no basis for a comparison
Hidden condition"50% off", but only if you spend over £200The condition was not shown upfront

The CMA also warns that a fake discount plus a countdown clock plus a "low stock" banner is worse than any one alone. Each claim pushes you to act fast when you do not need to.

4. How do I check a deal in five minutes?

You do not need to trust anyone's "was" price. You need the price history and the total price. Here is the routine.

  1. Find the price history. Price comparison sites such as PriceSpy show a chart of past prices for many products. For Amazon, a tracker such as CamelCamelCamel does the same. Look at the last three to six months, not the last week.
  2. Find the usual price. Ask where the line sat for most of that time. That is your real comparison, not the highest point on the chart.
  3. Work out the real saving. Take the usual price, subtract today's price. That is what Black Friday is actually worth to you.
  4. Check the total at checkout. Delivery, installation, removal of an old appliance, or a card fee must be clear. If a mandatory fee appears only at the last step, that is the drip pricing the law bans.
  5. Ignore the clock. A countdown that resets, or a deal that appears again a week later, was never urgent.
  6. Compare one other retailer. The same model number at another shop is the quickest sanity check of all.

Set a price alert on anything you want but do not need today. The Which? figures suggest you will often see the same price again before May.

5. Worked example: the Begums in Leicester

The Begums want a new 55 inch television for Christmas. On Black Friday a retailer shows it at £599, "was £899, save £300". These sums are illustrative, not a real listing.

Aisha Begum opens a price history chart. It shows the set at £649 from June to September, £899 for two weeks in October, and £599 now.

Our own arithmetic, from those inputs:

ComparisonSumSaving
Against the "was" price the shop shows£899 minus £599£300, or 33%
Against the usual price on the chart£649 minus £599£50, or 7.7%

A two week spike to £899 looks like the CMA's "short spike" example. The real deal is £50, not £300.

That may still be worth having. But it changes the decision. A £50 saving does not justify a bigger set than they planned, or a rushed choice because a clock says 14 minutes left.

They pay the £599 on a credit card. As a single item over £100, it falls inside Section 75. If the set arrives faulty, or never arrives, the card company is jointly liable with the retailer.

They also note the delivery date. If they change their mind, their 14 days to cancel start the day after it arrives, not the day they ordered.

6. What if I regret it after Black Friday?

Buying online gives you rights a shop till does not. They come from the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013.

  • 14 days to change your mind. For goods, the cancellation period ends 14 days after the day the goods reach you. If one order arrives in parts, it runs from the last delivery.
  • Refund within 14 days. The retailer must refund within 14 days of getting the goods back, or of you showing proof you sent them, if that is earlier.
  • Delivery refunded too. The retailer must refund the standard delivery charge. If you paid extra for faster delivery, it only has to refund the cheapest standard option.
  • Return postage is usually yours. You pay the direct cost of sending it back, unless the retailer agreed to cover it or did not tell you before you bought.
  • Handle with care. If you used the item beyond what is needed to check it works, the retailer can deduct for the loss in value.

Some goods are excluded, such as items made to your specification or clearly personalised, under regulation 28.

Faulty goods are a separate right. Under section 22 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 you have 30 days to reject them for a refund. See our guide to the Consumer Rights Act 30 day refund rule.

Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes your credit card provider jointly liable for misrepresentation or breach of contract. It does not apply where the single item's cash price is £100 or less, or more than £30,000. Debit cards do not have Section 75, but a chargeback may help. Our Section 75 versus chargeback guide explains the difference.

If you think a price was misleading, GOV.UK says to contact Citizens Advice on 0808 223 1133 in England and Wales. It can pass complaints to Trading Standards and share them with the CMA.

7. The honest verdict

Black Friday is a date, not a discount. Which? found 83% of tracked products at the same price or lower at some other point in the year.

The "was" price is the part to doubt. UK law now says it must be a genuine, usual selling price, and the CMA can fine firms that break it. That does not stop every shop trying.

Check the history, then the total. Work out the saving against the usual price, not the highest one. If it is small, buy only what you would have bought anyway.

Pay by credit card for anything over £100, clear it in full, and keep the delivery date so you know your 14 days.

Frequently asked

When is Black Friday 2026 in the UK? Friday 27 November 2026, the day after US Thanksgiving. Cyber Monday is Monday 30 November. Many UK retailers start sales a week or more earlier and run them into December, which is why the Which? research used a four week sales period rather than one day.

Is it illegal to show a fake "was" price in the UK? It can be. Since 6 April 2025 the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 bans misleading actions, including true information presented misleadingly. The CMA says the higher price in a price reduction claim must be a genuine and realistic selling price, which the business can prove.

Are Black Friday deals cheaper than the January sales? Not reliably. Which? tracked prices to May 2025 and found 83% of products were the same price or cheaper at least once outside the Black Friday period. Some fell again after it. Set a price alert and compare, rather than assuming either sale is the low point.

Can I return a Black Friday purchase made online? Usually yes. Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations you can cancel within 14 days of the goods arriving, for any reason. The retailer must refund within 14 days of getting the goods back. You normally pay return postage unless the retailer says otherwise. Goods made to your specification or clearly personalised are excluded.

Does Section 75 cover Black Friday purchases? Yes, if you pay at least partly by credit card and the single item's cash price is over £100 and no more than £30,000. It covers misrepresentation and breach of contract, such as faulty goods or non delivery. It does not cover simply changing your mind.
