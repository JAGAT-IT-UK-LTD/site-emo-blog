A Small Employer Gets Back More Statutory Maternity Pay Than It Paid Out

Ruhul Amin · Published 16 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Employers can reclaim most of the statutory family payments they make, and small employers reclaim more than 100% of them. That is deliberate, it is not widely known, and the difference between the two recovery rates turns on a single figure from last year's payroll.

The short version

  • 109% recovery if your total Class 1 National Insurance for the previous tax year was £45,000 or lower.
  • 92% recovery if it was above £45,000.
  • The test is last year's NI bill, not this year's, and not your headcount or your turnover.
  • Statutory Sick Pay cannot be recovered at all. That is a flat rule with no small employer exception.
  • Six payments qualify: SMP, SPP, ShPP, SAP, SPBP and SNCP.
  • On a typical 39 week maternity pay run, 109% is worth about £858 more to a small employer than simply breaking even.

1. The two rates and the line between them

HMRC sets the recovery rate for 2026 to 2027 as:

92% if your total Class 1 National Insurance, both employee and employer contributions, was above £45,000 for the previous tax year.

109% if your total Class 1 National Insurance for the previous tax year was £45,000 or lower.

Three things about that test are easy to get wrong.

  • It is last year's figure. The rate for 2026 to 2027 is decided by your total Class 1 NI in the previous tax year, not by what you are paying now.
  • It is total Class 1, not just employer NI. Employee contributions count towards the £45,000 as well, which means the threshold arrives sooner than employers expect.
  • It is not a headcount test. Two businesses with the same number of staff can land on different sides of it if one pays considerably more.

As a rough sense check, a payroll generating £45,000 of combined Class 1 NI is in the region of a £250,000 pay bill, which for many small employers is somewhere around eight to ten people. Below that, you are almost certainly on 109%.

2. Why small employers get more than 100%

Recovering 109% of a payment you made looks like an error until you see what the extra is for. When you pay statutory maternity pay, you do not only pay the £194.32. You also pay employer National Insurance on it, because statutory payments are earnings. The additional 9% exists to compensate a small employer for that secondary contribution.

So 109% is not a subsidy for having a baby on the payroll. It is the payment plus something towards the employer NI the payment itself generates. A larger employer recovering 92% absorbs both the 8% shortfall and the NI.

The practical consequence is the useful bit. For a small employer, statutory family pay is close to cost neutral, and the cash flow is the only real issue, because you pay it out through payroll and recover it against your PAYE bill. If you have avoided hiring someone because you were worried about the cost of maternity pay, that worry is largely misplaced, and the genuine costs are cover and disruption rather than the statutory payment.

3. Worked example: Ruth's business in Truro

Ruth, Truro. Runs a seven person agency. Her total Class 1 National Insurance last tax year was about £38,000, so she is under £45,000 and on the 109% rate.

The employee. On £30,000, so average weekly earnings of about £576.92, taking the full 39 weeks of Statutory Maternity Pay.

First 6 weeks at 90% of average weekly earnings, which is £519.23 a week: £3,115.38.

Remaining 33 weeks at the flat rate of £194.32: £6,412.56.

Total SMP paid out: £9,527.94.

Recovered at 109%: £10,385.46. That is £857.52 more than she paid out, towards the employer NI on those payments.

If she were above the threshold and on 92%, she would recover £8,765.71, leaving her £762.24 out of pocket on the payment itself plus the NI on top. The swing between the two rates on one maternity leave is about £1,620.

4. The Statutory Sick Pay exception

This is the part that has to be said plainly because it is the most common misunderstanding in this area. You cannot recover Statutory Sick Pay. HMRC states it directly in the same guidance. There is no small employer rate, no percentage, and no reclaim.

That has not always been true, and the memory of older schemes is why employers still ask. The position for 2026 to 2027 is that SSP, at £123.25 a week or 80% of average weekly earnings if lower, is a cost the employer carries in full.

So the honest planning position for a small employer is a split one. Family related statutory payments are close to cost neutral and should not weigh on a hiring decision. Sick pay is a genuine unrecoverable cost, and it is the one to think about when you are modelling what a period of long term absence does to a small business.

5. How it is actually claimed

Recovery is not a separate application. It runs through payroll, by reducing the PAYE and National Insurance you pay over to HMRC in the same period. Your payroll software will handle the mechanics once it knows the payments are statutory.

Three practical points:

  • Check which rate your software is applying. The 92% and 109% rates are a setting, and a business that has crossed the threshold in either direction can be on the wrong one for a year without noticing.
  • Cash flow, not cost, is the issue. You pay first and recover against the next PAYE bill. Where that is difficult, HMRC operates an advance funding route for employers who cannot afford to make statutory payments up front.
  • Keep the records. Recovery is claimed on your say so through payroll, and the supporting evidence for eligibility is something you need to be able to produce.

The honest verdict

If your total Class 1 National Insurance last year was £45,000 or lower, you recover 109% of statutory maternity, paternity, adoption, shared parental, parental bereavement and neonatal care pay. That is more than you paid out, and the extra exists to cover the employer National Insurance those payments generate.

Two things follow. First, the widespread small employer fear that maternity pay is a serious financial hit is largely unfounded, and if it has shaped a hiring decision it was the wrong input. Second, sick pay is the genuine exposure, because none of it comes back. Worth ten minutes checking which recovery rate your payroll software is set to, because crossing £45,000 in either direction changes it and nothing tells you.

Frequently asked

Which year's National Insurance decides my rate? The previous tax year. Your recovery rate for 2026 to 2027 depends on your total Class 1 National Insurance, employee and employer contributions combined, for the previous tax year. Crossing £45,000 in that year moves you from 109% to 92%.

Can I recover Statutory Sick Pay? No. HMRC states that you cannot recover Statutory Sick Pay. There is no small employer exception to this and no percentage recovery. It is a cost the employer carries in full.

Do I have to make a separate claim? No. Recovery is made through payroll by reducing the PAYE and National Insurance you pay over to HMRC for the period. Your payroll software handles it, which is also why it is worth checking the rate the software has been set to.

What if I cannot afford to pay the statutory payment up front? HMRC operates an advance funding route for employers who cannot afford to make statutory payments before recovering them. That is the mechanism for the cash flow problem, which for a small employer is usually the real constraint rather than the eventual cost.
