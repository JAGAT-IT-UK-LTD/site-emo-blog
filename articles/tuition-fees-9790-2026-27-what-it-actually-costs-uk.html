Tuition Fees Are £9,790 This Term And Will Rise Every Year. The Fee Is Not What A Graduate Pays. Here Is The Number That Is.

Students starting in England this month are the first to pay £9,790 a year, and the first cohort under a rule that lifts the cap with inflation every year from now on. The figure gets the headlines. It is also, for most people who take the loan, close to irrelevant. What decides the cost of a degree is the repayment formula, and that has not changed.

1. The fee: £9,790 now, £10,050 next year, and then inflation forever

For 2026 to 2027 the maximum fee a university in England can charge a home undergraduate is £9,790, and the Tuition Fee Loan covers up to that amount. Accelerated two-year degrees can charge up to £11,750. A classroom-based foundation year is capped at £5,760. The figures are on GOV.UK's student finance pages and the loan is paid straight to the university.

The 2.71% rise from £9,535 is the second in two years, after a fee that was frozen at £9,250 from 2017 to 2024. What is new is the mechanism. The government has legislated for the cap to rise each year in line with forecast RPIX inflation, and its own estimate for 2027 to 2028 is a further 2.68%, to about £10,050. That is based on the Office for Budget Responsibility's November 2025 forecast, so treat it as an estimate until the regulations for that year are laid. From 2028 to 2029 the automatic rise applies only to providers meeting the Office for Students' quality standards.

So a three-year course starting this month will cost roughly £9,790 plus £10,050 plus something over £10,300, call it £30,100 in fees, against £27,750 for someone who started three years ago at £9,250. About £2,400 more over the degree. Hold that number; it turns out to matter less than it looks.

2. The repayment formula, which is the actual price

Anyone starting an undergraduate course in England since August 2023 repays under Plan 5. The rules on GOV.UK:

  • You repay 9% of income above £25,000 a year (£2,083 a month, £480 a week). Below that, nothing.
  • Repayments come out through PAYE like tax, or through Self Assessment if you are self-employed.
  • Interest is currently 4.1%, which is RPI only, with no earnings-related addition.
  • Anything left is written off 40 years after the April following the end of the course.

Read the first line again. The monthly repayment depends on your salary, not on your fee, not on your maintenance loan, not on your interest. Two graduates on the same salary pay the same each month whether they borrowed £27,000 or £60,000. The size of the debt decides only how long the payments continue before the balance is cleared or written off.

The arithmetic, on our figures:

£25,000£0£0£0
£30,000£5,000£450£37.50
£35,000£10,000£900£75
£45,000£20,000£1,800£150

The £25,000 threshold has been fixed since Plan 5 began in 2023. The government has not announced a change to it, and it is the single number that would most alter these figures if it moved.

3. Who the fee rise actually costs

Take a graduate who borrows fees of about £30,100 plus three years of maintenance loan. With interest at RPI, the balance grows in cash terms but not in real terms. Whether they ever clear it depends on earnings over 40 years. At a steady £35,000 in today's money they repay £900 a year, £36,000 over 40 years, against a real-terms balance well above that once maintenance is included. The extra £2,400 of fees is added to a balance that is written off unpaid. Their cost of the fee rise: nothing.

Now take a graduate whose salary reaches £60,000 within a few years and keeps rising. They repay £3,150 a year and more, and they will clear the whole balance with interest well inside 40 years. For them every pound of fee is a pound repaid, plus interest. The £2,400 rise costs them £2,400 plus RPI. That is the group the fee rise is really a tax on: the highest-earning fifth or so of graduates, and it is a small share of the headline number of students.

The third group is the one that pays the fee in cash: families who choose not to take the loan, and international students, whose fees are set by the university and are not capped at all. For them the increase is a straight £255 a year now and around £260 more next year.

4. A worked example in Peterborough

Tomasz in Peterborough starts a three-year business degree this month. He takes the full fee loan, £9,790 this year, and a maintenance loan. His parents had been arguing about whether to pay the fees from savings to "save him the debt".

If he graduates in 2029 and earns £28,000 in his first job, he repays 9% of £3,000, which is £270 a year, £22.50 a month. If he reaches £40,000 by 30, that is £1,350 a year. Over a career averaging £40,000 in today's money he repays roughly £54,000 over 40 years, which is close to or below what he will owe once maintenance and RPI interest are counted. He may or may not clear it; it is on the margin.

Now suppose his parents pay the £30,100 in fees up front. Nothing changes about his monthly repayment on the maintenance loan; the 9% formula is the same. It only helps if he would otherwise have cleared the full balance, in which case the family saves the RPI interest on £30,100. If he would not have cleared it, they have spent £30,100 to reduce a number that was going to be written off. On a median-ish career, the parents' cash is better kept until it is clear which case he is.

5. What a family can actually decide

There are only three real levers, and the fee is not one of them.

The maintenance loan is the one to check. It is means tested on household income, and we set out the £25,000 threshold and the parental contribution it silently assumes in the guide linked below. The fee loan is not means tested; everyone gets it.

Course choice and length matter more than the fee cap. An accelerated two-year degree at £11,750 a year costs £23,500 in fees against about £30,100 for three years, and a year less of maintenance loan and living costs, with a year more of earning. That is a bigger saving than any fee freeze.

And timing. Courses starting on or after 1 January 2027 fall under the Lifelong Learning Entitlement, a different funding structure that GOV.UK flags on the student finance page. Anyone starting in January rather than September should read that guide before applying, not after.

6. The honest verdict

The fee is £9,790 and will rise every year. For most graduates that changes nothing they will ever pay. The monthly cost is 9% of income over £25,000, and it is the same whatever the fee was.

It costs the highest earners and cash payers. If you expect to earn £60,000 plus for most of a career, the rise is real money. If not, it is a change to a balance that gets written off.

Do not pay fees up front to avoid debt unless you already know the graduate will clear the loan. That is the one decision this article can save a family money on.

Frequently asked

Is the £9,790 fee the same in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland? No. The £9,790 cap is for England. The devolved nations set their own fees and support, and Scottish students studying in Scotland pay no tuition fee.

Will my monthly repayment go up because fees went up? No. Under Plan 5 you repay 9% of income above £25,000. The fee affects the balance, which affects how long you repay, not the monthly amount.

What interest rate is charged on Plan 5? Currently 4.1%, which is RPI. Plan 5 does not add an earnings-linked margin. Interest is applied from the first payment even while studying.

Will the £25,000 threshold rise? It has been fixed since 2023 and the government has not announced a change. If it rose, monthly repayments would fall; if it stayed frozen while wages rise, more of each pay rise goes to repayment.
