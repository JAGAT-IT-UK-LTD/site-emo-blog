A Cordless Vacuum Saves You £7 A Year In Electricity And Costs You A Battery Every Four Years. Do That Sum Before The Watts One.

Vacuum cleaners are sold on suction, watts and run time, and none of those decides what one costs to own. A corded machine is capped at 900 watts by law and uses about £12 of electricity a year; a cordless one uses less. The cost that actually separates them is a lithium battery that loses capacity from the day it is made and costs a quarter of the machine to replace, if it can be replaced at all. We priced eight years of ownership for both, and the answer turns on one line on the spec sheet.

The short version

  • Mains vacuums sold in the UK are limited to 900 W under the ecodesign rules that took effect on 1 September 2017 and were kept after Brexit. At one hour a week that is 47 kWh a year, £12 at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p.
  • A cordless vacuum draws far less from the wall: on our assumption of 120 Wh per charge from the socket and three charges a week, about 19 kWh a year, £5. The electricity difference is £7 a year. Ignore it.
  • The battery is the running cost. Lithium packs typically hold useful capacity for three to five years of regular use. Assume a replacement at £60 to £120, and a cordless machine costs £15 to £40 a year in batteries on top of its electricity.
  • Over eight years, a £150 corded vacuum costs about £290 to own; a £350 cordless one with a replaceable battery about £590 to £670, on stated assumptions. The convenience premium is roughly £38 to £48 a year.
  • A cordless machine with a sealed, non-replaceable battery has a life of one battery. That is the spec to check: user-replaceable pack, and the manufacturer's spare part price, before anything about suction.
  • Buy corded if you vacuum a whole house in one go and want the lowest cost; buy cordless if you vacuum little and often and value the ten minutes. Neither is wrong. Only the sealed-battery cordless is a bad buy.

1. Why the watts do not matter

Since 1 September 2017, mains-powered vacuum cleaners sold in the UK and the EU have been limited to a rated input of 900 watts under the ecodesign regulation for vacuum cleaners, and the rule was carried into UK law after Brexit. Before that, 2,000 watt machines were common and the number was the whole marketing pitch. It is now a ceiling, and most corded machines sit at or just under it.

Take the ceiling and price it. A 900 W vacuum used for one hour a week draws 0.9 kWh a week, 46.8 kWh a year. At the Ofgem price cap unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, 26.32p per kWh, that is £12.32 a year. Two hours a week, £25. Twenty minutes a week in a flat, £4.

Battery vacuums sit outside that regulation, and their motors are typically rated at 100 to 300 watts, but the number that matters is what the charger draws from the wall. A typical pack stores somewhere between 60 and 100 watt hours, and chargers are not perfectly efficient, so assume about 120 Wh from the socket per full charge. Three full charges a week, which is generous, is 18.7 kWh a year, about £5. These are our assumptions, stated so you can substitute your own machine's battery capacity from its spec sheet; the conclusion does not move much whatever you plug in.

So the electricity gap between the two types is around £7 a year, in favour of cordless. That is the number the "energy efficient" badge on the box is worth, and it is smaller than the cost of the replacement filters. Every other part of this article is about a bigger number.

2. The battery: how it fails and what it costs

A lithium battery loses capacity with every charge cycle and with age even when unused. A cordless vacuum that ran for 40 minutes when new will, after three to five years of regular use, run for 20, then 12, then stop holding a charge at all. Nothing else on the machine has failed; it simply cannot do a room any more.

At that point there are three possibilities, and which one you are in was decided the day you bought it. If the pack clips off and the manufacturer sells a replacement, you buy one and carry on. If the pack is inside the body but designed to be swapped with a screwdriver, the same, with more fiddling. If the pack is sealed in, the machine is finished, and the manufacturer's repair route, where one exists, usually costs more than a budget replacement.

We are not quoting manufacturers' spare battery prices because they change and vary by model, but the range for mainstream brands in 2026 sits broadly between £60 and £120, which for a £350 machine is between a sixth and a third of the purchase price. Spread over a three to five year battery life, that is £15 to £40 a year, which is two to eight times the electricity.

Two things extend the battery's life and both are free: do not leave it on the charger at full for weeks at a time if the machine does not manage that itself, and do not store it flat. Two things shorten it: running it to empty every time, and keeping it in a cold garage or a hot conservatory.

4. The one spec that matters, and the warranty question

Replaceable battery. Before suction, before run time, before the number of attachments: can the battery be swapped, and does the manufacturer list a spare? If the answer to either is no, the machine's life is its battery's life, and the table above says that costs you £60 a year more than the alternative. The EU has legislated for user-replaceable batteries in portable devices from 2027; the UK has not yet followed, so for now it is a spec to check, not a right.

Run time on the setting you will actually use. The headline minutes are on the lowest power with no motorised head. Carpet on the middle setting is often a third of that. If the honest run time will not do your floors in one charge, you will end up charging twice as often and halving the battery's life in years.

Warranty. Manufacturers commonly give two years on cordless machines and sometimes less on the battery itself; some premium brands give five on the machine but not the pack. Retailer guarantees differ more than prices do: some give a second year free, some sell it. A battery that dies in year three is exactly where the difference lands. Read the battery line of the warranty, not the headline.

Spares generally. A corded machine needs a belt and a filter and will take a generic bag. A cordless machine needs a battery, a specific filter, and eventually a motorised head with a worn brush bar. The brand that publishes a spares page with prices on it is the brand that expects the machine to last.

5. Worked example: Tomasz and Grace in Sheffield

Three-bed semi, two floors, one dog, corded upright from 2015 still working but heavy on the stairs

Tomasz wants a cordless stick because the stairs and the dog hair mean quick daily passes; Grace points out the upright works. They price both properly.

Keep the upright. Purchase nil. Electricity, an hour a week at 900 W, £12 a year. A belt and a filter, £5. Total £17 a year, for as long as it lasts.

Buy a cordless with a replaceable battery. Assume £350. Electricity £5, filters £5, one £80 battery at year four is £20 a year. Running cost £30 a year, plus the £350, which over eight years is another £44 a year. Total about £74 a year.

The premium for going cordless: about £57 a year, or £1.10 a week. There is no payback, because there is no saving; the question is whether daily dog hair off the stairs without a cable is worth £1.10 a week to them.

Decision. It is, but only for a machine whose battery clips off and is listed on the manufacturer's spares page. They keep the upright in the cupboard under the stairs for the twice-a-year deep clean, which also halves the charges the cordless has to do and stretches its battery. The sealed-battery model that was £60 cheaper is the one they do not buy.

6. The honest verdict

A vacuum's electricity is £5 to £12 a year whichever type you buy, and no purchase decision should turn on it. A cordless machine costs £35 to £60 a year more to own than a corded one over eight years, almost all of it battery, and that is the price of convenience, not a saving. If you want the cheapest clean floors, buy a corded machine and keep it for a decade.

If you want cordless, buy it knowing the premium, and buy only a machine with a user-replaceable battery that the manufacturer sells as a spare. A sealed-battery cordless vacuum is a four-year appliance sold at an eight-year price, and it is the one thing in this article we would tell you not to buy.

Frequently asked

How much does a vacuum cleaner cost to run per year in the UK? A mains vacuum is limited to 900 W, so an hour a week is 46.8 kWh a year, about £12 at the October 2026 cap rate of 26.32p. A cordless machine charged three times a week uses roughly 19 kWh, about £5, on our assumption of 120 Wh per charge from the socket.

Are cordless vacuums cheaper to run than corded? On electricity, yes, by about £7 a year. On total cost, no: the battery fades in three to five years and a replacement pack costs broadly £60 to £120, which works out at £15 to £40 a year and outweighs the electricity saving several times over.

How long does a cordless vacuum battery last? Typically three to five years of regular use before run time has fallen far enough to matter. Avoiding full discharges, not storing it flat and keeping it out of very hot or cold rooms extends it.

Is it worth replacing the battery in a cordless vacuum? If the manufacturer sells the pack and the rest of the machine is sound, usually yes: a £60 to £120 battery against a £350 machine. If the battery is sealed in, the repair route rarely makes sense and the machine's life was its battery's life.
