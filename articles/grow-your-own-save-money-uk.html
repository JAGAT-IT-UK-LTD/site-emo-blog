Does Growing Your Own Vegetables Save Money in the UK?

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Updated 18 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026

Growing your own vegetables does save money in the UK, but only if you grow the crops that are dear to buy and cheap to grow. Herbs, salad leaves, beans and soft fruit pay their way. Potatoes, onions and carrots almost never do, and most people lose money in year one because the setup costs arrive before the harvest.

That matters because the claims online run into thousands of pounds a year. The honest figure for a beginner is far smaller, and it depends on crop choice more than effort.

The short version
  • Most people lose money in year one, because the setup costs land before the first harvest does.
  • The saving comes from growing what is expensive to buy and cheap to grow. Herbs, salad leaves, runner beans, tomatoes, raspberries and rhubarb.
  • On Defra's wholesale price data for 26 October 2026, raspberries were £13.58 a kg and runner beans £4.41 a kg. Carrots were 60p and brown onions 65p.
  • Allotment rent for 2026/27 is £70.23 a year for a full plot in Cheshire West and Chester and £113.48 in Bradford, plus £22.84 for water there. Every council sets its own price.
  • There is no official figure for what a household saves. Treat any big number you see online as a claim, not a fact.

Does growing your own food actually save money?

There is a well worn claim that a small vegetable plot returns many times what you put in. It can, eventually.

It usually does not in the first year. Year one is when you buy the compost, the seed, the netting, the canes, the watering can and the pots. Then you harvest perhaps six weeks of produce from them.

The saving is real, but it comes from a narrow slice of what people plant. Two square metres of salad leaves and herbs will save you more than a whole bed of maincrop potatoes.

That is the entire trick, and most beginners do the opposite.

Think of it this way: you are not growing food, you are replacing specific supermarket purchases. Work backwards from your own receipt. If you never buy fresh coriander, growing coriander saves you nothing.

Which vegetables are worth growing to save money?

The best crops share three traits. They cost a lot per kilo, they spoil fast in the shop, and they crop for weeks from a small space.

The government's own wholesale price series shows the gap clearly. These are prices traders pay at wholesale markets, so shop prices are higher, but the ranking is what counts.

Which vegetables are not worth growing?

None of these are bad plants. They are simply a poor use of a small plot when the goal is saving money.

  • Maincrop potatoes. Cheap all year round, and they occupy a large area for months. Early new potatoes are a fairer argument, because those are dearer in the shops.
  • Onions. Defra's wholesale price was 65p a kg in late October 2026. They are slow to grow and need the ground all season.
  • Carrots. 60p a kg wholesale on the same date. Fussy about soil, and carrot fly will punish you unless you net properly.
  • Sweetcorn. Needs a block of plants to pollinate at all, takes up serious room, and yields a handful of cobs.
  • Brassicas such as cabbage and cauliflower. Long season, and pigeons and caterpillars treat them as a buffet. Netting is not optional and it costs money.

If you have a large allotment and enjoy growing them, grow them. If you have four square metres and want to cut the food bill, they are the wrong choice.

What does it look like for a real household?

Take Sam and Priya, who live in Chester and rent a half plot from Cheshire West and Chester Council. Their rent for 2026/27 is £35.11. Neither is 60 or over, so no concession applies.

Suppose they give one bed to raspberries and pick 3 kg in a season. At Defra's wholesale price of £13.58 a kg, that fruit is worth £40.74 before any shop markup. The raspberries alone cover the year's rent.

Now suppose they gave the same bed to carrots and lifted 3 kg. At 60p a kg wholesale, that is £1.80 of carrots. Same space, same rent, about one twentieth of the value.

The 3 kg is our assumption for the example, not a promise. Your yield depends on soil, weather and pests. The point is the ratio, and the ratio comes from official price data.

If they moved to Bradford, the same sum changes. A full plot there is £113.48 a year, plus £22.84 where water is supplied, so £136.32 in all. They would need far more high value crops to break even.

What costs do people forget?

The claim that growing your own saves thousands falls apart because it counts the harvest and not the inputs. A realistic first year list:

  • Compost. The largest recurring cost and the one that surprises people. Buying in bulk in early spring is usually cheaper than single bags in July. Making your own is cheaper still once a heap is going.
  • Seed. Individually cheap. Collectively, a first order adds up, and most packets hold more seed than you will use in three years. Split orders with a neighbour.
  • Water. If you are on a meter, a hot summer of hand watering is not free. A water butt pays for itself. If you are worried about your meter, read our guide to what a leak costs on a water meter.
  • Protection. Netting, fleece, slug defences, canes, string. Small amounts that add up fast.
  • Losses. Slugs, pigeons, blight, a fortnight away in August. Budget for losing some of everything, because you will.
  • Your time. Not a cash cost, but be honest that a plot is several hours a week from March to September.

Year two is where the economics improve sharply. The pots, canes, netting and water butt are already paid for, and only compost and seed recur.

How much does an allotment cost in the UK?

There is no national price. Each council, town council or parish council sets its own rent, and the gap is wide.

Two examples we checked on the councils' own pages:

  • Cheshire West and Chester: £70.23 for a full plot and £35.11 for a half plot in 2026/27. People aged 60 or over pay half. The council has already set £75.15 for a full plot in 2027/28.
  • Bradford: £113.48 a year, plus a water charge of £22.84 where it applies. A concessionary rate of £56.74 is available to people on certain benefits, such as Pension Credit. A state pension alone does not qualify.

The real cost is often the wait. GOV.UK says you apply through your local council, which will either offer a plot or put you on a waiting list.

  • Put your name down now, even if you are not ready. It usually costs nothing and the clock starts.
  • Ask about half plots. They are cheaper, and a full plot is more work than most beginners expect.
  • Check the tenancy rules before you commit. Many sites require a minimum level of cultivation, restrict sheds, ban bonfires, and can end a tenancy if a plot is neglected.
  • Some sites let you share a plot officially. Half the work, half the rent, and someone to water while you are away.

If there is no site near you, the law gives residents a lever. Under section 23 of the Small Holdings and Allotments Act 1908, any six registered electors or council tax payers in the area can ask the council in writing to provide allotments, and the council must take that request into account.

Can you grow your own food with no garden?

Yes. A windowsill, a balcony or a doorstep is enough for the highest value crops. That is convenient, because the highest value crops are also the smallest.

  • Windowsill: basil, chives, chillies, mint, and salad leaves in a shallow trough.
  • Balcony or doorstep: add tomatoes in a large pot, spring onions, a dwarf French bean, and strawberries in a hanging basket.
  • Buy the supermarket herb pot and rescue it. Those pots hold many seedlings crammed together. Split one into three or four pots of fresh compost, and a herb that dies in a fortnight becomes plants that last months. It is the cheapest possible way in.

Container growing needs more watering and more feeding than ground, because the plant has no reserves to draw on. That is the trade off for needing no garden.

Peat free compost behaves differently rather than badly. It dries out faster and needs feeding sooner, so water more often and add a liquid feed after the first few weeks.

What do you do with a glut?

Everything ripens at once. That is not a failure, it is how growing works. It is why so many first year plots end with a bin full of courgettes.

Three fixes, in order of usefulness:

  1. Sow in succession, not in one go. A short row of salad every three weeks instead of a whole packet in April. Same seed, a far longer useful harvest.
  2. Freeze what freezes well. Beans, soft fruit, chillies, herbs in oil. This is where real money is saved, because it replaces winter shopping rather than summer shopping. Check first whether a second freezer is worth its running cost.
  3. Grow fewer plants of more types. Two courgette plants, not five. Nobody has ever regretted planting fewer courgettes.

Surplus can also go into batch meals. Our guide to slow cooker batch cooking shows what that actually saves. If you are tempted to sell the extra, read the rules on selling garden produce in the UK first.

Frequently asked

Does growing your own vegetables really save money? Yes, if you pick the right crops. Herbs, salad, beans and soft fruit replace dear shop purchases. Cheap staples like carrots and onions, at 60p and 65p a kg wholesale in October 2026, rarely repay the space. Expect to lose money in year one while you buy tools, compost and pots.

What is the most expensive vegetable to buy but easy to grow? On Defra's wholesale data, raspberries stand out at £13.58 a kg on 26 October 2026, with runner beans at £4.41 a kg. Fresh herbs and bagged salad leaves are also costly per gram in the shop. All of these grow well in the UK with little skill.

How much does an allotment cost per year in the UK? It depends on the council. In 2026/27 a full plot costs £70.23 in Cheshire West and Chester and £113.48 in Bradford, plus £22.84 for water there. Half plots and concessions for older people or people on benefits can halve the rent. Ask your council for its current price list.

Is it cheaper to grow from seed or buy plants? Seed is far cheaper per plant and gives you more choice of variety. Small plants from a garden centre make more sense if you only want two of something, which is usually true of tomatoes and courgettes. Share seed packets with a neighbour, because most packets last several seasons.

Do I need a greenhouse to save money growing food? No. A greenhouse extends the season and helps tomatoes and chillies, but it is a large cost that takes years to repay in produce. A cheap plastic growhouse against a sunny wall does most of the job. Start without one and buy later only if you keep going.

The verdict

Grow the expensive, perishable things and buy the cheap, storable ones. Herbs, salad, beans and soft fruit will genuinely take money off your shopping bill. Potatoes, onions and carrots will not. Do it because you enjoy it, and treat the saving as a real but modest bonus rather than the reason.
