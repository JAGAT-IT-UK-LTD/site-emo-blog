Working From Home Tax Relief Ended in April 2026: Who Can Still Claim Four Back Years, and What Each Year Pays

Ruhul Amin · Published 5 September 2026 · Updated 11 November 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked November 2026

Working from home tax relief ended on 6 April 2026, so employees cannot claim the £6 a week flat rate for 2026 to 2027 onwards. But the four previous tax years are still open, worth up to £62.40 a year at basic rate and £124.80 at higher rate. The catch is that you must have been required to work from home, not just allowed to, and the oldest year closes on 5 April 2027.

The short version

  • Working from home tax relief ended on 6 April 2026. For the 2026 to 2027 tax year and later, employees cannot claim it. Budget 2025 inserted section 360B into ITEPA 2003 to disallow the deduction. Source: GOV.UK and HMRC's tax information and impact note, checked 11 November 2026.
  • You can still claim for the four previous tax years, 2022/23 to 2025/26, but only if you had to work from home. That means your employer had no office, or your job required you to live far from it. Choosing to work from home, including under a hybrid contract, never qualified.

1. What ended on 6 April 2026, and why?

Working from home tax relief let employees deduct the extra household costs of working at home. It sat in section 336 of the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003.

Working out the real cost of extra heating, electricity and business calls was impractical. So HMRC allowed a flat rate instead, with no receipts needed. From the 2020 to 2021 tax year that flat rate was £6 a week, or £312 a year.

During the pandemic years, 2020 to 2021 and 2021 to 2022, the test was widened. Anyone told to work from home because of Covid could claim, and millions did.

Budget 2025, on 26 November 2025, announced the end of the relief. HMRC's tax information and impact note says a new section 360B disallows the deduction for household costs an employer does not reimburse. It took effect from 6 April 2026.

The stated reason is compliance. HMRC says that after checking claims, over half were found to be ineligible. The note estimates 300,000 people are affected, losing £62 a year at basic rate or £124 at higher rate.

The Budget on 28 October 2026 did not bring the relief back. GOV.UK's guidance, checked on 11 November 2026, still says the relief has ended.

GOV.UK's guidance page now says it in one line: from the tax year 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, you will not be able to claim tax relief for working from home. It adds the sentence this article is about. You can still claim for the four previous tax years.

2. Who can still claim working from home tax relief?

The four open years are 2022 to 2023, 2023 to 2024, 2024 to 2025 and 2025 to 2026. For each of them the old test applies in full. It was always narrower than most people believed.

GOV.UK's wording is short. You can claim if you have to work from home, for example because your job requires you to live far away from your office, or your employer does not have an office.

You cannot claim if you choose to work from home. GOV.UK gives two examples. Your employment contract lets you work from home some or all of the time. Or your employer has an office, but you sometimes cannot go there because it is full.

A hybrid arrangement, however permanent, is a choice in HMRC's eyes. The pandemic easement ended with the 2021 to 2022 year. It does not help with any of the four open years.

So the people with a live claim are a specific group:

  • fully remote employees of companies with no UK office,
  • field based staff whose contract requires them to work from a home base,
  • employees whose role was made home based by the employer, in writing, rather than by preference.

If that is you and you never claimed, the next section is your money. If it is not, the honest answer is that no claim was ever available. The abolition changes nothing for you.

3. How much is working from home tax relief worth for each year?

The flat rate is £6 a week, £312 a year. The relief is given at your top rate of tax, not paid as cash in full.

GOV.UK's own example: a basic rate taxpayer claiming £6 a week gets £1.20 a week in relief. Over 52 weeks that is £62.40 a year, on our arithmetic.

Your tax rateRelief a weekRelief a yearAll four open years
Basic rate, 20%£1.20£62.40£249.60
Higher rate, 40%£2.40£124.80£499.20
Additional rate, 45%£2.70£140.40£561.60

Those are the maximum sums for a full year, full week claim. They are what a qualifying employee who never claimed can still recover. Scottish taxpayers get slightly different sums, because the Scottish income tax bands and rates differ.

You could instead claim the exact extra costs, with evidence, if they were higher than £6 a week. In practice almost nobody does. The extra cost of heating one room for a working day is hard to prove and rarely much above the flat rate.

Broadband and rent do not count. You use them privately as well, so they are not extra costs of the job.

4. A worked example in Cardiff

Owen works for a software company that closed its Cardiff office in the summer of 2022. It has had no UK premises since. His contract was varied that August to make his home his place of work.

He earns £34,000, so he is a basic rate taxpayer. He has never claimed anything. He assumed the relief was a pandemic scheme that had ended.

He qualifies for all four open years. The employer had no office, and the arrangement was the employer's, in writing. Each year is worth £62.40, so £249.60 in total.

He claims through HMRC's online service in November 2026, with the contract variation as evidence. All four years have ended, so each one comes back as a refund rather than a tax code change.

From 6 April 2026 there is nothing more to claim. His tax code for 2026 to 2027 should not carry any homeworking allowance. If an old claim was rolled forward into it, the code is wrong. He should get the wrong tax code fixed rather than underpay tax for a year and face a bill later.

His colleague Bethan, at the same company, moved to a higher rate salary in 2024. Her claim is £62.40 for each of the first two years and £124.80 for each of the last two. That is £374.40. Same employer, same evidence, different rate.

Their friend Rhys works for a bank with a Cardiff office and a hybrid policy of three days at home. He has read the same headlines and wants to claim. He cannot, for any of the four years.

His contract lets him work from home. It does not require it. That was always the rule, and the abolition has not changed it.

5. How do you claim tax relief for working from home, and what evidence is needed?

Use HMRC's online service on GOV.UK. It first checks whether you can claim, then takes the claim. This is the same route as form P87, the claim for work expenses an employer never reimbursed.

If you complete a Self Assessment tax return, GOV.UK says you must claim on the return instead. If you are filing for 2025 to 2026 now, the online return is due by 31 January 2027, and our guide to the 31 January 2027 Self Assessment deadline sets out what you pay and when.

For any flat rate claim for 2022 to 2023 or later, you must send evidence that you had to work from home. The usual forms are:

  • your contract, or a contract variation naming your home as your place of work,
  • a letter from your employer saying you were required to work from home,
  • a written statement that the employer had no office you could use.

For a claim on exact costs rather than the flat rate, you also need the receipts or bills.

When is the deadline?

GOV.UK says you can claim for the four previous tax years. In practice that puts the last day for 2022 to 2023 at 5 April 2027. The 2023 to 2024 year closes on 5 April 2028, and so on.

HMRC pays refunds for closed years directly. There is no current year left to adjust through your tax code.

Two cautions before you claim

First, do not claim for a year in which you chose to work from home because everyone was doing it. That is the group HMRC says made up more than half of the claims it checked, and it is now checking.

Second, if you are a company director working from home for your own company, the rules are different. That is better handled through the company and is outside this article.

6. Can my employer still pay me a working from home allowance?

Yes. The abolition removes the employee's own deduction. It does not remove the employer's ability to pay for home working.

HMRC's expenses and benefits guidance still lets an employer pay £6 a week, or £26 a month for monthly paid staff, towards extra household costs. That is free of tax and National Insurance, and needs no evidence. Above that, the employer must show the payment is no more than the real extra cost.

The impact note says so plainly: the measure does not affect employers who reimburse home working costs.

For a fully remote employee the £312 a year has not vanished. It has moved from HMRC's side of the ledger to the employer's.

Whether the employer pays it is up to the employer. For anyone negotiating a remote role or a pay review, it is a small, specific, tax free item worth asking for.

It costs the employer £312 a year per person and is worth £312 in the employee's pocket. The same £312 paid as salary would lose tax and National Insurance on the way. If you are already asking for a flexible working arrangement, put the allowance in the same written request.

7. What about working from home tax relief if you are self employed?

The self employed were never under the employee rule, and Budget 2025 did not touch them. If you run a business from home, you can still claim a share of your household costs against your profits.

The simplest route is HMRC's simplified expenses flat rate. GOV.UK sets it by hours worked at home each month:

Hours of business work at home a monthFlat rate a month
25 to 50£10
51 to 100£18
101 or more£26

You must work at least 25 hours a month at home to use it. The flat rate does not cover phone or internet. You can add the business share of those separately.

A sole trader working 101 hours or more a month at home can claim £26 a month, £312 a year. At basic rate that cuts income tax by £62.40, on our arithmetic, with a little National Insurance saved too.

Many people now have a job and a side business. You can claim use of home against the business even though the employee relief has gone. Our guide to side hustle tax rules in the UK covers when you need to register and what else counts.

8. The honest verdict

The £6 a week relief is gone from 6 April 2026, and it was never available to anyone who chose to work from home. Hybrid workers lose nothing because they had nothing.

If your employer had no office or required you to work from home, and you never claimed, four years are still open: £62.40 a year at basic rate, £124.80 at higher, with evidence. The 2022/23 year closes on 5 April 2027, so do it now.

From now on the only employee route is the employer paying £6 a week tax free. Ask for it in writing. It is the same money and the same rule, just a different payer. The self employed keep their own use of home claim.

Frequently asked questions about working from home tax relief

Can I still claim working from home tax relief?

Not for 2026/27 or any later year, because the relief ended on 6 April 2026. You can still claim for the four previous years, 2022/23 to 2025/26, if your employer required you to work from home in those years. Choosing to work from home, including under a hybrid contract, has never qualified for the relief.

How much is working from home tax relief worth?

On the flat £6 a week, one year is worth £62.40 at basic rate, £124.80 at higher rate and £140.40 at additional rate. All four open years at basic rate come to £249.60. You can claim exact costs instead if they were higher, but you need bills and receipts to prove them.

How far back can I claim tax relief for working from home?

GOV.UK says you can claim for the four previous tax years. That means 2022/23 is the oldest year still open, and it must be claimed by 5 April 2027. The years 2023/24, 2024/25 and 2025/26 close on 5 April in 2028, 2029 and 2030. Claims for 2022/23 onward need evidence you had to work from home.

Can my employer still pay me for working from home?

Yes. Your employer can pay up to £6 a week, or £26 a month if you are paid monthly, free of tax and National Insurance and without evidence. Budget 2025 did not change this. Anything above that limit needs proof that it matches your real extra household costs, or it becomes taxable pay.

Can the self employed claim for working from home?

Yes. The abolition only affects employees. If you are self employed and work 25 hours or more a month at home, you can use HMRC's simplified flat rates of £10, £18 or £26 a month, depending on hours. Or you can claim a fair share of your actual household bills against your business profits.
