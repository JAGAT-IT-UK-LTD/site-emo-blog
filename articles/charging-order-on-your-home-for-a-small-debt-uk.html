The Debt on Your House and the Sale of Your House Are Two Different Court Applications

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

A charging order turns an unsecured county court judgment into a debt secured on your property. That is a serious thing and it is worth understanding properly, which means separating it from the thing people assume follows automatically and usually does not.

The short version

  • What it does: if the land or property is sold, the debtor must pay the charge before they get their money. That is GOV.UK's own description.
  • What it does not do: it does not sell your house. Forcing a sale is a separate application to the court, decided separately.

1. What a charging order actually is

If a creditor has a county court judgment against you and you have not paid it, one of the enforcement routes open to them is to ask the court to charge your land or property. They apply on form N379, an application for a charging order on land, under Part 73 of the Civil Procedure Rules.

GOV.UK describes the effect in a single sentence: if the land or property is sold, the debtor must pay this charge before they get their money.

Read that carefully, because it is doing two jobs. It confirms the debt is now secured on the property, which is a real change in status. And it frames the consequence as something that happens if the property is sold, not as something that causes the property to be sold.

In practice, a charging order sits on the title. If you later sell or remortgage, it gets paid out of the proceeds ahead of you. It also means the debt does not simply fade with time in the way an unenforced judgment can.

2. The separate application nobody separates

The thing that frightens people about a charging order is the idea that the creditor now decides when the house gets sold. They do not.

Enforcing the charge by forcing a sale is an order for sale, and it is a further application that the court considers on its own merits. It is not automatic, it is not a formality, and it is comparatively uncommon on small consumer debts.

That distinction is the single most useful thing on this page. A letter saying a charging order has been applied for is not a letter saying your home is being sold. They are two different stages, with a court decision in between, and there is a specific statutory floor sitting under the second stage for a large class of debts.

3. The 1,000 pound floor, and where it does not apply

The Charging Orders (Orders for Sale: Financial Thresholds) Regulations 2012 came into force on 6 April 2013. They set a financial threshold of £1,000.

Where they apply, no order for sale may be made if the amount of the judgment debt that remains unpaid at the date of the application to enforce is less than £1,000. In other words, a creditor cannot force the sale of a home to recover a sub-£1,000 balance.

Now the limit on the limit, and it matters as much as the protection itself. The Regulations apply where the charging order secures a judgment enforcing payment of money owed under an agreement regulated under the Consumer Credit Act 1974. That is credit cards, personal loans, store credit, catalogue debt and the like.

It does not cover every debt. Council tax arrears, rent arrears, unpaid trade invoices, money owed to another individual after a dispute, and other non-regulated judgment debts fall outside it. If your debt is not consumer credit, the £1,000 floor is not there for you, and that is a genuinely important thing to establish early rather than assume.

4. Worked example: the Ashworths in Redditch

The Ashworths have two judgment debts against them. One is £740 remaining on a credit card. The other is £2,600 owed to a contractor after a dispute over building work.

The credit card debt. The card provider can still apply for a charging order on their home, and the court can still grant one. The charge attaches. But because the agreement is regulated under the Consumer Credit Act 1974 and the unpaid balance is £740, which is under £1,000, an order for sale cannot be made to recover it. The debt sits on the title and gets settled if and when they sell.

The contractor debt. This is not a regulated consumer credit agreement. The 2012 Regulations do not apply to it at all, so there is no £1,000 statutory floor. At £2,600 it would be over the threshold anyway, but the point is that the protection was never in play for this debt.

What that means for them. Two charges, two completely different risk profiles, and the one that feels smaller and more ordinary, the credit card, is actually the better protected of the two. If they have limited money and are deciding which to clear first, that fact belongs in the decision.

Enforcement against wages is a separate route with its own protections, and we have set out what an attachment of earnings order can and cannot take from your pay in its own guide.

5. What to do if one is applied for

  1. Read the form and the dates. There is a stage at which an interim charging order becomes final, and there is a window in which you can object. Missing it is how people lose arguments they would have won.
  2. Establish what kind of debt it is. Regulated consumer credit or not. That single fact determines whether the £1,000 floor is available to you.
  3. Check whether Breathing Space applies. A creditor cannot enforce a judgment against someone who has the protection of the Debt Respite Scheme, and they have responsibilities to tell the court.
  4. Get free advice before paid advice. National Debtline, StepChange and Citizens Advice all deal with charging orders regularly and none of them charges you. There is no stage of this where a fee-charging company is the obvious first call.
  5. Do not ignore it because it has not been sold. A charging order is permanent until the debt is cleared, and it will surface at the worst possible moment, usually mid-sale or mid-remortgage.

This is general information about published court rules, not legal advice. If a charging order has been applied for against your home, speak to one of the free advice services named above.

The honest verdict

A charging order is serious and it is not the disaster it is usually described as. It converts an unsecured judgment into a debt secured on your property, and it gets paid before you do when the property changes hands. That is real. It is also not a sale, and a sale requires a second, separately decided application.

The £1,000 threshold is genuinely protective for credit card and loan debts, and genuinely absent for council tax, rent arrears and ordinary disputes. Work out which kind of debt you are dealing with before you work out how worried to be, and get that from a free debt adviser rather than from the creditor's letter.

Frequently asked

Can they force the sale of my home for a small debt? Not for a regulated consumer credit debt under 1,000 pounds. The Charging Orders (Orders for Sale: Financial Thresholds) Regulations 2012 prevent an order for sale where the unpaid judgment debt is less than 1,000 pounds. Other kinds of debt are not covered by that threshold.

Does a charging order mean I have to sell? No. It means that if the property is sold, the charge is paid before you receive anything. Forcing a sale is a separate application to the court, decided on its own merits.

What form does a creditor use? Form N379, an application for a charging order on land, under Part 73 of the Civil Procedure Rules.

Does the order disappear if I ignore it? No. It stays attached until the debt is cleared, and it will be dealt with when you sell or remortgage. Time does not remove it.
