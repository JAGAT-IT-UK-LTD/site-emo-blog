Your Company Car Fuel Rate Changed on 1 September, and the Electric Gap Is 8p a Mile

Advisory fuel rates are the quietest quarterly number in UK payroll and one of the easiest to get wrong, because they are routinely confused with the 55p rate that applies to your own car. Here are the figures that took effect on 1 September 2026, what each column is actually for, and the one gap that leaves electric drivers out of pocket.

The short version

  • Petrol: 14p up to 1400cc, 17p from 1401cc to 2000cc, 27p over 2000cc.
  • Diesel: 15p up to 1600cc, 16p from 1601cc to 2000cc, 22p over 2000cc.
  • LPG: 11p up to 1400cc, 13p from 1401cc to 2000cc, 20p over 2000cc.
  • Electric: 7p on a home charger, 15p on a public charger. That split is the single most useful thing on the page.
  • These are company car rates only. If you drive your own car on business, your rate is 55p, which is a completely different table.
  • Hybrids count as petrol or diesel, not as electric, for this purpose.

1. The rates from 1 September 2026

HMRC publishes these quarterly. The set below applies from 1 September 2026 and is taken from HMRC's rates and thresholds page for employers, which records the 1 September update.

HMRC published those rates on 21 August 2026 and they hold until the next quarterly review on 1 December 2026, with a month of grace in which the previous set may still be used. Four of them moved on 1 September and two of the four moved down. Petrol over 2000cc went up a penny to 27p. Diesel between 1601cc and 2000cc fell from 17p to 16p, diesel over 2000cc fell from 23p to 22p, and LPG over 2000cc fell from 21p to 20p. If your employer set the rate in June and has not looked since, the driver of a 2.0 diesel is being reimbursed a penny a mile more than the approved amount and the driver of a 2.5 petrol a penny a mile less.

None of this is a judgement call at HMRC. The petrol rates are built from a pump price of 159.9p a litre, diesel from 179.2p and LPG from 95.0p, each divided by a mean miles per gallon taken from manufacturer figures weighted by three years of fleet sales, then rounded to the nearest whole penny. The electric rates run on 3.59 miles per kilowatt hour, with electricity at 24.47p per kWh at home and 54.00p per kWh on a slow or fast public charger. That is the whole reason the public rate is more than double the home rate, and the reason a driver who charges mostly away from home and is paid the 7p home rate is out of pocket on every business mile.

Two structural points worth noting. Diesel beats petrol at the small end, 15p against 14p, then loses badly at the top, 22p against 27p. And hybrids are treated as either petrol or diesel cars for this purpose, so there is no hybrid column to look for.

2. What advisory fuel rates are actually for

This is where most of the confusion lives. Advisory fuel rates do two jobs, and neither of them is the job people assume.

  • Reimbursing business fuel in a company car. Your employer can pay you this much per business mile to cover fuel, with no tax or NI consequence.
  • Repaying private fuel. If your employer pays for all your fuel including private mileage, you can use these rates to pay back the private element and avoid the car fuel benefit charge, which is expensive and is an all or nothing charge.

What they are not is the rate for using your own car. That is the mileage allowance payment, and from 5 June 2026 it is 55p for the first 10,000 business miles. The reason the two numbers are so far apart is that 55p is meant to cover the entire cost of running a vehicle, including insurance, servicing and depreciation, while an advisory fuel rate covers fuel and nothing else. If you are being reimbursed 17p a mile for using your own car, someone has used the wrong table.

One more detail from HMRC's own page, which matters for anyone modelling an electric company car: electricity is not a fuel for car fuel benefit purposes.

3. The 7p and 15p split, and who loses on it

HMRC splitting the electric rate by charging location is sensible, because the underlying costs genuinely differ by that much. It also creates the clearest unfairness on the page.

The 7p home rate assumes a domestic electricity price. The 15p public rate assumes you are paying public charging prices, which are higher and carry 20% VAT rather than the domestic rate. If you have a driveway and a home charger, 7p is close to reality. If you live in a flat or a terrace with no off street parking, you physically cannot access the cheaper option, and your employer's policy may still only offer you 7p because that is the number someone put in the expenses system.

That is an 8p per mile gap, applied to someone whose housing situation is the only reason they are on the wrong side of it.

4. Worked example: Tom in Swindon

Tom, Swindon. Fully electric company car, 7,000 business miles a year. He rents a terrace with no off street parking, so every charge is a public one.

Reimbursed at the home rate of 7p. 7,000 at 7p is £490.

Reimbursed at the public rate of 15p. 7,000 at 15p is £1,050.

The gap is £560 a year, out of his own pocket, for business miles he drove for his employer.

What fixes it. Not a claim form. His employer applying the correct rate for how he actually charges. HMRC publishes both figures precisely because both situations exist, and an expenses policy that only knows about 7p is not following the guidance, it is ignoring half of it.

5. When you can use a higher rate than the table

The advisory rates are a safe harbour, not a cap. HMRC's guidance is explicit that an employer can use a different rate where the actual cost per mile is higher and can be shown, without that creating a taxable profit.

That matters in three situations:

  • Public charging. Covered above, and the cleanest case, because the receipts exist.
  • Genuinely thirsty vehicles. A large engine working hard, towing or fully loaded, can beat the banded assumption.
  • Pump prices moving mid quarter. The rates are set quarterly and fuel prices are not. HMRC reviews them four times a year, which means there are stretches where the published figure lags the forecourt.

The condition is evidence. Keep the charging receipts or the fuel receipts and the mileage log. Without those, a higher rate is an assertion, and the excess over the advisory rate is taxable pay.

The honest verdict

Advisory fuel rates are boring and small, and they are still worth ten minutes once a quarter, because the amounts compound quietly and nobody is checking them on your behalf.

Two things are worth acting on from this set. If you drive your own car and are being paid an advisory fuel rate, that is simply the wrong table and the right number is 55p. If you drive an electric company car and cannot charge at home, the published public rate of 15p exists precisely for you, and being paid 7p is not a rule, it is an expenses policy that has only read half the guidance. Both are fixed by a short conversation with payroll rather than by a claim to HMRC.

Frequently asked

Do these rates apply to my own car? No. If you use your own car for business, the figure is the approved mileage allowance payment, which rose to 55p for the first 10,000 business miles on 5 June 2026, then 25p after. Advisory fuel rates apply only to company cars.

What about a hybrid? HMRC treats hybrid cars as either petrol or diesel cars for advisory fuel rate purposes, so use the petrol or diesel column that matches the engine and its size. There is no separate hybrid rate.

How often do these change? Quarterly. HMRC reviews advisory fuel rates four times a year. This set applies from 1 September 2026. Between reviews the published figure can lag actual pump and charging prices in both directions.

Can my employer pay less than the advisory rate? Yes, and some do. The advisory rate is the amount that can be paid without a tax charge, not a minimum. If you are paid less than the actual cost of business fuel in a company car, that is a matter for your employer's expenses policy rather than something HMRC will refund to you.
