Probate Costs 526 Pounds, or Nothing at All, and the Line Between the Two Is 5,000 Pounds

Ruhul Amin · Published 14 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Most guides to probate tell you it is expensive and complicated. The fee itself is neither. It is a single flat charge with one threshold, and the two places people lose money are the valuation that sits either side of that threshold and the box asking how many copies you want.

The short version

  • The application fee is £526 where the estate is valued at more than £5,000. Source: GOV.UK, page updated 9 September 2026.
  • There is no fee at all if the estate is £5,000 or less. Not a reduced fee. Nothing.
  • Extra copies of the grant cost £2 each when ordered with the application, and £16 each afterwards. That is eight times the price for the same piece of paper, decided by timing alone.
  • A second application, where probate has already been granted, costs £22 and is payable even when the estate is under £5,000.
  • Help with fees exists and is claimed online or on form EX160, but it never covers the cost of the extra copies.
  • You do not have to use a solicitor. The fee above is the court fee. A professional's charge is separate and is not fixed by anyone.

1. What the probate fee actually is

In England and Wales there is one court fee for applying for probate, and it does not scale with the size of the estate. GOV.UK states it plainly: if the value of the estate is over £5,000, the application fee is £526. That is the same £526 whether the estate is £5,100 or £5.1 million.

This surprises people, because almost every other cost attached to death does scale. Solicitors commonly quote a percentage. Estate agents charge a percentage. Inheritance Tax is a percentage. The court fee is not. It is a fixed charge for processing an application, and the Probate Registry does the same amount of work either way.

Two other fixed amounts sit alongside it. The registrar charges separately again for death certificate copies, and most estates need several, because each bank and pension provider keeps the one you send: what extra copies of a death certificate cost is worth knowing before you order. Extra copies of the probate document cost £2 each if you order them when you submit the application. If you need more after the application has gone in, they cost £16 each. And if probate has already been granted and a second application is needed, for example where an executor held what is called power reserved on the first application and now wants to act, that second application costs £22.

Everything else you might be quoted is somebody's professional fee, not a government charge. That distinction matters, because the two get merged in quotes and in advertising, and a firm quoting "probate from £1,500 including fees" is bundling a £526 court charge that you could pay yourself into a figure that looks like a single price.

2. Why 5,000 pounds is a cliff edge and not a slope

GOV.UK: "There's no fee if the estate is £5,000 or less." There is no tapering, no reduced band, no half fee. At £5,000 exactly you pay nothing. At £5,000.01 the fee is £526.

That structure makes the valuation the single most financially significant step in a small estate. Not the form, not the solicitor, not the tax position. The valuation.

Being honest about what this does and does not mean is important. It does not mean you should shade a valuation downwards to get under the line. Valuing an estate is a legal duty and the figures are declared to HMRC as well as to the Probate Registry, so understating is not a saving, it is a false declaration. What it does mean is that in a genuinely borderline estate, getting the valuation right rather than guessing high is worth real money, and that a small estate is exactly the case where many families never needed to apply for probate at all.

That last point is the bigger saving and it is easy to miss. Probate is not always required. Many banks and building societies will release balances below their own internal threshold on sight of a death certificate and a small estates form, and those thresholds are commonly set somewhere between £5,000 and £50,000 depending on the institution. Jointly held property and joint accounts usually pass to the survivor without any grant. Before paying £526, it is worth asking every organisation holding money what their threshold is, in writing.

3. The copies decision, which is worth more than most people think

This is the part of the probate fee page that almost nobody reads carefully, and it is the only part where a decision you make in thirty seconds changes the bill by a meaningful amount.

When probate is granted you get the grant document. Every organisation holding assets will want to see it, and many want to keep a certified copy rather than pass the original round. A bank, a second bank, a pension provider, a share registrar, an insurer and the Land Registry can easily be six organisations for an ordinary estate.

Order those six copies with the application and they cost £2 each, so £12. Realise you need them a fortnight later and the same six copies cost £16 each, so £96. The difference is £84, for identical documents, decided entirely by whether you counted the organisations before you pressed submit or after.

The practical advice, then, is dull and specific: before you apply, write a list of every organisation that holds anything in the deceased's sole name, count it, add two, and order that many copies. At £2 each the cost of over-ordering is trivial. At £16 each the cost of under-ordering is not.

4. Worked example: Grace in Nuneaton

Grace is dealing with her father's estate. He owned no property, having rented his flat, and his assets are a current account of £3,850, a savings account of £1,120, and a small credit union balance of £180.

The valuation. £3,850 plus £1,120 plus £180 is £5,150. That is over the threshold, so if probate is needed the fee is £526.

What she does first. She writes to all three institutions and asks, in each case, what their threshold is for releasing funds without a grant of probate. Two of the three say they will release on a small estates indemnity form and a death certificate. The third, holding £1,120, insists on a grant.

The decision that follows. Grace now has a genuine choice, and it is arithmetic rather than sentiment. Applying for probate to release £1,120 costs £526, which is 47 per cent of the money she is trying to get at. Before spending that, the sensible step is to go back to the third institution in writing, point out the proportion, and ask them to reconsider under their own discretion, which many will do when the fee approaches the balance. If they will not, the £526 still leaves her £594 better off than abandoning the account, so it is worth paying, but it is worth paying reluctantly and second.

The copies. If she does apply, she needs copies for the one institution insisting on a grant, plus one for her own file, plus one spare. Three copies ordered with the application: £6. The same three ordered later: £48.

The honest summary of Grace's position is that the biggest saving available to her was never in the fee. It was in the two phone calls that removed two thirds of the reason to apply at all.

5. Help with fees, and the bit it does not cover

If you are on a low income or on certain benefits you may be able to get help with the probate fee and other court fees. You apply online through the Get Help With Court Fees service, or by filling in form EX160, and GOV.UK is explicit that you should do this before applying for probate.

There are two details here that catch people out, and both are on the page in plain sight.

First, if you apply for probate online you pay the full fee at the time regardless. A successful help with fees claim produces a refund afterwards, not a waiver at the point of payment. If the £526 is the reason you cannot proceed, that matters, and applying by post with form PA1P or PA1A may be the better route.

Second, and this is stated outright: you cannot get help with the fee for extra copies. The £2 and £16 charges stand whatever your circumstances. Which loops back to section three. If money is tight, the copies decision is the one place where thirty seconds of counting genuinely saves you tens of pounds that nobody is going to refund.

The honest verdict

The probate fee is one of the few government charges that is genuinely simple, and the complexity people associate with probate comes from the estate, not the fee. £526 over £5,000, nothing at or under it, £2 a copy now or £16 a copy later, £22 for a second application. That is the whole of it.

If you take two things from this page, take these. Ask every institution whether they need a grant at all before you pay anything, because for a small estate the answer is often no and the £526 was avoidable. And count your copies before you submit, because that is a decision worth up to about £84 on an ordinary estate and it cannot be undone at the original price.

Where this page does not help you: if the estate holds property, has a complicated trust, involves a disputed will, or has an Inheritance Tax liability, the court fee is the smallest number you will deal with and professional advice is worth its cost. Nothing above changes that.

Frequently asked

Is the probate fee the same across the whole UK? No. The £526 fee and the £5,000 threshold are the England and Wales position. Scotland has its own system, where the equivalent is confirmation rather than probate and the fees are set separately, and Northern Ireland has its own arrangements again. Check the relevant service if the person died domiciled outside England and Wales.

Can I apply for probate myself without a solicitor? Yes. The application is designed to be made by an executor or administrator directly, online or on form PA1P if there is a will and PA1A if there is not. The court fee is the same either way. A solicitor's charge is on top and is not regulated to a fixed amount, so if you use one, get the basis of charging in writing before you instruct.

What is 'power reserved' and why does it cost £22? Where a will names several executors, one or more can choose not to act for now while reserving the right to step in later. That is power reserved. If they later do step in, that is a second application on an estate where probate has already been granted, and GOV.UK prices it at £22. It is payable even if the estate is under £5,000.

How long does the application take? That varies and GOV.UK does not publish a guaranteed timescale on the fees page, so this guide will not invent one. Expect the valuation and paperwork stage to take longer than the registry stage in most straightforward estates, and expect any Inheritance Tax position to have to be settled with HMRC before the application can complete.
