September CPI came in at 3.5%: here is what it did to your April 2027 benefits

The ONS published September 2026 inflation on 21 October: CPI was 3.5%, up from 3.1% in August. A week later DWP used it to set most benefit rates for April 2027. Here is what rises, by how much, and the two payments that rise by more.

The short version

  • The figure is 3.5%. The ONS published it on 21 October 2026: CPI rose 3.5% in the 12 months to September 2026, up from 3.1% in August.
  • Most benefits rise 3.5%. DWP confirmed on 28 October that most working age, disability and carer benefits, plus Statutory Sick Pay and Statutory Maternity Pay, rise by 3.5%, mainly from 12 April 2027.
  • The State Pension rises 3.9%. Earnings grew faster than prices, so the triple lock uses earnings. That is around £490 a year more on the full new State Pension.
  • Universal Credit rises by more. The standard allowance goes up by around £219 a year for a single person aged 25 or over, because the Universal Credit Act 2025 adds an extra uplift on top of CPI.
  • Two things are frozen. The benefit cap and Local Housing Allowance rates stay at their current levels for 2027 to 2028.

1. What the ONS published on 21 October

On Wednesday 21 October 2026 the Office for National Statistics published Consumer price inflation, UK: September 2026, as planned. The headline: CPI rose by 3.5% in the 12 months to September 2026, up from 3.1% in August.

Prices rose 0.4% in the month itself. The ONS says transport, mainly motor fuels, made the largest upward contribution. CPIH, the measure that adds owner occupiers' housing costs, was 3.6%. Core CPI, which leaves out energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, was 2.8%.

There was no speech and no press conference. A statistics release went up on a website, and it quietly set the income of millions of households for the year from April 2027.

Inflation is not expected to fall straight away. In its Budget forecast a week later, the OBR said it expects CPI to peak at 3.8% in the first quarter of 2027. Our Autumn Budget 2026 guide covers the rest of that day.

2. How the 3.5% became your April 2027 rates

The chain is short, and this year it ran exactly to timetable.

  1. The ONS published September CPI on 21 October 2026: 3.5%.
  2. The Work and Pensions Secretary, Pat McFadden, finished the annual statutory review of benefit rates and announced the result in a written statement to Parliament on 28 October 2026, Budget day.
  3. DWP will place the full list of proposed rates for 2027 to 2028 in the Libraries of both Houses and on GOV.UK. When we checked on 9 November, we could not find that list on GOV.UK yet.
  4. The new rates mainly come into effect from 12 April 2027.

One detail that trips people up. The ONS bulletin carries several measures, and they do not agree. CPI is the one used for benefits. CPIH read 3.6% this time, and RPI is an older measure still used for some rail fares and student loans. If you see 3.6% quoted for benefits, that is the wrong measure.

Running the chain backwards explains the payments you get now. September 2025 CPI was 3.8%, and that is the rise that applied from April 2026. We looked at what it added in working age benefits up 3.8%.

3. How much each benefit rises in April 2027

The September figure is not the only number in play. The State Pension follows the triple lock, and earnings grew faster than prices this year.

A note for Scotland. The rises for Personal Independence Payment, other disability benefits and Carer's Allowance apply in England and Wales. In Scotland these are devolved, and Northern Ireland sets its own social security.

4. The exception: Universal Credit rises by more

This is the part most headlines leave out, and it changes the answer for millions of claimants.

For most working age benefits, the rise is simply CPI. For the Universal Credit standard allowance, it is not. The Universal Credit Act 2025 takes the standard allowance out of the normal review. It sets a minimum each year from 2026 to 2027 through to 2029 to 2030: the inflation path, then an extra uplift on top.

The uplift in the Act is 2.3% for 2026 to 2027 and 3.1% for 2027 to 2028. Because each year's uplift is measured against the inflation path, the rise this April works out at about 4.3% for the standard allowance, not 3.5%.

DWP put it in pounds. A single person aged 25 or over gets around £219 a year more. A couple where one is 25 or over gets around £344 a year more.

Your other elements, such as the housing element or child elements, follow their own rules. The health element moves differently again, which we set out in how the health element works for new claims in 2026.

5. A worked example: Marcus in Sunderland

Marcus is 34, single, lives in Sunderland and claims Universal Credit. He has no housing element because he lodges with family. He is an illustration, but every rate below is official.

For 2026 to 2027 the standard allowance for a single claimant aged 25 or over is £424.90 a month, up from £400.14 in 2025 to 2026. Over a year that is £424.90 multiplied by 12, which is £5,098.80.

Marcus sees the headline "benefits up 3.5%". He works out 3.5% of £424.90, which is about £14.87 a month, and assumes that is his rise. It is not.

The Act sets the minimum like this. Start from £400.14, the 2025 to 2026 rate. Add September 2025 CPI of 3.8% and September 2026 CPI of 3.5%. Then add the 2027 to 2028 uplift of 3.1%. £400.14 times 1.038 times 1.035 times 1.031 is about £443.21 a month.

That is £18.31 a month more than now, or £219.72 over a year. It matches DWP's own figure of around £219 a year. So Marcus gets about £3.44 a month more than the headline 3.5% would suggest.

Treat £443.21 as the legal minimum, worked out from the Act. The exact published rate will be in DWP's full list for 2027 to 2028, and that list is the figure to plan around.

6. What happens next

The decision is made. What is left is the paperwork.

DWP has said it will publish the full list of proposed State Pension and benefit rates for 2027 to 2028 on GOV.UK. When it appears, it will give the exact pounds and pence for every benefit. Until then, use the percentages above.

The Budget on 28 October did not change the uprating. It did announce a change to the triple lock, but not yet. The current triple lock stays in place through April 2029. From April 2030, the earnings part is replaced by whatever rise keeps the State Pension at the same value relative to earnings as in April 2029.

The next CPI figure, for October 2026, is due on 18 November 2026. It does not affect April 2027 benefit rates. Only the September figure counts.

Frequently asked

What was the September 2026 CPI inflation rate? CPI rose by 3.5% in the 12 months to September 2026, up from 3.1% in August, according to the ONS release published on 21 October 2026. CPIH, which includes owner occupiers' housing costs, was 3.6%. Motor fuels made the largest upward contribution to the rise.

How much will benefits go up in April 2027? Most working age benefits, disability benefits, Carer's Allowance, Statutory Sick Pay and Statutory Maternity Pay rise by 3.5%, in line with September CPI. The Universal Credit standard allowance rises by more. The benefit cap and Local Housing Allowance rates are frozen for 2027 to 2028.

How much will the State Pension go up in April 2027? The full new and basic State Pension rise by 3.9%, in line with average weekly earnings growth to May to July 2026, because that was higher than CPI or 2.5%. DWP says that is around £490 a year more on the full new State Pension.

How much will Universal Credit go up in April 2027? The standard allowance for a single person aged 25 or over rises by around £219 a year, DWP says, and by around £344 a year for a couple where one is 25 or over. That is more than 3.5% because the Universal Credit Act 2025 adds an extra uplift.

When do the new benefit rates start? The new rates apply in the 2027 to 2028 tax year and mainly come into effect from 12 April 2027, according to the Work and Pensions Secretary's statement. The exact first payment at the new rate depends on your benefit and your payment or assessment cycle.
