Dropshipping UK 2026: Is It Worth It? The Real Margin on a £40 Sale

1. What is dropshipping, minus the marketing?

You list a product you do not own. A customer buys it from you. You pass the order to a supplier, who ships it straight to your customer.

You never touch the stock. That is the appeal, because it removes the two things that sink most small shops: buying stock up front, and storing it.

What it does not remove is the cost of finding a customer. In 2026 that cost is the whole game, and it is where most beginner guides go quiet.

2. Is dropshipping legal in the UK?

Yes. Dropshipping in the UK is a legal way to sell. It is not a loophole and it is not a scam.

It does come with the same duties as any other shop. You must follow consumer law, handle VAT correctly, and tell HMRC about your income once it passes the trading allowance.

Marketplaces add their own rules. eBay, for example, allows dropshipping from a wholesale supplier. It does not allow you to buy the item from another retailer or marketplace and have it shipped to your buyer, and it can remove listings or suspend accounts that do.

3. How much do UK dropshippers keep per order?

Less than the videos suggest. The gross margin, the gap between your price and the supplier's price, is the number that gets shown. The net margin, after ads and fees, is the number you live on.

Here is one order, using real UK platform fees and a realistic ad cost. The supplier and ad figures are an illustration, so put your own numbers in.

Now add the part nobody models. One refunded £40 order, where the goods never come back, costs you about £36.65 in supplier, ad and fee money. That wipes out the profit from roughly eleven good ones.

So your refund rate is not a detail. It is the business.

The £14 gross margin is the number in the YouTube thumbnail. The £3.35 is the number in your bank account, and only if nobody asks for a refund.

4. Why did the ad maths break?

Dropshipping boomed between roughly 2016 and 2021. Paid social traffic was cheap and the format was new. Both have changed.

The cost of reaching a thousand people on Facebook, Instagram and TikTok has risen a long way since then. The exact figure moves by month, audience and niche, so read it from your own ad account rather than from a guru's slide.

The logic is simple. If your cost to reach people doubles and your conversion rate stays the same, your cost per customer doubles. On a £3.35 net margin, that takes you straight into a loss.

The practical result: you cannot test cheaply any more. Old guides talk about testing a product on £5 a day. That rarely produces enough data to tell you anything useful in time.

Budget £600 to £1,200 of testing spend before you have a product that works. Accept that much of it is simply the price of finding out.

5. Do you need to register for VAT to dropship in the UK?

This is the most expensive thing beginners get wrong, and course sellers most often leave out.

Since January 2021, goods sent to customers in Great Britain in consignments of £135 or less are taxed differently. HMRC's guidance says VAT is charged at the point of sale as UK supply VAT, not as import VAT at the border, and the seller must charge and account for it.

For a seller based outside the UK, there is no threshold. HMRC says an overseas seller must charge UK VAT and register for VAT in the UK.

For a small seller based in the UK, the normal registration threshold is £90,000 of taxable turnover in a rolling 12 months. HMRC's low value goods guidance does not spell out how that threshold interacts with direct imported sales for a small UK business. So do not guess. Ask HMRC or an accountant in writing before your first sale, and keep the answer.

  • The £135 is the consignment value, not the price of one item. HMRC adds item values together unless they are sent separately.
  • Over £135, normal rules apply: import VAT and any customs duty at the border, plus a customs declaration.
  • If you sell through an online marketplace and the goods are £135 or less, the marketplace is usually the one that accounts for the VAT. Check which applies to you.
  • VAT registered businesses keep digital records and file through Making Tax Digital software.

Getting this wrong does not bring a polite letter. It brings a bill for VAT you never collected, and you cannot go back to customers to charge them.

6. What changes when the £135 duty relief ends?

Today, most parcels worth £135 or less come into the UK with no customs duty. That relief is going.

The government announced the change at Budget 2025 and confirmed it in a policy paper in July 2026, updated on 28 October 2026. The relief ends on a date the Treasury sets, and no later than October 2028. That is six months earlier than the March 2029 date first floated.

The plan is that sellers, or the online marketplace where it handles the sale, become responsible for the duty. For a dropshipper importing cheap goods, that is a new cost on every order and more paperwork.

If your margin is £3.35 today, a duty charge on a £26 supplier price can take a real slice of it. Model it now, before you commit to a low ticket product.

7. Who is liable when a parcel goes wrong?

Many people think of themselves as a middleman. UK law does not. Your customer bought from you, so under the Consumer Rights Act their rights are against you.

If the item arrives faulty, not as described, or not at all, you have to put it right. eBay puts the same point plainly: you are still responsible for safe delivery within the time you stated.

A supplier who has gone quiet, or sits eight time zones away, is not a defence your customer has to accept. You refund first. Then you try to recover the money from the supplier, and often you cannot.

8. Who pays for returns?

Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations, a UK customer who buys online has 14 days from delivery to cancel without giving a reason. Not because it is faulty. Because they changed their mind.

Where an order comes in separate parcels, the 14 days start from the last delivery. Then come the details that cost dropshippers money.

  • The customer then has a further 14 days to send the item back after telling you they are cancelling.
  • You must refund within 14 days of getting the item back, or of getting proof they sent it. The refund includes the standard delivery you charged.
  • If you did not clearly tell the customer they pay return postage, you pay it. Sending an item back to a supplier abroad often costs more than the item.
  • You can deduct for handling beyond what was needed to inspect the item, but only if you warned them in advance.

Your returns page is not admin. It is a money control. Written well, it moves return postage to the customer. Left vague, every changed mind is a full loss plus postage.

9. A worked example over three months

Dan, 27, Nottingham. Starting budget £900. The fees below are Shopify's published UK rates. The product, supplier price and ad costs are an illustration.

Month one. Shopify Basic on monthly billing at £25, a theme and two apps at £34, a domain at £12. That is £71 of set up.

He tests four products with £150 of ads each, so £600. Three sell nothing. One sells nine units at £40. Revenue £360, supplier cost £234, card fees about £9.

Month one: about minus £554.

Month two. He scales the one product that worked. 61 orders at £40 is £2,440. Supplier £1,586. Ads £549, so about £9 a customer. Card fees £64. Store plan and apps £59.

Four customers cancel inside the 14 days. His returns page never mentioned return postage, so he pays it: £160 refunded plus £34 of postage. The four items come back to his spare room, unsold.

Month two: about minus £12. Sixty one sales, and still a loss.

Month three. He rewrites his returns page so customers pay return postage. But as he widens his audience, his cost per customer rises from £9 to £11.40. The same 61 orders now cost £695 in ads.

Two customers cancel and post the items back at their own cost. He refunds £80. Card fees £64, store £59.

Month three: about minus £44.

Three months: about minus £611 on £900 of capital, plus roughly 140 hours of work. He has six returned items he may sell later, and he knows one product that sells.

That is not a failure story. It is the ordinary version. Anyone telling you month one is profitable is usually selling you a course.

Notice what would have changed Dan's result. A clear returns page from day one saves £34. A product with £20 of margin instead of £14, at the same ad cost, turns months two and three into a profit. A customer who arrives from search, not an ad, saves him £9 to £11.40 every time.

10. Where does dropshipping still work in the UK?

Dropshipping is not finished. The version that works in 2026 looks different from the 2019 version.

  • Higher price points. On a £400 item, a 20% margin is £80. That absorbs an £11 customer cost easily. On a £15 item it never will.
  • UK suppliers. Harder to find and thinner margins, but delivery in days rather than weeks, fewer refunds, returns you can actually handle, and no exposure to the coming duty change.
  • Traffic you do not rent. If customers come from search or an audience you built, you delete the £9 ad line. That is the gap between £3.35 and about £12 an order.
  • A narrow niche you understand. A generic gadget store competes with everyone on earth. A store for one hobby, run by someone who does that hobby, can charge more and gets repeat buyers.

If you would rather hold a little stock, our guides to Amazon FBA costs in the UK and Etsy fees for UK sellers run the same arithmetic for those routes.

Who should not start. If £900 is money you need, do not put it into ad tests. If you want income within thirty days, this is the wrong model, because month one is nearly always negative.

And if the plan is to find a trending TikTok product and run ads, remember that thousands of other people are running the same play on the same product in the same week.

Is dropshipping worth it in the UK? The honest verdict

Dropshipping in the UK is a real business, and treating it as one is the only version that works. The people who succeed rarely found a secret product.

They got their VAT position confirmed in writing. They wrote a returns policy that protects them. They sold something with enough margin to survive an £11 customer cost. And they built a traffic source they do not have to rent.

The people who fail bought a course, spent £400 testing low ticket gadgets on paid ads, then met a VAT question they had never asked.

If you want to try it, budget £900 to £1,500, expect month one to be negative, sell something above £100, and check the VAT and duty rules before your first sale, not after your first quarter.

Frequently asked

Is dropshipping legal in the UK? Yes. Dropshipping is a legal way to run a shop in the UK. You are the seller in law, so the Consumer Rights Act and the 14 day cancellation right apply to you. You must also handle VAT correctly and tell HMRC about your income once it passes the trading allowance. Marketplaces such as eBay add their own dropshipping rules.

Do I need to register for VAT straight away? If your business is based overseas, yes, there is no threshold. If you are UK based, the normal threshold is £90,000, but HMRC's guidance on direct imported sales of £135 or less is not written for small UK sellers. Get a written answer from HMRC or an accountant before launch. Sales through a marketplace are often taxed by the marketplace.

Do I need a limited company to dropship? No, you can dropship as a sole trader. A limited company separates your personal money from the business and can look more credible to suppliers, but it adds Companies House filing and accounts. Neither choice changes your VAT position. Our sole trader or limited company guide sets out the costs.

What about the trading allowance? The trading allowance is £1,000 a year now, rising to £3,000 from April 2027. It is measured on gross income, not profit. Dan passes it in his second month. Once past it, he must register for Self Assessment by 5 October after the tax year ends. Our side hustle tax guide explains the steps.

Is print on demand better than dropshipping? It is a different trade off. Margins are similarly thin, but print suppliers are more often in the UK or Europe, so delivery is faster and returns are less painful. You also sell your own designs, which is harder to copy. Our print on demand guide breaks down the profit per item.
