Dropshipping is not a scam and it is not dead. It is a real, legal business model with a margin problem, a compliance problem and a liability problem, and almost every beginner guide skips all three. Here is the arithmetic before you spend anything.

1. What dropshipping actually is, minus the marketing

You list a product you do not own. A customer buys it from you. You forward the order to a supplier, usually overseas, who ships it directly to your customer. You never touch the stock.

That is genuinely appealing, because it removes the two things that kill most retail startups: buying inventory up front, and storing it. What it does not remove is the cost of finding a customer, and in 2026 that is the entire game.

It is completely legal in the UK. It is not a loophole and it is not a scam. It is a real business model with real economics, and the economics are the problem.

2. The margin, line by line

Published figures put typical dropshipping net margins at 15% to 20%. Strong, well optimised stores reach around 30%. Beginners routinely come in below 10%.

Here is what that looks like on one order, using UK numbers:

LineAmountRunning total
Customer pays you£40.00£40.00
Supplier cost including shipping-£26.00£14.00
Advertising to acquire that one customer-£9.00£5.00
Payment processing-£1.50£3.50
Store platform and apps, per order-£0.60£2.90
Before refunds and chargebacksabout £3

Now add the part nobody models: a single refunded order at £40 wipes out the profit from roughly thirteen successful ones. Your refund rate is therefore not a minor detail, it is the whole business.

The £14 gross margin is the number in the YouTube thumbnail. The £3 is the number in your bank account, and only if nobody asks for a refund.

3. Why the ad maths broke

Dropshipping worked spectacularly between roughly 2016 and 2021 because paid traffic was cheap and the format was novel. Both have changed.

Facebook CPM now sits around $8.77, against $4 to $5 a few years ago. TikTok runs roughly $5 to $12. That is not a small adjustment. If your cost to reach a thousand people doubles and your conversion rate stays the same, your cost per customer doubles, and on a £3 net margin that takes you straight into a loss.

The practical consequence: you cannot test cheaply any more. Guides written in 2019 talk about testing products with £5 a day. At current CPMs, £5 a day does not produce enough data to tell you anything within a useful timeframe. Budget realistically for £600 to £1,200 of testing spend before you have a product that works, and accept that a large share of that is simply the cost of finding out.

4. The VAT rule that catches almost everyone

This is the single most expensive thing beginners get wrong, and it is the thing course sellers most reliably omit.

Since January 2021, for consignments valued at £135 or less sold directly to customers in Great Britain, UK supply VAT is charged at the point of sale, not as import VAT at the border. To account for that, you generally need to be UK VAT registered, regardless of the £90,000 turnover threshold.

Read that again, because it inverts what most people assume. Most small UK businesses register for VAT when they cross £90,000. A dropshipper importing low value consignments direct to GB customers may need to register from the first order.

  • The £135 is the consignment value, excluding transport and insurance, not the price of one item.
  • Over £135, normal rules apply: import VAT and any customs duty at the border, plus a customs declaration.
  • If you sell through a marketplace rather than your own store, the marketplace is often the one that accounts for the VAT. Check which applies to you before you assume.
  • VAT registered businesses must keep digital records and file through Making Tax Digital software. Spreadsheets and manual filing are no longer the route.

Getting this wrong does not produce a polite letter. It produces a bill for VAT you never collected from customers, and you cannot go back and charge them for it.

5. You are the seller. That means you are liable

The mental model that sinks people is thinking of themselves as a middleman. UK law does not see it that way. Your customer bought from you, so under the Consumer Rights Act your customer's rights are against you.

If the item arrives faulty, not as described, or does not arrive at all, that is your problem to fix. Your supplier being unreachable, or in a different time zone, or having gone quiet, is not a defence your customer has to accept. You refund, then you try to recover from the supplier, and often you do not.

6. Returns, and who pays the postage

Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations, a UK customer buying online has 14 days from delivery to cancel without giving any reason. Not because the item is faulty. Because they changed their mind. Where an order arrives in separate parcels, the clock starts on the final delivery.

Then the details that cost dropshippers money:

  • The customer has a further 14 days to send the item back after telling you they are cancelling.
  • You must refund within 14 days of receiving the item back, or of receiving evidence they sent it, and that refund includes the standard delivery you charged.
  • If you did not clearly tell the customer that they pay return postage, you pay it. This is the one that hurts, because returning an item to a supplier in China is often more expensive than the item.
  • You can deduct for handling beyond what was needed to inspect the item, but only if you warned them in advance.

Practical consequence: your returns policy page is not admin, it is a financial control. Written properly it moves return postage to the customer. Left vague, every changed mind is a full loss plus postage.

7. A worked example over three months

Dan, 27, Nottingham. Starting budget £900.

Month one. Shopify at £25, a theme and two apps at £34, a domain at £12. He tests four products at £150 each in ad spend. Three produce no sales at all. One sells nine units at £40. Revenue £360, supplier cost £234, ads on that product £150, fees £14. Month one: minus £563.

Month two. He scales the one product that worked. 61 orders at £40 is £2,440 revenue. Supplier £1,586. Ads £549, so about £9 a customer. Payment fees £92. Platform and apps £59. Four customers cancel under the 14 day right, and because his returns page never mentioned return postage, he pays it: four refunds at £40 plus £34 of postage. Month two: about £120 profit.

Month three. The product still sells, but his ad account CPM drifts up as he widens the audience, and cost per customer rises from £9 to £11.40. Same 61 orders now cost £695 in ads. Month three: about minus £26.

Three month total: minus £469 on £900 of capital, and roughly 140 hours of work. That is not a failure story, it is the ordinary version. Dan now knows one thing that actually sells, which has real value, but he has paid £469 and 140 hours to learn it. Anyone telling you month one is profitable is selling you something.

8. Where it still genuinely works

Dropshipping is not finished. The version that works in 2026 looks different from the 2019 version.

  • Higher price points. On a £400 item a 20% margin is £80. That absorbs an £11 customer acquisition cost comfortably. On a £15 item it never will. Low ticket dropshipping is where the maths is worst.
  • UK based suppliers. Slower to find, thinner margins, but three day delivery instead of eighteen, far fewer refunds, and returns you can actually process.
  • Traffic you are not renting. If your customers come from search or from an audience you built, you delete the £9 line from the table. That is the difference between £3 and £12 an order.
  • A narrow niche you understand. Generic gadget stores compete with everyone on earth. A store for one specific hobby, run by someone who does that hobby, can charge more and gets repeat buyers.

Who should not start. If £900 is money you need, do not put it into ad testing. If you want income within thirty days, this is the wrong model, because the first month is nearly always negative. And if the plan is "find a trending product on TikTok and run ads", understand that ten thousand other people are running the same play on the same product in the same week.

The honest verdict

Dropshipping is a real business, and treating it as one is the only version that works. The people who succeed are not the ones who found a secret product. They are the ones who registered for VAT properly, wrote a returns policy that protects them, sold something with enough margin to survive an £11 customer acquisition cost, and built a traffic source they do not have to rent.

The people who fail bought a course, spent £400 testing low ticket gadgets against a $8.77 CPM, then got a VAT bill.

If you want to try it, budget £900 to £1,500, expect month one to be negative, sell something above £100, and read the VAT rules before your first sale rather than after your first quarter.

Frequently asked

Do I need to register for VAT straight away? If you are importing consignments worth £135 or less directly to customers in Great Britain, generally yes, from the start, regardless of the £90,000 threshold. If you sell through a marketplace, the marketplace may account for it instead. This is worth twenty minutes with an accountant before you launch, not after.

Do I need a limited company? No, you can dropship as a sole trader. A limited company gives you liability separation and looks more credible to suppliers, but it adds filing obligations. Neither choice changes your VAT position.

What about the £1,000 trading allowance? It applies to gross income, not profit, so on the example above Dan crosses it in his first week of real sales. Once past it he needs to register for Self Assessment.

Is print on demand better? Different trade offs. Margins are similarly thin, but suppliers are more often UK or EU based, so delivery times and returns are less painful. We break the numbers down separately.
