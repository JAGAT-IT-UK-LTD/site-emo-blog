How to Become a Virtual Assistant in the UK, and What to Charge

To become a virtual assistant in the UK you need two services to sell, a sole trader registration once you pass the trading allowance, and a rate that is not a wage. UK freelance VAs averaged £32.35 an hour in the Society of Virtual Assistants 2024 survey. Employed VAs average about £13.65. This guide explains the gap, so you do not price yourself at the wrong end of it.

The short version

  • Freelance VAs in the UK averaged £32.35 an hour in the Society of Virtual Assistants 2024 survey. Specialists charge £50 an hour or more.
  • Employed virtual assistants average £26,627 a year on Indeed, which is about £13.65 an hour on a 37.5 hour week.
  • The gap is not profit. It covers your tax, National Insurance, holiday, sick pay, pension, software, insurance and every unbilled hour spent finding clients.
  • Most VAs stay small. In the same survey 39.1% earned under £10,000 a year. It is a real income only if you price properly and sell retainers.
  • Charging £12 an hour is the classic beginner error. After costs and unbilled time it falls below the £12.71 National Living Wage benchmark.
  • Tax: the trading allowance is £1,000 until 5 April 2027, then £3,000. The ICO data protection fee is £52 a year.

1. How much do virtual assistants charge per hour in the UK?

The best published figure comes from the trade itself. The Society of Virtual Assistants surveys UK VAs, and its 2024 average was £32.35 an hour.

The same source says specialists charge £50 an hour or more.

Now the sobering number. In the same 2024 survey, 39.1% of VAs earned under £10,000 a year. A good hourly rate is not a good income unless you can fill the hours.

Where you land is decided far more by what you can do than by how long you have done it. A VA who can run a client's bookkeeping or CRM is in a different market from one offering inbox management.

2. Why is the freelance rate double the employed one?

New VAs underprice because they compare their rate with a salary. That is the wrong comparison. Work through where £30 an hour goes.

LineEffect on £30 an hour
Income tax and National InsuranceComes out of your rate, not on top of it. 20% tax and 6% Class 4 National Insurance on profit between £12,570 and £50,270
No paid holidayAn employee on a five day week gets 28 days paid. You bill nothing those days
No sick pay, no employer pensionBoth are yours to fund
Software, insurance, ICO fee, accountantSmall each month, but they are yours alone
Unbilled timeMarketing, proposals, invoicing, chasing payment. Track it, because it is larger than you expect

Do the arithmetic. Say you bill 25 hours a week at £30 but spend 8 more unbilled. You are working 33 hours for £750.

That is £22.73 an hour before tax and costs. It is not far above the employed rate. So £30 an hour is a normal price, not a greedy one.

A freelance rate is not a wage with a bigger number on it. It is a wage plus everything an employer would otherwise pay for on your behalf.

3. Should you charge hourly or sell a retainer?

Hourly billing is how almost everyone starts, and it has two real problems. Your income stops when you stop. And you are punished for getting faster.

Retainers fix both. The client buys a block of hours each month, agreed in advance, and you get predictable income.

The sums are simple. At £30 an hour, a 20 hour retainer is £600 a month. A 40 hour retainer is £1,200. Three clients on 40 hours each is £3,600 a month.

  • Sell blocks, not open ended time. Ten, twenty or forty hours a month.
  • Keep any discount modest. A small reduction for a committed retainer is fair. A third off is not.
  • State whether hours roll over. Usually they should not, or you build up a debt of hours you can never deliver.
  • Invoice at the start of the month, not the end. This one change fixes most cash flow problems for new freelancers.

Two or three part time retainers make a full time income. It is a far steadier business than twenty hourly clients.

4. Which virtual assistant skills pay more?

General admin sits at the bottom of the range because the supply is large. The jump toward £50 comes from a skill the client cannot easily replace.

  • Bookkeeping. Reconciling accounts, chasing invoices, preparing records for an accountant. Making Tax Digital for Income Tax went live in April 2026 for sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000. It widens to over £30,000 in April 2027 and over £20,000 in April 2028, so demand is rising.
  • CRM and systems. Setting up and running HubSpot, Notion, Airtable or automations. Business owners will pay well never to touch this themselves.
  • Executive support. Diary management for a truly busy person, and inbox triage with the authority to decide. This is trust based work and it is priced that way.
  • Industry knowledge. A VA who understands estate agency compliance, or how a physiotherapy clinic books, needs no training and can charge for that.
  • Content and social. Not just scheduling. Writing the posts, editing the video, handling the replies.

The pattern is plain. You are paid for what the client cannot do or refuses to do, not for hours of general helpfulness.

5. How do you become a virtual assistant with no experience?

  • Pick two services, not twelve. "Inbox and diary management for consultants" gets clients. "Anything you need" does not.
  • Register for Self Assessment once you pass the trading allowance. That is £1,000 of gross trading income in a tax year now, and £3,000 from 6 April 2027. You can start trading before you register.
  • Get professional indemnity insurance quotes before your first client. Many clients ask, and you want to be able to say yes. Our guide to professional indemnity insurance for freelancers explains what it covers.
  • Write one simple contract. Scope, rate, hours, payment terms, notice period, confidentiality. A template is fine, but have one.
  • Find the first three clients through people, not platforms. Local business groups, LinkedIn, and telling everyone you know.
  • Set your rate in the low to mid twenties and hold it. Low enough to win early work, high enough that you are not trapped there.
  • Handle client data properly. You will hold personal data, so UK GDPR applies to you. Sole traders who use personal information must pay the ICO data protection fee unless exempt. It is £52 a year for the smallest tier, with £5 off by direct debit.

You do not need paid software to begin. Our list of free software for UK small businesses covers invoicing, records and password tools.

6. A worked first year

Priya, 41, former office manager in Reading. She starts part time around school hours. Her prices are an example, not a market survey.

Setup costs: £233. Insurance £96 on the quote she chose, domain and email £30, password manager £30, accounting software £30 for the year, and the ICO fee at £47 by direct debit. No course, no website builder, no logo designer.

Months one to three. One client at £22 an hour, six hours a week for twelve weeks. £1,584 gross. She spends about the same time again unbilled on outreach, so her true rate is nearer £11. This is normal and it is temporary.

Months four to six. A second client, and she moves the first onto a 20 hour monthly retainer at £24. £3,400 gross, with unbilled time falling as referrals start.

Months seven to nine. She learns Xero properly, two evenings a week for a month, and adds bookkeeping. New clients pay £32. Existing clients move to £28 at renewal. £5,100 gross.

Months ten to twelve. Two retainers plus ad hoc work, about 22 billed hours a week at an average £30. £7,900 gross.

Year one: £17,984 gross on part time hours. Take off about £600 of costs and her profit is roughly £17,384.

The tax, if this is her only income. Profit above the £12,570 personal allowance is £4,814. Income tax at 20% is £963 and Class 4 National Insurance at 6% is £289. She sets aside about £1,250.

Her rate went from £22 to £30 not because a year passed. It rose because she added one skill clients could not do themselves.

7. What keeps virtual assistant rates low?

  • Competing with overseas rates. You will never win on price against a VA in a lower cost country, and the clients who shop that way are not the clients you want. Sell what distance makes hard: your time zone, your knowledge of UK business, your accountability.
  • Scope creep. "Could you also just..." is how a 10 hour retainer becomes 18 hours for the same money. Track your time from day one and raise it at 12 hours, not at 18.
  • Never raising rates on existing clients. Review once a year, give a month of notice, and expect to lose one client now and then. That is the cost of not being underpaid by all of them.
  • Discounting to win the first client. A client won at £15 rarely accepts £30 later. Your first rate is close to your ceiling with that client.
  • Working without a contract. Most disputes are about scope, not money, and a one page contract prevents nearly all of them.
  • Forgetting the trading allowance counts gross income. At £22 an hour you cross £1,000 in about 46 billed hours. From April 2027 the £3,000 line takes about 137 hours.

The honest verdict

Virtual assistant work is one of the few side hustles that can clear the £12.71 benchmark from the first client, provided you do not price yourself as an employee. The trade's own survey average is £32.35 an hour, so asking for the high twenties is not ambitious.

Be clear eyed about the other survey number. Nearly four in ten UK VAs earn under £10,000 a year. The ones who earn more sell retainers, not loose hours, and one specialist skill, not general helpfulness.

Bookkeeping is the highest return addition for most people, and Making Tax Digital keeps widening the market for it.

Start in the low to mid twenties, get three clients through people you know, add a specialism in the first year, and review your rate every twelve months. That is the whole plan. The part most people skip is the review.

Frequently asked

How do I become a virtual assistant in the UK with no experience? Start from the office skills you already have and choose two services to sell. Tell your own network first, agree a simple written contract, and take one client at a rate in the low to mid twenties. Register for Self Assessment once your gross income passes the trading allowance.

How much do virtual assistants charge per hour in the UK? The Society of Virtual Assistants 2024 survey put the UK freelance average at £32.35 an hour, with specialists at £50 or more. Employed virtual assistants average £26,627 a year on Indeed, about £13.65 an hour. New freelancers usually start below the survey average and move up.

Do I need a qualification to be a virtual assistant? No. Clients want evidence and references, not a certificate. Software certifications, especially in accounting packages, can help you charge more. Spend your first money on insurance and a contract template, not on a general VA course, and learn a specialist tool once you have a paying client.

Do I need to register with the ICO as a virtual assistant? Usually, yes. If you handle personal information for clients as a sole trader, you must pay the ICO data protection fee unless an exemption applies. The smallest tier is £52 a year, or £47 by direct debit. The ICO website has a short self assessment to confirm.

Can I be a virtual assistant part time from home? Yes, and most people start that way, around a job or school hours. Remember the unbilled time. If you have 12 hours a week, plan to bill 8 or 9 of them. A part time retainer of 20 hours a month suits this pattern better than ad hoc hourly work.
