The most expensive thing in your house is probably a toilet you cannot hear

Severn Trent reckons between 5 and 8 per cent of British toilets are leaking, almost all of them silently, and that a bad one wastes up to 400 litres of treated drinking water a day. On a meter that is not a rounding error. It is one of the largest single line items you can remove from a household budget in an afternoon.

The short version

  • Up to 400 litres a day from a leaking toilet, according to Severn Trent, which also puts the share of leaking toilets at 5 to 8 per cent.
  • That is £597.80 a year at £4.0945 a cubic metre: Severn Trent's 2026/27 metered water rate, plus used water, which is billed on 95 per cent of what goes through the meter.

  • The test is free and takes five minutes. Severn Trent gives away dye tablets. Nothing needs to be bought before you know.

1. How big the problem actually is

Severn Trent's news release on leaky loos, checked again on 9 November 2026, is unusually direct for a utility. It states that a leaky toilet can waste up to 400 litres of drinking water a day, which is enough to fill five baths, and that this can more than double the average family's water bill.

Doug Clarke, the company's water resources lead, is quoted saying that having a leaky loo is basically the equivalent of turning the tap on and leaving it to run all day, and that between 5 and 8 per cent of all toilets are leaking, mostly dual flush ones. Nationally the company puts the total at 400 million litres a day, which it describes as enough to supply the populations of Edinburgh, Bristol, Manchester, Sheffield, Cardiff, Belfast and Liverpool combined.

Worth flagging, as we do whenever a number comes from an interested party: Severn Trent benefits from customers using less water, because demand it does not have to supply is capital it does not have to spend. That does not make 400 litres wrong, and the company is also giving away the test kit for free, but you should know whose interest a figure serves.

The mechanism matters because it explains the silence. Clarke's explanation is that the prime culprits are dual flush toilets, where people either do not understand what the two buttons are for or simply use the big button every time, and where the buttons can get stuck, meaning a constant stream of water flows down all day. The water dribbles invisibly inside the cistern, down the back of the pan and into the standing water, where it makes no sound and leaves no mark.

The water rates used throughout this guide. Severn Trent's published household charges for 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027, checked on 9 November 2026. For a metered home in zones 1 to 8, water supply is £2.5503 per cubic metre and used water discharged directly or indirectly to a public sewer is £1.6255 per cubic metre.

There is one detail most write ups miss. Severn Trent's scheme of charges says that for most customers it assumes 95 per cent of the water supplied goes back to the sewer, and bills used water on that. So each cubic metre through your meter costs £2.5503 plus 95 per cent of £1.6255, which is £4.0945. We use Severn Trent because it publishes the whole scheme openly. Your company's rates will be different. Put yours in and the method still works.

2. What that costs in money

Four hundred litres is 0.4 cubic metres. Over a year that is 146 cubic metres. At £4.0945 per cubic metre through the meter, that leak costs £597.80 a year.

It is worth checking that against Severn Trent's own claim, because the two were arrived at independently. Severn Trent puts its average household water and sewerage bill for 2026/27 at around £48.94 a month, about £587 a year. Add £597.80 and you get £1,184.80, a little more than double, which is exactly what the company said a bad leak can do. Two separate routes to the same answer is the best evidence either of them is right.

For a wider view, Discover Water, using Water UK data, puts the average combined household water and sewerage bill in England and Wales for 2026-27 at £639. A bad leak on a meter is almost a second bill of that size.

The £150 is our stated assumption for a plumber's visit and a replacement flush valve, not a quoted price, and it is deliberately on the high side. If the fix is a new flush valve you fit yourself, it is a fraction of that and the payback on the bad leak row drops to a matter of days.

Read the unmetered row carefully, because it is the honest one. If you are billed on rateable value, a leaking toilet costs you nothing at all and no amount of arithmetic will change that. It is still 146 cubic metres of treated drinking water a year going through your house and into the sewer, and it is still worth fixing, but we are not going to pretend there is a payback where there is not one.

3. How to find out in five minutes

The whole point of this article is that the test is free, so do the test before you do anything else.

The dye test. Severn Trent gives away dye tablets to its customers. You drop one into the cistern, wait a few minutes without flushing, and look in the pan. Colour in the pan means water is moving from the cistern into the bowl when it should not be. The company says there is no mess and the dye disappears in a few flushes. Other water companies run the same giveaway, so check yours before buying anything.

The pencil test, if you cannot wait. Wipe the back of the pan dry above the water line with toilet paper, then mark the water line in the pan lightly. Do not flush. Come back in half an hour. A wet trail down the back of the pan, or a moved line, is a leak. This costs nothing and needs no kit.

The meter test for the whole house. Turn off every tap and appliance, read the meter, leave it an hour with nothing running, and read it again. If the numbers have moved, something in the house is using water. That catches leaks a toilet test will not, including underground supply pipe leaks, which are a different and much more serious problem.

If you do find a leak and need someone, Severn Trent points customers at the WaterSafe approved plumber list, which is a national scheme rather than a company one. The part itself is usually a flush valve or a float valve, both of which are routine replacements rather than major work.

4. A worked example

Bernadette, Derby. A Severn Trent customer, metered, three in the house, dual flush toilet fitted when the bathroom was done in 2021. Her bill has crept up for two years and she has put it down to rising prices, which is reasonable: Severn Trent says its typical combined bill rose 9.8 per cent in April 2026 alone, from £1.47 to £1.61 a day.

She does the pencil test on a Saturday morning. Thirty minutes later there is a clear wet trail down the back of the pan. The flush valve seal has perished.

She cannot measure the rate exactly, so she works it out from the bill instead. Her metered consumption is roughly 60 cubic metres a year higher than it was in 2022, with no change in the household. Sixty cubic metres at £4.0945 is £245.67 a year, which implies a leak of around 164 litres a day, comfortably inside Severn Trent's range.

The plumber charges her £110 including the valve. Against £245.67 a year, that is £110 divided by £245.67, which is 0.45 of a year, or about 163 days. Everything after that is money she keeps.

The part of this worth copying is not the fix, it is the diagnosis. She did not need to know the litres per day. She needed to know how much her consumption had moved and what a cubic metre costs, and both of those are printed on her own bill.

The verdict

Test your toilet this week, because the test is free and a bad leak costs about £600 a year at 2026/27 metered rates. There is no purchase to weigh up and no payback period to worry about until you already know you have a leak.

If you find one and you are metered, fix it immediately, because even a £150 callout is back in your pocket in about three months on a 400 litre a day leak. If you are unmetered, fix it anyway, but know that you are doing it for the water rather than the money, and we are not going to dress that up as a saving.

Frequently asked

How do I know if my toilet is leaking if I cannot hear it? Most leaks are silent. Use a dye tablet in the cistern, which many water companies give away free, or dry the back of the pan and mark the water line, then check again half an hour later without flushing. A wet trail means a leak.

Why are dual flush toilets worse? Severn Trent's water resources lead calls them the prime culprits. He points to two things: people not understanding the two buttons and using the large flush by default, and the buttons themselves getting stuck, which leaves a constant stream of water running into the pan all day without a sound.

Will my water company help with the cost? Do not count on it for a toilet. Severn Trent's 2026/27 scheme of charges offers one leakage allowance per occupier for a leak on the supply, repaired within 28 days and not caused by negligence. It is written around supply pipe leaks, so ask before assuming a leaking cistern qualifies.

Does this matter if I am not on a meter? Not to your bill. An unmetered bill is based on rateable value and does not move with usage, so a leak costs you nothing in money. It is still 146 cubic metres a year of treated drinking water at 400 litres a day.
