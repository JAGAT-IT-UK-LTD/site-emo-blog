The Cheap Land Registry Download Is Not Proof of Anything, and That Catches People Out

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked September 2026

HM Land Registry sells the same property information at three prices, including free. Which one you need depends entirely on whether you are satisfying your own curiosity or satisfying a court, and the difference between those two is four pounds and a postal delay.

The short version

  • A property summary is free. It gives the address, the description, the tenure type, and whether restrictive covenants or easements exist at all.
  • A title register or a title plan costs £7 each to download. The register names the owner, the last sale price, whether there is a mortgage and the detail of covenants and easements.
  • Downloaded copies cannot be used as proof of ownership. GOV.UK states this plainly.
  • An official copy costs £11 per document and cannot be downloaded. It has to be posted to you.
  • This covers England and Wales. Scotland uses Registers of Scotland, Northern Ireland has its own land registry.
  • The free summary tells you whether covenants exist, not what they say. To read them you have to buy the register.

1. Three prices for the same property

HM Land Registry lets anyone look up information about any registered property in England or Wales, whether or not you own it. There are three tiers, and the pricing is unusual in that the cheapest tier is genuinely free.

What you getCostHow you get it
Property summaryFreeDownload
Title register£7Download
Title plan£7Download
Official copy (register or plan)£11 per documentPosted to you only

The free property summary contains the address as held by Royal Mail, the property description, the tenure type, and whether there are any restrictive covenants or easements. Note that last part carefully. It tells you whether they exist. It does not tell you what they say.

The title register at £7 is what people mean when they say title deeds. It usually carries the title number, who owns the property, how much it last sold for, whether there is a mortgage on it, and the detail of any restrictive covenants and easements. In some cases even the register does not spell the covenants out, and instead names the document that does.

The title plan, also £7, shows the property's location and general boundaries. The word general is doing real work there, and we come back to it below.

2. Why the download is not proof

This is the part worth the price of the article. GOV.UK says it in one sentence: you can download online copies of the information but you cannot use them as proof of ownership.

If you need to prove who owns a property, for example in a court case, you have to order an official copy. Official copies cannot be downloaded at all. They are sent to you by post and cost £11 per document rather than £7.

So the £4 gap is not buying you better information. The content is the same. It is buying you a document that carries evidential status, which the downloaded version does not have.

People discover this at the worst moment, usually when a solicitor or a court office rejects a PDF they printed off six weeks earlier. If there is any chance the document is going to be used in a dispute rather than just read, order the official copy from the start and skip the £7 entirely.

3. Which one you actually need

Work down this list and stop at the first one that matches.

  1. You want to know who owns a house, or whether it has covenants at all. The free property summary. Do not pay anything.
  2. You want to read what a covenant or easement actually says, or find out what the property last sold for, or whether there is a mortgage on it. Title register, £7.
  3. You want to see the extent and general boundaries. Title plan, £7. Buying both the register and the plan is £14.
  4. Anything that might end up in front of a court, a tribunal or a formal dispute. Official copy, £11 per document, posted.

You need to sign in to use the service, and you need a debit or credit card for anything that is not free.

4. Worked example: Sinead in Colchester

Sinead is buying a terraced house and the estate agent has mentioned in passing that there is a covenant about the front garden. She wants to know what it says before she spends money on a survey.

What she should do first. Pull the free property summary. It costs nothing and will confirm whether restrictive covenants exist on the title. If the answer is no, she has saved £7 and can stop.

What she should do next. If covenants do exist, buy the title register at £7. That is the document that tells her what the covenant requires, or names the deed that does.

What she should not do. Buy the title plan at the same time out of habit. Her conveyancer will obtain the plan as part of the purchase. Buying it herself is £7 spent twice.

Her total cost is £7, not £14, and not £22. And if the covenant turns out to be a genuine problem she is arguing about later, that is the point at which the £11 official copy becomes worth ordering, not before.

One more thing worth knowing for anyone at this stage of a purchase: the Land Registry fee is trivially small next to the transaction tax, and we have set out what stamp duty actually costs a first-time buyer in 2026 separately.

5. What this does not tell you

Three honest limits, because a £7 document can give false confidence.

Boundaries are general, not exact. The title plan shows general boundaries. It is not a surveyed line you can use to settle a fence dispute to the centimetre. HM Land Registry has separate guidance on property boundaries for exactly this reason.

Not everything is registered. Some land in England and Wales has never been registered, usually because it has not changed hands since registration became compulsory in the area. If a search returns nothing, that is not always an error.

It says nothing about flooding, planning or local charges. Flood risk reports for properties in England come from the Environment Agency and are free. Local land charges are a separate search. Neither is in the title register.

The honest verdict

Start free. The property summary answers the two questions most people actually have, which are who owns it and whether there is anything odd attached to it, and it costs nothing. Only move to the £7 title register when you need to read the covenant rather than just know it exists.

The one rule worth remembering is the one GOV.UK states outright and almost nobody reads: a downloaded copy is not proof of ownership. If the document is going anywhere near a court, pay the £11 and wait for the post. Paying £7 first and £11 afterwards is the expensive way to learn that.

Frequently asked

Can I find out who owns a house I do not own? Yes. The register is public. You can search for any registered property in England or Wales, and the free property summary plus the 7 pound title register will name the owner.

Is the 7 pound title register the same document as the 11 pound official copy? The content is the same. The difference is status. The official copy can be used as proof of ownership, for example in a court case, and the downloaded version cannot.

Why can I not download the official copy? HM Land Registry only issues official copies by post. That is what makes them official copies rather than downloads, and it is why they take longer to arrive.

What if the property is in Scotland or Northern Ireland? These fees do not apply. Scotland uses Registers of Scotland and Northern Ireland has its own land registry, each with its own fees and process.
