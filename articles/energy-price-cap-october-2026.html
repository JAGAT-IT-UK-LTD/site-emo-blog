Energy Price Cap October 2026: Up 4% to £1,723, and the VAT Cut Is Hiding a 5.8% Electricity Rise

The energy price cap for October 2026 is £1,723 a year for a typical home on direct debit, up 4% or £60, and it runs from 1 October to 31 December 2026. Under that headline, electricity rose 5.8% before tax and a VAT cut paid for it, while gas rose 8.7%. Ofgem sets the January 2027 cap on 25 November, so this is the last full look at the rates before then.

The short version

  • Set by Ofgem on 26 August 2026: the cap rose 4% to £1,723 a year for a typical direct debit household, from 1 October to 31 December 2026. That is £60 a year, or £5 a month, on typical use.
  • The electricity unit rate rose 5.8% before tax. It looks like it barely moved, from 26.11p to 26.32p per kWh, but the old figure included 5% VAT and the new one includes none. Strip the VAT out and July's rate was 24.87p.
  • Gas is doing the damage. The gas unit rate is up 8.7%, from 7.33p to 7.97p per kWh. Gas is now about half of a typical dual fuel bill.
  • The VAT cut now lasts longer. The 28 October Budget extended 0% VAT on household electricity to 30 June 2027. It was due to end on 31 March 2027.
  • £1,723 is not comparable to the numbers you remember. Ofgem shrank its typical household in July 2026. On the old yardstick, the same rates come to £1,935.
  • Payment method is worth £138 a year. Direct debit £1,723, prepayment £1,678, standard credit (paying on receipt of a bill) £1,861.
  • The January 2027 cap is not out yet. Ofgem announces it on 25 November 2026. Anything you read before then is a forecast.
  • Electricity only homes are roughly flat. Below about 4,100 kWh a year you pay slightly less than in July, above it slightly more. At 26.32p a unit the big electricity items are worth checking, and a hot tub on its own runs to several hundred pounds a year.

1. What is the energy price cap for October 2026?

Ofgem announced the energy price cap for October 2026 at 7am on Wednesday 26 August 2026. It covers 1 October to 31 December 2026.

For a typical household paying by direct debit for gas and electricity, the cap went from £1,663 to £1,723 a year. That is a rise of 4%, or £5 a month. If the real problem is what you already owe rather than the next bill, the Ofgem 2026 debt relief scheme sets out whose arrears can be written off.

The other two payment methods get far less coverage. Ofgem's level tables give these figures:

  • Prepayment meter: £1,678 a year. Still the cheapest capped rate, about £45 a year below direct debit.
  • Standard credit, paying on receipt of a bill: £1,861 a year. That is £138 more than direct debit for exactly the same energy.

Here are the price cap rates per kWh that actually govern your bill, from Ofgem's published tables. These are the averages for England, Scotland and Wales on direct debit. Your region will differ.

Electricity. 26.11p per kWh and 57.19p a day, moving to 26.32p per kWh and 54.83p a day.

Gas. 7.33p per kWh and 29.04p a day, rising to 7.97p per kWh and 29.68p a day.

Electricity prices from 1 October include no VAT. Gas still carries 5%.

Ofgem put the rise down to wholesale gas prices, up 11% over the three months before the announcement, driven by the conflict in the Middle East. About 35% of households, around 11 million, are on fixed tariffs and were not affected. Standing charges and repairs are the two costs a fix does not touch, which is why boiler cover against paying for repairs yourself is worth pricing separately.

2. Is the VAT cut hiding an electricity price rise?

Look at the electricity unit rate again. 26.11p to 26.32p is a rise of less than one percent. Ofgem's press release described electricity bills as broadly stable, and most coverage repeated it.

That same unit rate decides whether charging a car at home beats public charging, so what an EV home charger costs to install only makes sense read next to it.

It is stable for you, this quarter. It is not stable underneath.

The 26.11p you paid in July included 5% VAT. The 26.32p you pay now includes none, because the government zero rated household electricity from 1 October 2026. So the like for like comparison is 24.87p against 26.32p.

Unit rates are what turn an appliance into money. They decide whether to swap the last halogen bulbs now or wait for each one to fail, and they explain why a C on the rescaled label is not the old C.

The pre tax price of electricity went up 5.8%. The VAT cut absorbed all of it and a little more.

The standing charge did the same. It seems to fall from 57.19p to 54.83p a day. Strip the VAT out of July's figure and it was 54.47p, so the underlying charge went up by 0.7%.

This matters because the zero rate has an end date. The Budget on 28 October 2026 moved it from 31 March 2027 to 30 June 2027, and HMRC's VAT on fuel and power notice now gives that date. If 5% VAT comes back on 1 July 2027 on top of today's base, 26.32p becomes 27.64p and the standing charge becomes 57.57p.

Nothing in the wholesale market has to change for that jump. The tax break simply stops covering a rise that already happened. The full detail, including who qualifies, is in our guide to the electricity VAT cut and what it is worth to you.

None of this is hidden or improper. Ofgem's own note says the rise would have been about £45 higher without the VAT change. But "electricity bills stay broadly stable" and "the price of electricity did not rise" are two different statements. Only the first one is true.

3. Why is £1,723 not the number you remember?

In July 2026, Ofgem changed its Typical Domestic Consumption Values. These are the assumed yearly usage it multiplies the rates by to get the headline figure.

The typical household went from 2,700 kWh of electricity and 11,500 kWh of gas a year to 2,500 kWh and 9,500 kWh. That is about 7% less electricity and 17% less gas.

Run the same October rates against the old yardstick and the cap comes out at £1,935, up from £1,862. Ofgem gives both figures in the notes to its press release, and our own sums against the published unit rates match them to the pound.

So the percentage is honest. Four percent is four percent on either measure. What changed is the number people anchor to.

If your house has not become 17% more gas efficient since 2023, £1,723 was never going to be your bill. The gap between headline and reality just got wider by about £200. Some households get help without applying, and the rules on who receives the 150 pound Warm Home Discount automatically are worth reading before you ring anyone.

Ofgem says plainly that the new values lowered the bill figure. Your own meter readings are the only yardstick that matters for your budget.

4. What happens to the price cap in January 2027?

Nobody knows yet. Ofgem's published timetable says it will announce the cap for 1 January to 31 March 2027 on 25 November 2026. As of 9 November 2026, that figure has not been published.

Ofgem then sets the April to June 2027 cap on 23 February 2027 and the July to September 2027 cap on 26 May 2027. It can publish earlier if it needs to.

Energy price cap predictions for January 2027 are already everywhere, and they do not agree. They are forecasts from wholesale prices, not the cap. We keep them in one place in what the January 2027 cap is expected to do, and we will update this guide with Ofgem's real figure once it is out.

One thing is already settled. The electricity VAT cut now covers the whole of January to March 2027 and the April to June quarter too. So the January cap will still be quoted with 0% VAT on electricity and 5% on gas.

5. Worked example: the Begum household in Watford

A three bedroom semi with gas central heating, two working adults and a teenager. Their smart meter says they used 3,100 kWh of electricity and 12,000 kWh of gas over the last twelve months. That is above typical, which is true of most family homes that size.

At July rates: electricity 3,100 × 26.11p = £809.41, plus 365 days × 57.19p = £208.74. Gas 12,000 × 7.33p = £879.60, plus 365 × 29.04p = £106.00.
Annual total: £2,003.75

At October rates: electricity 3,100 × 26.32p = £815.92, plus 365 × 54.83p = £200.13. Gas 12,000 × 7.97p = £956.40, plus 365 × 29.68p = £108.33.
Annual total: £2,080.78

The increase is £77 a year, not £60. And their bill is £358 above the headline £1,723.

Two things to take from that. Their electricity cost fell by about £2, because the lower standing charge offset the higher unit rate. Their gas rose by £79, so the gas side did all the damage.

Most of a year's gas is burned in the colder months. So more of that rise lands this winter than it will next summer.

The crossover point is worth knowing if you have no gas at all. The electricity standing charge fell in cash terms while the unit rate rose, and the two cancel out at about 4,100 kWh a year. Below that, your electricity costs slightly less than in July. Above it, slightly more.

The quickest way for an all electric home to get below that line is hot water, where a cylinder on a timer beats an immersion left on all day. Either way the change is small, which is good news for heat pump and all electric homes after several bad years.

The same unit rate makes summer cooling expensive. A portable air conditioner run through a hot spell costs far more than most people think before they meter it. The headline figure is worked through in what the 4 percent rise to a 1,723 pound cap adds to a typical annual bill.

6. What is actually worth doing now?

Four things, in order of how much they are worth.

Check the opening reading on your October bill. If your supplier estimated across 30 September, it can charge September usage at October rates. Send a photo of the meter if the figure looks wrong. If you have a smart meter the reading goes in on its own, which is one of the few differences a smart meter makes to what you are actually billed.

Check whether you pay on receipt of a bill. That £138 a year gap is the largest free saving in this cap, and it is entirely in your control. Prepayment is cheaper still on capped rates, though switching to it on purpose is rarely the right move for other reasons.

Look at fixed deals, with your own numbers, before 25 November. Ofgem said fixes were available at £100 or more below the October cap. That is a real saving if your usage looks like the typical household, and worth more if you use a lot of gas. Compare unit rates and standing charges, not headline annual figures, and check exit fees. The method is in working out whether your fix is beating the cap or quietly losing to it, and the timing question is in whether to fix now or wait for the 25 November announcement.

Do the cheap draught proofing now. Gas is about half of a typical dual fuel bill and it is the part that rose. Sealing a draughty door and putting foil behind radiators on an outside wall costs little and works on the largest line on your bill. If you rent, choose the kind that unclips when you leave, which is what belongs in an energy saving kit you can take with you to the next tenancy.

The honest verdict

A £5 a month rise is not a crisis, and for homes with no gas it is close to nothing. That is the accurate headline and it deserves saying plainly.

The two things worth carrying away are less comfortable. Electricity did not stay flat. It rose 5.8% before tax and a temporary VAT cut paid for it, so 1 July 2027 is now the date to watch. And £1,723 is measured against a smaller household than the one in your head, so budget against your own kWh.

There is nothing to buy here and no rush. Check your October opening reading, get off paying on receipt if you are on it, and spend twenty pounds on draught proofing. Then wait for the real January figure on 25 November before you decide on a fix.

Frequently asked questions about the October 2026 price cap

Is my bill capped at £1,723? No. The cap limits unit rates and standing charges, not your total bill. £1,723 is what a typical home using 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas pays on direct debit. If you use more, you pay more, and most family homes use more than that.

What is the energy price cap per kWh from October 2026? On direct debit, averaged across England, Scotland and Wales, electricity is 26.32p per kWh with a 54.83p daily standing charge. Gas is 7.97p per kWh with a 29.68p daily standing charge. Electricity carries no VAT and gas carries 5%. Your own region's rates will be a little different.

When will the January 2027 price cap be announced? Ofgem's timetable says 25 November 2026, for the period 1 January to 31 March 2027. Until then any figure you see is a forecast based on wholesale prices. The electricity VAT cut runs to 30 June 2027, so it will still be inside the January figure.

Can I still get the £150 Warm Home Discount this winter? In England and Wales, eligibility for winter 2026 to 2027 was judged on 23 August 2026. You needed a qualifying benefit on that date and your name on the electricity bill. Your supplier applies the discount by 31 March 2027. Full rules are in our Warm Home Discount guide for winter 2026.

Does any of this apply in Northern Ireland? No. Ofgem's cap covers England, Scotland and Wales only, and Northern Ireland has its own regulator and market. The electricity VAT cut does not apply there either, so household electricity in Northern Ireland stays at the reduced 5% VAT rate, HMRC says.

The VAT position is split as well: gas stayed at 5% while electricity went to zero, so a gas heavy home sees far less of the cut than the headline suggests.
