Whether You Need a Licence Comes Down to Where the Animal Sleeps

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Pet sitting covers several different jobs and the law treats them very differently. One piece of legislation decides it for England, it is short, and it is worth reading the actual list rather than a summary of a summary.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The instrument is SI 2018/486, the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018. Schedule 1 lists every licensable activity.
  • Four boarding activities are named. Providing boarding for cats, boarding in kennels for dogs, home boarding for dogs, and day care for dogs.
  • The condition is accommodation for OTHER people's cats or dogs, in the course of a business, on premises where providing that accommodation is a purpose of the business.
  • Dog walking is not in Schedule 1. Neither is visiting and feeding a pet in the owner's own home. Those are not licensable activities under these Regulations.
  • The business test is deliberately wide. A council must consider whether you carry on the activity with a view to profit, or earn any commission or fee from it.
  • England only. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own regimes, and this instrument does not apply there.

1. What Schedule 1 actually lists

The question "do I need a licence to pet sit" gets answered in blog posts far more often than it gets answered from the legislation. The legislation is Schedule 1 of the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018, and Part 3 is the part that concerns pet sitters.

Paragraph 4 describes the licensable activity as: "Providing or arranging for the provision of accommodation for other people's cats or dogs in the course of a business on any premises where the provision of that accommodation is a purpose of the business by:

  • providing boarding for cats;
  • providing boarding in kennels for dogs;
  • providing home boarding for dogs; or
  • providing day care for dogs."

That is the whole list for boarding. Four items. Schedule 1 also covers selling animals as pets, hiring out horses, breeding dogs, and keeping or training animals for exhibition, none of which apply to an ordinary pet sitter.

Note two words that do a lot of work. Other people's. Looking after your own animals is obviously not in scope. And accommodation, which is the concept the whole Part turns on.

2. The dividing line, in one sentence

Read against the four items, the rule that emerges is simple enough to hold in your head:

If other people's cats or dogs come to your premises, you are probably licensable. If you go to theirs, Schedule 1 does not list what you are doing.

So, taking the common versions of the job:

What you doNamed in Schedule 1?
Dogs stay overnight at your homeYes, home boarding for dogs
Dogs come to you for the day and go home at nightYes, day care for dogs
Cats boarded at your premisesYes, boarding for cats
Purpose-built kennelsYes, boarding in kennels for dogs
You walk dogs and return them homeNot listed
You visit, feed and let out a pet at the owner's houseNot listed
You house sit at the owner's home while they are awayNot listed
You arrange boarding by matching owners with other hostsYes, arranging for the provision is expressly included

That last row is worth pausing on, because it is the one people miss. The words are "providing or arranging for the provision of accommodation". Running the booking platform is caught as well as running the spare room.

3. The business test catches more people than expected

Every activity in Schedule 1 is qualified by "in the course of a business", and Part 1 of the same Schedule tells the council how to decide that.

Paragraph 1: the circumstances a local authority must take into account "include, for example, whether the operator (a) makes any sale by, or otherwise carries on, the activity with a view to making a profit, or (b) earns any commission or fee from the activity."

Read limb (b) slowly. It is not a turnover threshold. It is not a question of whether this is your main job. Earning any fee is one of the things the council must take into account.

This does not mean every person who ever took twenty pounds to mind a neighbour's dog is committing an offence. The Schedule says these are circumstances the authority must take into account, and it says "include, for example", so the list is not exhaustive and the judgement sits with the council. But anyone planning to board dogs at home regularly for money should not be relying on being too small to count. That is not what the test says.

The practical step is dull and effective: ring your own council's licensing team and describe what you actually do. Fees, inspection arrangements and star ratings are set locally and vary widely, so there is no national figure to quote here, and this page will not invent one.

4. What the Regulations do not do

Being precise about the limits matters as much as the list itself.

They do not apply outside England. This is a statutory instrument for England. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate arrangements, and you cannot read this Schedule across.

They do not make dog walking licensable. Some councils operate separate dog walking permits for their own parks and open spaces under different powers, and commercial walkers are often subject to limits on how many dogs they may walk at once in particular areas. That is a local land management rule, not this Schedule.

They do not remove your other duties. The Animal Welfare Act 2006 duty of care applies to anyone responsible for an animal whether or not any licence exists.

They do not set your prices, and nothing does. There is no official or published rate for pet sitting in the UK, and any article that quotes one is quoting somebody's guess. What you can do is build a rate from things you can actually count: your time, your travel, your insurance, and, if you are licensable, the council fee and the cost of meeting the conditions.

They do not tell HMRC anything. A licence and a tax return are unrelated. Gross trading income over £1,000 in a tax year takes you into Self Assessment regardless of licensing.

Worked example: Fiona in Shrewsbury

Fiona starts offering three services and assumes one licence covers all of it. Schedule 1 says otherwise.

Midday visits to let dogs out at the owners' houses. Not listed in Schedule 1. No licence under these Regulations.

Group walks from a car, dogs returned home afterwards. Not listed in Schedule 1. She checks her council's rules on commercial dog walking in its parks separately, because that is a different power.

Two dogs staying at her house while owners are on holiday. This is home boarding for dogs, named at paragraph 4(c), it is for other people's dogs, and she is charging a fee, which paragraph 1(b) tells the council to take into account. This one needs a licence.

What she decides. She keeps the first two running immediately and applies before taking any boarding bookings, because the boarding is the only part that is licensable and the only part with a lead time. Two thirds of her business was never waiting on the council.

What she does not do is assume the answer is the same in all three cases, which is the mistake the single word accommodation is there to prevent.

5. Insurance, which is a separate question

Licensing and insurance get discussed together and they are not the same thing. Nothing in Schedule 1 is an insurance requirement, though a council setting licence conditions will generally expect appropriate cover to be in place for a licensable activity.

The exposures for this work are specific: an animal in your care is injured or becomes ill, an animal you are responsible for injures a person or another animal, a key you hold is lost, or damage occurs in a client's home while you are there. Public liability alone does not automatically deal with all of that, and care, custody and control of the animal is the part that general policies most often exclude.

The honest instruction here is not a product recommendation. It is to ask any insurer to confirm in writing that the specific activities you carry out are covered, naming home boarding, day care, walking or key holding as applicable, before you rely on the policy. This site does not recommend individual insurance products.

The honest verdict

For England, the answer is in four lines of Schedule 1. Boarding cats, boarding dogs in kennels, home boarding dogs and dog day care are licensable when done as a business with other people's animals on premises where that accommodation is a purpose of the business. Arranging that boarding for others is caught too.

Dog walking, drop-in visits and house sitting at the owner's home are not in that list. The dividing line is whose premises the animal is on, and the practical shorthand is where the animal sleeps.

Where this page does not help you: it cannot tell you your council's fee, because those are set locally and vary widely, and it cannot price your service, because no official rate exists. It also does not apply outside England. Ring your own licensing team, describe what you actually do rather than what you call it, and get the answer for your own address.

Frequently asked

Do I need a licence to walk dogs in England? Not under these Regulations. Dog walking is not one of the activities listed in Schedule 1 of SI 2018/486. Some councils separately restrict or permit commercial dog walking on their own land under different powers, so check your local authority's rules for its parks and open spaces.

What if I only board one dog occasionally for a friend? The Schedule qualifies each activity with in the course of a business, and Part 1 tells the council to take into account whether you make a profit or earn any commission or fee. There is no exemption based on being small, so describe your actual arrangement to your council rather than assuming.

Does the licence cover me across the country? No. Licences are issued by the local authority for the premises, and these Regulations are for England only. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland operate separate regimes.

How much should I charge? There is no published or official rate, and this site will not print a made-up one. Build it from your own numbers: hours including travel, fuel, insurance, any council fee, and the cost of meeting licence conditions. Then check what that implies as an hourly rate before you decide.
