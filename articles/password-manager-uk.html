Password Manager UK: Is the Free One Enough?

22 August 2026 · 8 min read · Updated 18 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026

For most UK households, a free password manager is enough. Bitwarden Free and Proton Pass Free both store unlimited passwords on unlimited devices, and the one built into your phone or browser is fine if you use one platform. Paying only makes sense for family sharing, emergency access or a mix of devices your free option will not cover.

That matters because the real danger is not a clever hacker guessing your password. It is one old password, reused, opening every account you own.

The short version
  • For most households the free tier is genuinely enough. Bitwarden Free and Proton Pass Free both allow unlimited devices.
  • Bitwarden Premium works out at about £17.50 a year including VAT for a UK buyer, and Families about £42.33 a year for up to six people. Both are converted from US dollars, so they move with the exchange rate.
  • The one already on your phone and laptop is better than the excellent one you never set up.
  • The real win is never reusing a password, because reuse turns one company's breach into your problem.
  • Turn on two step verification on your email first. The NCSC calls it the most important step.
  • Write down the master password and the recovery code and keep them somewhere physical and safe.

Why does a password manager matter?

The threat most people imagine is someone guessing their password. That is not what usually happens.

What happens is credential stuffing. A company you signed up to years ago is breached. Your email address and password end up in a list.

Software then tries that pair against hundreds of other sites, automatically and cheaply. If you reused the password, the attacker is in your email. From your email they can reset everything else.

The National Cyber Security Centre puts it plainly. If you reuse a password, criminals only need one password to get into all your accounts.

So the most valuable change is not password strength. It is uniqueness. A different password everywhere means one breach stays one breach.

A password manager is simply the only practical way to keep a hundred different passwords and still log in.

Check whether your address is already in known breaches. Have I Been Pwned lets you search an email address against breached sites. On the day we checked, its home page listed 1,052 breached websites and over 17.8 billion breached addresses. Most people find at least one.

Worked example: one family, three choices

Take the Okafor family in Leeds. Two adults and two teenagers. Mum uses an iPhone, Dad an Android, and the shared laptop runs Windows.

A mixed household like this is exactly where built in managers get awkward. Apple's passwords sit best on Apple devices, Google's on Android and Chrome.

Choice one, all free. Four Bitwarden Free accounts cost £0. Each person gets unlimited devices. The catch is sharing. Each account can share with only one other user, so the Netflix and broadband logins need some juggling.

Choice two, Bitwarden Families. About £42.33 a year covers up to six people. That is roughly £10.58 a head for the four of them, or about £3.53 a month for the household. They get unlimited sharing, emergency access and authenticator codes.

Choice three, 1Password Families. $4.49 a month in year one is $53.88, rising to $71.88 a year at the $5.99 price. It covers five people, so there is room for one more. The sterling cost depends on the exchange rate on the day.

Our view for the Okafors: start free. If the sharing limit irritates them after a month, Bitwarden Families is the cheapest upgrade we found with a confirmed UK price.

When is paying worth it?

Paying makes sense in four situations, and not really otherwise.

  1. Your free option will not reach all your devices. If it does not sync, you will not use it, and an unused password manager protects nothing.
  2. You share logins with a partner or family. Family plans are the best value in this category. The alternative is passwords in a WhatsApp message.
  3. You want emergency access. If something happened to you, could anyone get into your accounts? People dismiss this feature and then wish they had it.
  4. You run a business. Shared team vaults with per person access are worth paying for. A spreadsheet of passwords is not a substitute. Our list of the best free software for a UK small business covers the rest of the toolkit.

What is not a good reason to pay: storage you will never use, or a dark web report that a free breach checker already gives you.

A bundled VPN is not a reason either. Most people do not need one, as our guide to VPNs in the UK explains.

Is Google or Apple's built in password manager good enough?

Yes, for many people. Every major phone, computer and browser now has a password manager built in, free, syncing across that maker's devices.

The NCSC says browser and device managers are fine on your own devices. Its caveat is not to save passwords on shared or public computers, and to keep automatic updates on.

The real weakness is mixing platforms. An iPhone with a Windows laptop, or an Android with a Mac, is where built in options get awkward. Awkward means abandoned. Mixed households are the strongest case for a separate manager, and the NCSC makes the same point.

One more caution. If someone has your computer unlocked, browser saved passwords can be easy to view. That is a reason to lock your laptop, not a reason to avoid the feature.

Is a password manager safe if the company is breached?

This is the obvious objection, and it deserves a straight answer.

Reputable password managers use zero knowledge encryption. Your vault is locked and unlocked on your device with a key from your master password. The provider never holds that key.

It has been tested in real life. In December 2025 the Information Commissioner's Office fined LastPass UK Ltd £1.2 million over a 2022 breach affecting up to 1.6 million UK users. A hacker reached a backup database holding names, email addresses, phone numbers and stored website addresses.

The ICO found no evidence that the hackers could decrypt customer passwords. It still calls password managers a safe and effective tool.

The honest caveat is that a stolen vault is only as safe as its master password. A short or reused one can be attacked offline at leisure.

So the rule is this. Make the master password the long one. The NCSC suggests three random words, which are easier to remember and harder to crack than a short string of symbols. Never use it anywhere else.

How do you set one up in an hour?

Do not try to move two hundred accounts on day one. You will stop halfway and give up.

  1. Pick one manager. Built in if you live on one platform, a cross platform one if you do not. Finishing matters more than the choice.
  2. Set a long master password. Three or more random words. Write it on paper.
  3. Save the recovery code somewhere physical, with the master password. The NCSC says writing passwords down is fine if you keep the record secure.
  4. Fix email first. A new unique password and two step verification. Email is the reset route for everything else.
  5. Then the money accounts. Bank, PayPal, anything with a card saved.
  6. Then let it happen naturally. Each time you log into something over the next month, change that password and save it. The important ones get done with no marathon session.
  7. Run the health check if your manager has one. Fix reused passwords first.

If a scammer does get in and talks you into sending money, your bank may have to refund you. Our guide to APP fraud reimbursement explains what you are owed.

What about two step verification and passkeys?

Two step verification matters more than password strength on your key accounts. The NCSC calls turning it on the first and most important step.

In rough order of security: a hardware key, then an authenticator app, then a text message code. Text codes are the weakest because of SIM swap fraud, but they are still far better than nothing.

Turn it on for the password manager itself too. Then someone who learns your master password still cannot get in.

Passkeys are where this is heading, and the NCSC now calls them its first choice of login. A passkey replaces the password with a key stored on your device and unlocked by your fingerprint, face or PIN.

There is nothing to type, nothing to reuse and nothing to phish. Both Bitwarden Free and Proton Pass Free store passkeys.

Adoption is uneven. Many large services offer passkeys, plenty do not, and recovery after losing a device is still the rough edge. Use passkeys where offered and keep the manager for everything else.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to use a password manager? Yes, for almost everyone it is far safer than reusing passwords. The ICO fined LastPass £1.2 million over a 2022 breach, yet found no evidence customer passwords were decrypted, and it still calls password managers safe and effective. The weak point is a short master password, so make yours long.

What is the best free password manager in the UK? Bitwarden Free and Proton Pass Free both give unlimited passwords on unlimited devices, a generator and passkey support. Bitwarden is open source and can be self hosted. Proton Pass Free adds weak password alerts and ten email aliases. Either is a sound start; pick the one whose app you find easier.

Is Google Password Manager safe enough? On your own devices, yes. The NCSC says built in browser and device managers are fine, as long as you keep updates on and avoid saving passwords on shared computers. Its weakness is mixed households, for example an iPhone with a Windows laptop, where a cross platform manager is easier to keep using.

Is a notebook in a drawer really that bad? Not for a home user with no visitors. The NCSC says writing passwords down is fine if the record is kept secure, and a notebook beats remote attackers. The problems are that it cannot generate strong passwords, it is not with you when you are out, and a fire or flood takes it all.

Should I change all my passwords regularly? No. Forced regular changes lead to weaker, patterned passwords. Change a password when there is a reason: a breach, a suspicion, or someone leaving a shared account. Use a unique password for every site, check Have I Been Pwned now and then, and let your manager flag reused or breached ones.

The verdict

Use whichever password manager you will actually use. For most UK households that is a free one, either Bitwarden Free, Proton Pass Free or the one built into your devices. Make the master password long, turn on two step verification for your email today, and stop reusing passwords. Paying is a convenience choice for families and mixed households, not a safety one.
