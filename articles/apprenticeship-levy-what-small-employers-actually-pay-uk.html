If Your Pay Bill Is Under £3 Million, the Apprenticeship Levy Is Not Your Problem

Ruhul Amin · Published 16 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The Apprenticeship Levy comes up constantly in small business conversations and it almost never applies to the people asking about it. The rule is two numbers long and they cancel each other out neatly. Here is the arithmetic, and the thing worth paying attention to instead.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The rate is 0.5% of your annual pay bill. It is payable monthly through PAYE.
  • It applies to employers with a pay bill of more than £3 million, including connected companies and charities.
  • There is a £15,000 annual allowance, which is exactly 0.5% of £3 million. That is not a coincidence.
  • So an employer at or under £3 million pays nothing. The allowance cancels the charge precisely.
  • Connected companies get one £15,000 allowance between them, which is where group structures get caught.
  • Not paying the levy does not shut you out of apprenticeships. Government funding for training is available to non-levy employers too.

1. The rule, in two numbers

HMRC states it plainly. Employers and connected companies with a total annual pay bill of more than £3 million are liable to the Apprenticeship Levy, which is payable monthly. Employers not connected to another company or charity get an annual allowance that reduces the amount payable.

For 2026 to 2027 the two figures are:

Apprenticeship Levy charge: 0.5% of the annual pay bill.

Apprenticeship Levy allowance: £15,000 a year.

Now notice what those two numbers do to each other. 0.5% of £3 million is exactly £15,000. The allowance is not a rough concession, it is the charge on the threshold, set so that the levy bites only on the pay bill above £3 million and not a pound below it.

That single observation answers most of the questions people have about this tax.

2. The arithmetic, at four different pay bills

Same calculation every time: take the pay bill, multiply by 0.5%, subtract £15,000, and if the answer is negative you pay nothing.

Pay bill £1.2 million. 0.5% is £6,000. Less £15,000 allowance. Nothing to pay.

Pay bill £3 million. 0.5% is £15,000. Less £15,000 allowance. Nothing to pay, exactly.

Pay bill £4 million. 0.5% is £20,000. Less £15,000 allowance. £5,000 to pay.

Pay bill £10 million. 0.5% is £50,000. Less £15,000 allowance. £35,000 to pay.

To be caught you need a pay bill above £3 million, which is roughly ninety people on £33,000, or fifty on £60,000. If your payroll does not look like that, the levy is not something you need to hold in your head.

3. Worked example: Neil's firm in Stoke-on-Trent

Neil, Stoke-on-Trent. Runs a fabrication business with 28 employees and an annual pay bill of about £1.2 million. He has been told by a training provider that taking on apprentices will involve the levy, and he has spent a fortnight worrying about a cost he was not going to incur.

His levy calculation. £1.2 million at 0.5% is £6,000. The allowance is £15,000. He owes nothing, and would owe nothing even if his pay bill doubled.

What he would have to reach. A pay bill above £3 million, which on his current average salary is roughly a hundred and twenty more employees.

The thing he should actually check. Whether his business is connected to another company. Neil owns a second, smaller company through the same holding structure. Connected employers share one £15,000 allowance and their pay bills are counted together. At a combined £1.6 million he is still well clear, but it is the question that decides the answer, and nobody had asked it.

4. Who does get caught, and how

Three situations account for most employers who are surprised by a levy bill.

  • Connected companies. The pay bills of connected companies and charities are added together and they share a single £15,000 allowance. Two businesses at £1.8 million each are individually nowhere near the threshold and jointly over it.
  • Growth through the threshold mid year. The levy is payable monthly, so an employer whose pay bill crosses £3 million during the year starts paying during the year rather than in a single reckoning at the end of it.
  • A pay bill that is bigger than the salary bill. The levy is charged on the pay bill, which is broader than basic salaries. Bonus heavy businesses can sit closer to the line than their headline salary costs suggest.

If any of those describe you, the levy is a real calculation and worth putting to your accountant rather than working out from a summary like this one.

5. What small employers should look at instead

The genuinely useful point is the one the levy question obscures. Not paying the levy does not exclude you from apprenticeships. Employers who are not levy payers can still access government funding towards the cost of apprenticeship training. The funding rules, the employer contribution and which standards are covered change from time to time, so the place to check is the apprenticeship funding guidance rather than any fixed percentage quoted in an article, including this one.

Three things that are worth your attention if you are a small employer thinking about an apprentice:

  • The wage, not the levy. Apprentices aged under 19, and those aged 19 and over in the first year of their apprenticeship, have a minimum hourly rate of £8 from 1 April 2026. After the first year, an apprentice aged 19 or over moves onto the ordinary rate for their age.
  • The employer National Insurance position. There is a separate NI category for apprentices under 25 with its own upper secondary threshold, which changes the employer cost meaningfully.
  • Employment Allowance. Far more relevant to a business of Neil's size than the levy ever will be, and worth £10,500 against the employer NI bill. Covered in our guide to who can claim Employment Allowance.

The honest verdict

This is a tax on large employers that occupies a disproportionate amount of small employer worry, and the reason is that the £3 million threshold is rarely stated alongside the 0.5% rate. Quoted on its own, 0.5% of a pay bill sounds like something everyone pays.

If your pay bill is under £3 million, including any connected companies, the answer is simply that you do not pay it, and there is no planning to do. The one check worth making is the connected companies question, because that is where an employer who is comfortably clear on their own numbers can turn out not to be. Everything else about apprenticeships, the wage rate, the National Insurance category and the training funding, matters far more to a small business than the levy does.

Frequently asked

My pay bill is £2.9 million. Do I pay anything? No. At £2.9 million the charge is £14,500 and the allowance is £15,000, so nothing is payable. The allowance is set at exactly 0.5% of £3 million, which is why the threshold and the allowance line up so cleanly.

Do connected companies each get £15,000? No. Connected companies and charities share a single £15,000 allowance between them, and you choose how it is allocated. This is the most common reason an employer who expected to be under the threshold turns out to be over it.

Can I take on an apprentice if I do not pay the levy? Yes. Non-levy employers can access government funding towards apprenticeship training. The employer contribution and the funding rules change periodically, so check the current apprenticeship funding guidance rather than relying on a percentage quoted anywhere, including here.

Is the levy the same thing as employer National Insurance? No, they are separate charges. Employer Class 1 National Insurance is 15% on earnings above a £5,000 secondary threshold for 2026 to 2027 and applies to employers of every size. The Apprenticeship Levy is 0.5% of the pay bill and only bites above £3 million.
