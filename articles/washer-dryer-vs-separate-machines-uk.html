A washer dryer label carries two of everything, and most people read the wrong half

Ruhul Amin · Published 9 November 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked November 2026

The choice between one machine that does both and two machines that each do one is usually presented as a question about how much space you have. It is really a question about a number the law already makes the manufacturer print on the label, in a column nobody looks at. Here is which number, and what it is worth in money.

The short version

  • Two of everything is a legal requirement. Regulation 2019/2014 makes a washer dryer label show two efficiency classes, two energy figures, two capacities, two water figures and two durations.
  • The left hand column is the complete cycle, meaning wash and dry in one go. That is the one that describes what you actually do with the machine.
  • The drying capacity is the smaller number, so a machine sold on its washing capacity may need two goes to dry one wash load.
  • On our stated illustrative figures the separates are about £33 a year cheaper to run, which against a £250 price gap is a payback of about 7.6 years.
  • So buy the washer dryer for space, not for money, and buy separates for money only if you were keeping them a long time anyway.

1. What the law makes the label show

The rules are in Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2019/2014, which applies in Great Britain as assimilated law, as modified by the Ecodesign for Energy-Related Products and Energy Information (Amendment) (EU Exit) Regulations 2020, S.I. 2020/1528, and further amended with effect from 1 October 2021 by S.I. 2021/1097. We read Annex III again on legislation.gov.uk on 9 November 2026, and it shows no outstanding UK changes.

For a plain washing machine, Annex III requires one of each: one energy efficiency class, one weighted energy consumption per 100 cycles in kWh, one rated capacity for the eco 40-60 programme, one weighted water consumption per cycle in litres, one programme duration, plus a spin drying efficiency class and a noise class.

For a washer dryer, the same annex requires two of each, laid out side by side. Two scales and two classes, for the complete cycle on the left and the washing cycle on the right. Two weighted energy figures per 100 cycles, one for each. Two rated capacities, one for each. Two weighted water consumption figures per cycle in litres, one for each. And two cycle durations.

That layout is doing something specific. The regulator knew that a washer dryer performs worse and holds less when it has to dry, and rather than average the two into one flattering figure, it required both to be printed next to each other. The information you need is already on the sticker. The difficulty is that the right hand column looks better, and it is the one that gets quoted.

2. The capacity number that decides everything

Annex I of the same regulation defines rated drying capacity as the maximum mass in kilograms, stated by the supplier at 0.5 kg intervals, of dry textiles of a particular type which can be treated in one drying cycle on the selected programme.

It is a separate declared figure from the washing capacity, and on washer dryers it is routinely a good deal smaller. That is not a defect, it is physics: drying needs air movement around the load, and a drum packed to its washing capacity has nowhere for that air to go.

The practical consequence is the thing to weigh. If a machine washes a full load but can only dry part of it, then every time you use it as a washer dryer you either wash a smaller load than the machine can handle, or you wash a full one and dry it in two goes. Both of those cost time, and the second costs energy twice over, because the drying phase runs from the start each time.

There is a second definition worth knowing, because it explains why the two energy figures are not comparable in the way you would expect. Annex I defines weighted energy consumption for the washing cycle as the weighted average at rated washing capacity and at half and at a quarter of it, while weighted energy consumption for the wash and dry cycle is the weighted average at rated capacity and at half of it only. The washing figure is averaged over three load sizes, the complete cycle figure over two. They are measured on different bases, so do not subtract one from the other and call the difference the cost of drying.

3. What the two routes cost to run

Now put numbers to it. Everything in this table is built from illustrative label figures, clearly labelled as such, because we do not publish consumption figures for named models that we have not read off a product information sheet. The rates are real: electricity at 26.32p per kWh from the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, and water at £4.0945 per cubic metre on Severn Trent's 2026/27 metered rates. That is £2.5503 for water supply plus used water at £1.6255, which Severn Trent's scheme of charges bills on 95 per cent of the water supplied, so £1.5442 of it. The assumption is 200 loads a year.

One warning on the electricity rate. When we checked on 9 November 2026, Ofgem had not yet announced the cap that starts on 1 January 2027, and the Commons Library reports forecasts of a rise of around 15 per cent in electricity unit prices. A higher rate makes every electricity figure below bigger, but it does not change the gap between the first two rows. Both use 500 kWh a year, so the whole difference between them is water.

The last row matters and is the reason this article does not simply tell you to buy separates. Not every washer dryer uses mains water to condense the drying air. Older and cheaper condensing designs do, which is where the 90 litres in row one comes from, and heat pump washer dryers generally do not. The label prints the water figure for the complete cycle, so you can tell which kind you are looking at before you buy, without asking anybody.

There is one cost the table cannot show and it belongs in the decision anyway. A washer dryer is a single point of failure. When it breaks you lose both functions at once, and a household that has given up its drying rack then has nowhere to put a week of wet clothes. Two machines fail independently, and a broken dryer is an inconvenience rather than a crisis. That is not something you can put a pound sign on, but on a purchase where the running cost difference is thirty odd pounds a year, the risk of losing both functions at once is arguably the larger factor. The durable things worth comparing at the point of sale are the ones this site always names: guarantee length, whether the retailer or the manufacturer stands behind it, and whether spare parts are sold separately.

Read down the payback column and the honest conclusion is that this is a slow decision either way. A £33 a year difference against a £250 price gap takes 7.6 years to come back, which is a long time for a laundry appliance. What changes the answer is behaviour: the row where half the year is line dried pays back in under three years, and the row where the dryer is barely used makes the whole comparison irrelevant.

4. A worked example

Kirsty, Dumfries. Two bed flat, kitchen with exactly one appliance space, currently a nine year old washing machine and a drying rack in the hall.

She wants to stop drying clothes in the hall and is choosing between a washer dryer in the existing space and a stacked pair, which would mean losing a cupboard.

In the shop she does the one thing this article is about: she reads the left hand column. The machine she likes is sold as an 8 kg machine, and the washing cycle column agrees. The complete cycle column says 5 kg. Her normal wash is a full drum of towels and bedding, which means every single time she wants them dried she will be running the drying phase twice.

That reframes it completely. On the running cost alone the gap between the two routes is around £33 a year on the illustrative figures above, and against a £250 price difference that is a payback of 7.6 years, which she would probably ignore. Two drying runs per wash is not a 7.6 year problem, it is a twice a week problem.

She buys the separates and loses the cupboard. If her wash loads had been small, or if the complete cycle capacity had been 7 kg rather than 5 kg, the washer dryer would have been the right answer and the £33 a year would not have been enough to change it.

The verdict

Buy the washer dryer if you have one appliance space, and stop feeling bad about it, because the running cost difference on our illustrative figures is about £33 a year and pays back a £250 gap only after about 7.6 years.

Before you buy either, read the complete cycle column on the label, which the law requires to be there. If the drying capacity is much smaller than your usual wash load, the machine will cost you two drying runs every time, and no energy label arithmetic will fix that.

Frequently asked

Why does a washer dryer label have two sets of numbers? Because Regulation 2019/2014 requires it. Annex III specifies two efficiency classes, two energy figures per 100 cycles, two rated capacities, two water figures and two durations, for the complete cycle on the left and the washing cycle on the right.

Do all washer dryers use extra water to dry? No. Condensing designs that use mains water to cool the drying air do, and heat pump designs generally do not. The label shows a water consumption figure for the complete cycle, so you can see which you are looking at.

Is the drying capacity always lower than the washing capacity? The regulation treats them as two separate declared figures and requires both to be shown, which tells you something in itself. Check the specific machine rather than assuming a ratio. The complete cycle capacity sits in the left hand column of the label, next to the washing capacity on the right, so the comparison takes seconds in the shop.

Can I compare the two energy figures on the label directly? Not by subtracting them. Annex I defines the washing cycle figure as a weighted average at rated, half and quarter capacity, and the wash and dry figure as a weighted average at rated and half capacity only. They are measured on different bases, so the gap is not the cost of drying.
