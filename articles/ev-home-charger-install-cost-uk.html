A Home Charger Does Not Cut the Price of Electricity. It Cuts the Time

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

1. Rate, not price, is the product

This is the point that gets lost in every conversation about home charging, so it is worth stating baldly. A wall charger does not change the price of a kilowatt hour. The electricity comes through the same meter on the same tariff whether it goes into the car through a 1,200 pound unit or through a lead into a socket in the garage.

What changes is the rate of delivery. A standard three pin socket, used with the granny lead that comes with most cars, delivers about 2.3kW continuously, and it is deliberately limited because a domestic socket and its wiring are not designed to carry 13 amps for fifteen hours at a stretch. A single phase home charger delivers 7.4kW, which is a little over three times as much.

Work an ordinary charge. A 60 kWh battery going from 20 per cent to 80 per cent takes on 36 kWh. At 7.4kW that is about 4.9 hours. At 2.3kW it is about 15.7 hours.

Now put that against the cheap rate window. Most off peak EV tariffs give somewhere around seven hours overnight. The 7.4kW charger finishes comfortably inside it. The granny lead is still going at breakfast, and everything after the window ends is billed at the day rate.

That gap is where the entire financial case lives. Not in the price of the electricity. In whether the charge finishes before the cheap rate stops.

2. What moves the install cost

Quotes for a 7.4kW unit installed land between 800 and 1,600 pounds, and the difference is rarely the charger itself. Units from the main manufacturers sit within a couple of hundred pounds of each other. The variables are all in the house.

Cable run. The dominant cost. A charger on the wall next to the consumer unit is a short job. One at the far end of a detached garage, across a driveway and under a path, means armoured cable, trenching and reinstatement, and can add 400 pounds or more on its own.

Consumer unit. The charger needs its own dedicated circuit with the correct protective device. If the existing board is full, or is an older type that cannot take the required protection, it needs a spare way fitting or replacing. A new consumer unit is 450 to 800 pounds and is the commonest reason a quote jumps.

Earthing arrangement. Many UK houses have a PME supply, and connecting an outdoor charger to it requires either an earth rod or a chargepoint with integrated open PEN protection. Most modern units include it. If yours does not, add an earth rod installation.

Load management. If the main fuse cannot support the charger at full output alongside a shower and an oven, the installer will fit a current transformer clamp so the charger throttles itself when the house is busy. That is a small cost and it prevents a much larger one.

Get three quotes and ask each one to state the cable run length and whether a consumer unit change is included. Those two lines explain nearly every difference you will see.

The fourth row is on the table to kill an upsell. A 22kW charger needs a three phase supply, which almost no UK house has, and even where it exists it charges no faster than 7.4kW on most cars because the car's onboard AC charger is the limit. Do not pay for it at home.

4. The rules, the certificate and the smart charging requirement

Installing a chargepoint means a new dedicated circuit, which is notifiable work under Approved Document P in England and Wales. In practice that means using an installer registered with a competent person scheme, who self certifies the work and issues a certificate. Keep it. A buyer's surveyor will ask, and so will your insurer after any electrical incident.

New home chargepoints sold in Great Britain must also have smart functionality, which means they are capable of scheduling, they default to charging outside peak hours unless you override them, and they have a randomised delay on start to stop thousands of them switching on at the same second. That last feature confuses people who expect the charge to begin the instant they plug in. It is doing what it is meant to.

Two practical consequences. First, if your car can already schedule its own charging reliably, some of the charger's smart function is duplicated, and that is worth knowing before paying extra for an app. Second, some off peak EV tariffs require a compatible smart charger to qualify, so check the tariff terms before you buy the unit rather than after.

On grants: the general grant for homeowners with off street parking ended some years ago. What remains is narrower and aimed mainly at flats, rented accommodation and households without a driveway. Check the current position on GOV.UK rather than assuming either way, because it has changed several times.

5. Worked example: the Osei driveway in Reading

Kwame Osei has a 2019 hatchback with a 64 kWh battery and a driveway in Reading. He does about 9,000 miles a year and has been charging on the granny lead since March.

Two quotes. 940 pounds for a unit on the front wall, a six metre run from the consumer unit, which has a spare way. And 1,480 pounds from a firm that wants to replace the consumer unit as part of the job.

He asks the second firm why. The answer is that the existing board is a 2004 unit without the protection type the charger needs. He asks the first firm the same question and gets a clear answer: they are fitting the protection inside the chargepoint instead, which is permitted and is how most modern units are supplied. Both answers are legitimate. The cheaper route is correct for his house.

On his mileage, about 2,570 kWh a year, that is roughly 71 charges of 36 kWh. Moving those from partly peak rate to entirely off peak saves about 274 pounds a year on the assumptions above. Payback on 940 pounds is 3.4 years.

What actually made his decision was neither number. On the granny lead he had twice come down in the morning to find the charge had not finished and he could not get to a meeting. The charger removed that, and the 274 pounds a year was the justification rather than the reason.

One thing he got right: he checked his off peak tariff terms first and confirmed it did not require a specific brand of charger. Two of the tariffs he had been considering did.

Frequently asked

Is a granny lead dangerous? Not inherently, but it draws near the limit of a domestic socket for many hours at a time, and old, loose or unswitched outdoor sockets do overheat. Never use an extension lead or a reel. If you are going to charge this way regularly, have an electrician fit a dedicated outdoor socket on its own circuit, which is the second row of the table.

Tethered or untethered? Tethered has the cable permanently attached, which is more convenient daily and slightly less flexible if you change cars. Untethered takes any cable and looks tidier. It is a preference, not a cost question, and the price difference is small.

Does it add value to the house? Estate agents say yes and there is no reliable published figure, so treat any number you are quoted with suspicion. What it certainly does is remove an objection for a buyer who has an EV, which matters more each year.

What if I move? The unit is fixed wiring and normally stays. It is possible to take it, but the making good and the reinstallation usually cost more than a new charger at the next house.

The honest verdict

If you have a driveway and you drive more than about 6,000 miles a year, fit the 7.4kW unit. On the assumptions set out above it saves around 300 pounds a year against a granny lead and pays back in roughly three and a half years, and the saving comes from finishing inside the cheap window rather than from any discount on the electricity.

If you drive under about 4,000 miles a year, a dedicated outdoor 16A socket at 250 to 400 pounds does most of the job for a quarter of the money. And nobody with a single phase domestic supply should be buying a 22kW charger.
