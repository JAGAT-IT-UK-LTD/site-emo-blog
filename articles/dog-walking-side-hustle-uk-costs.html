Walking Dogs Needs No Licence. Boarding One at Your Own House Costs £533.

The rule that decides whether your dog side hustle is free to start or a regulated, inspected business is not about how much you earn or how many dogs you take. It is about whose house the dog is in. Almost every guide to this gets it backwards.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • Dog walking is not a licensable activity in England. DEFRA's own statutory guidance excludes "businesses that look after dogs in their owner's homes, such as, dog sitters and dog walkers". No council licence, no inspection, no fee, no waiting.
  • The moment a dog spends the day or the night at your address, it becomes licensable. That is dog day care or home boarding under the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018.
  • A new home boarding or home day care licence costs £533 in Derby for 2026/27, and £378 to renew. Commercial premises day care is £689. Vet fees are charged on top. Every council sets its own scale.
  • Below the £1,000 trading allowance you need no licence at all, and the guidance says so in plain words. Above it you are not automatically in scope either.
  • Your revenue per walk is capped by your council, not by you. There is no national limit, but Public Spaces Protection Orders commonly cap one person at four to six dogs. At £14 a dog that is the difference between £56 and £84 for the same hour.
  • Rover takes 15% of a one-off booking but only 5% of a repeat weekly one. On £3,600 of turnover, that gap is £360 a year for doing the same work.

1. The line that decides everything: whose house is the dog in

Search for how to start a dog walking business and you will find two kinds of article. One tells you that you need a council licence. The other never mentions licensing at all. Both are wrong, because the answer depends entirely on a distinction neither of them makes. If the start-up cost is what puts you off, proofreading as a side hustle needs a laptop and nothing else.

The Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (England) Regulations 2018 licence five activities. Two of them matter here: providing or arranging boarding for cats or dogs, which includes home boarding, and providing day care for dogs. DEFRA's statutory guidance for local authorities, last updated on 1 June 2026, then lists what falls outside. It is worth reading the exact wording:

Activities that do not require a licence include "businesses that look after dogs in their owner's homes, such as, dog sitters and dog walkers". Sitting in the owner’s home is therefore a different business on paper, with its own insurance and its own rates, set out in what pet sitting pays and what cover you need.

The reason is that the dog stays in its own environment. You collect it, you walk it, you put it back. Nothing about your home is inspected, because your home is not part of the service. Turn that around and the picture changes completely. The guidance is equally explicit that "a business that looks after dogs in a business owner's own home" is licensed under home boarding.

So the fork is physical, not financial. Lead in hand on a public footpath, no licence. Dog asleep on your sofa for money, licence.

There is one more exemption that almost nobody quotes. The same guidance says that if someone has a trading income below the HMRC trading income allowance of £1,000, they do not require a licence for their activities. Board a neighbour's spaniel a handful of weekends a year for under £1,000 and you are outside the regime. Cross £1,000 and you are not automatically inside it either, because the council still has to decide you are trading. But £1,000 is where the question starts being asked.

2. What a licence actually costs, and what it buys

Fees are set by each council, so there is no national figure. Derby City Council's schedule for 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027 is a fair mid-range example:

Home boarding for dogs. New £533, renewal £378.

Day care for dogs, domestic premises. New £533, renewal £378.

Day care for dogs, commercial premises. New £689, renewal £533.

Boarding in kennels. New £689, renewal £430.

Vet fees, where the council requires an inspection by a vet, are charged in addition. A re-score inspection is a further £257.

Put £533 next to a typical UK home boarding rate of around £45 a night and the licence is covered by roughly twelve nights of boarding. On renewal at £378 it is about eight nights. Framed that way the fee is not the barrier people imagine.

The standards are the barrier. To be granted a licence at all your home has to meet the general conditions in Schedule 2. In practice that means at least one working smoke detector on every floor and at least one carbon monoxide detector, two secure physical barriers between a dog and any exit, a written emergency plan, written feeding, cleaning and disease control procedures, a registered vet, sight of every dog's vaccination record, and three years of records for every animal. Your house stops being just your house.

There is a reward for going further. Businesses meeting the higher standards get a four or five star rating, which earns a licence lasting two or three years instead of one, and a lower fee. If you are serious about boarding, aiming straight at four stars is cheaper over a five year run than scraping a one star licence and paying to renew every twelve months.

3. The council rule that caps what you earn per walk

Here is the constraint that decides the economics of walking, and it is missing from almost every guide. There is no national legal limit on how many dogs one person may walk. What exists instead is a patchwork of Public Spaces Protection Orders made by individual councils, and they commonly cap one person at four to six dogs in a public space.

Reigate and Banstead caps it at four. Sefton allows a professional walker six, but only if they are not walking with or alongside anyone else, dropping to four if they are. Some boroughs vary the limit park by park. DEFRA's day care guidance sets six as the standard for licensed facilities: "a dog walker may walk no more than 6 dogs at the same time".

Multiply that out. At a mid-range £14 per dog for a group walk, a four dog cap means £56 for an hour of work and a six dog cap means £84. Same hour, same effort, same fuel, 50% more money, decided by which side of a boundary line you live on. Check your council's PSPO before you price anything, because it sets your ceiling.

Rates themselves sit at roughly £10 to £15 for a 30 to 60 minute group walk and £15 to £25 for a solo walk, higher in London and the South East, lower in the North, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

4. The real start-up bill, including the two everyone forgets

Public liability insurance. Non negotiable. You are taking someone's dog off its lead in a public place. Advertised starting prices sit around £5.41 a month at Protectivity and from about £141 a year at Cliverton, with typical £5 million cover landing somewhere between £70 and £150 a year. Treat those as starting prices, not quotes.

Car insurance, the first thing people forget. A standard social, domestic and pleasure policy does not cover carrying other people's animals for payment. You need business use, and you should tell your insurer specifically what you are carrying. Budget a three figure uplift and get it confirmed in writing.

Key liability, the second thing people forget. Most walking clients give you a key. If one is lost and a property has to be re-secured, that is separate cover to check on your policy.

Kit. A crate or boot guard, a coupler lead, spare slip leads, a canine first aid kit, high visibility clothing and a serious supply of waste bags. A few hundred pounds in year one and a fraction of that afterwards.

What you do not need. No DBS check is legally required to walk dogs. No qualification is legally required either. Anyone telling you to buy an accreditation before you can start is selling you something.

Platform commission. On Rover in the UK, walkers and sitters take home 85% of a one-off booking and 95% of a recurring weekly one. Owners separately pay a 15% booking fee capped at £49. The 10 percentage point gap is the single most important number on the platform: your whole strategy should be converting one-off bookings into standing weekly slots, because that is where the fee halves and then halves again.

5. Worked example: Sadia in Watford, weekends only

Sadia works full time and wants to earn from dogs around her job. She has two realistic options and they are not equal.

Option A, walking. The problem is timing. Demand for dog walking is concentrated between roughly 11am and 2pm on weekdays, precisely when Sadia is at work. At weekends most owners walk their own dogs. Assume she fills one group walk each weekend day with three dogs at £14: 3 × £14 × 2 days = £84 a week. Over 46 weeks that is £3,864 gross. Take off £150 insurance and about £400 of fuel and kit and she nets roughly £3,300. No licence, no inspection.

Option B, home boarding. One dog, Friday evening to Sunday evening, at £45 a night. That is £90 a weekend. Across 40 weekends, £3,600 gross. Year one costs: £533 licence plus £150 insurance, so she nets about £2,917. Year two, on the £378 renewal, about £3,072.

The turn. On one dog, boarding is worse. But Sadia's licence states a maximum number of dogs, and the licence fee does not change if that number is two. Two dogs a weekend: £7,200 gross, less £533 and £150, is about £6,517 in year one. The fixed cost has not moved. That is the whole point.

Both figures are before tax. Both are trading income, so both use the £1,000 trading allowance and then Self Assessment above it. We cover that separately in our guide to side hustle tax rules.

The honest verdict

Dog walking is a poor side hustle and a decent full time job. The demand sits in the middle of the working day, which is the one block of time an employed person does not have. If you are keeping your job, weekend-only walking will pay for a food shop and not much more.

Home boarding is the opposite. It fits around employment almost perfectly, because the work happens in the evenings and at weekends in a house you already pay for. The licence is real, the inspection is real, and the fee at £378 to £533 is genuinely worth twelve nights of trade. The honest question is not whether you can afford the licence. It is whether you are willing to have your home inspected, keep written records for three years, and lose the ability to say no on a Friday night.

If the answer is no, walk dogs at weekends, keep it under the £1,000 trading allowance, and enjoy it for what it is. There is no shame in a side hustle that stays small, and no rule that says every hobby has to scale.

Frequently asked

Do I need a licence to walk dogs in England? No. DEFRA's statutory guidance expressly excludes dog walkers and dog sitters who look after dogs in the owner's home. You will still need public liability insurance and, if you transport dogs, business use on your car policy.

What if I only take one dog into my house occasionally? If your total trading income stays below the £1,000 trading allowance, the guidance says no licence is required. Above that, the council decides whether you are trading, using HMRC's nine badges of trade. Ring your licensing team before you advertise, not after.

Is it the same in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland? No. The 2018 Regulations apply to England only. Scotland has the Animal Welfare (Licensing of Activities Involving Animals) (Scotland) Regulations 2021, Wales has its own 2021 regulations and is developing a broader model covering pet services. Northern Ireland runs a separate regime. Check with your own council.

How many dogs can I legally walk at once? There is no national limit. Your council's Public Spaces Protection Order sets it, commonly at four to six, and it can differ between parks in the same borough. DEFRA's licensed day care standard is a maximum of six at one time.
