A settlement offer is three numbers pretending to be one

Ruhul Amin · Published 12 September 2026 · 5 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The figure at the bottom of a settlement agreement is usually made of parts that are taxed in completely different ways. Until you split it, you cannot tell whether the offer is generous or thin.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The first combined £30,000 of statutory redundancy pay, enhanced redundancy and severance is usually tax free, per GOV.UK.
  • Notice pay is never in that £30,000. Pay in lieu of notice, garden leave pay, unpaid wages, holiday pay and bonuses are all taxed as earnings.
  • Statutory redundancy is capped. From 6 April 2026 a week's pay is capped at £751 and the maximum statutory redundancy pay is £22,530.
  • You must take independent advice for the agreement to be legal, and the adviser cannot be acting for your employer.
  • Acas recommends at least 10 days to consider an offer, and more if you need a reasonable adjustment.

1. The three numbers inside one offer

Almost every settlement offer arrives as one figure. It is a bundle, and the parts behave differently.

  • Money you were already owed. Unpaid wages, accrued holiday, any bonus due. Not compensation, pay you had earned.
  • Notice. Worked, on garden leave, or paid in lieu. Either way it is money you would have had anyway.
  • Compensation. Statutory redundancy plus whatever the employer adds to close the matter. The only genuinely new money.

Split it before you judge it. A £24,000 offer that is £9,000 of notice and £3,000 of owed holiday is really a £12,000 offer.

The statutory floor is worth knowing. Per GOV.UK, statutory redundancy pay is half a week's pay per full year under 22, one week from 22 to 40, and one and a half weeks from 41, with service capped at 20 years. For redundancies on or after 6 April 2026 a week's pay is capped at £751 and the maximum is £22,530.

2. What is taxed, and what is not

This is where offers are quietly worth less than they look. Per GOV.UK you pay tax and National Insurance on unpaid wages, holiday pay, bonuses, payments for agreeing to a restrictive covenant, and any payment covering your notice period, whether paid in lieu or as part of a severance sum.

You do not usually pay tax on the first combined £30,000 of statutory redundancy pay, additional severance or enhanced redundancy payments, and non-cash benefits such as company property you keep. Above £30,000 you pay tax, and your employer pays employer Class 1A National Insurance on the excess.

Three things GOV.UK also lists as not taxed, which people forget to ask for: employer pension contributions made as part of the settlement, within the Annual Allowance; legal costs where the employer pays your solicitor directly; and a termination payment made because of an injury, illness or disability that stops you doing the job. That middle one is why the fee contribution should be a separate line paid to the solicitor, not added to your lump sum.

3. What makes it binding, and the 10 day rule

Acas describes a settlement agreement as in writing, legally binding, voluntary and reached through negotiation. It can cover a financial payment, a reference, an agreement not to bring tribunal claims, and a confidentiality clause, and can be made with a worker, a former worker or a job applicant.

For the agreement to be legal, Acas is explicit that you must get advice from a relevant independent adviser on how it affects your right to claim, and that the adviser must not be employed by or acting for your employer. A qualified lawyer counts, as does a certified and authorised trade union officer, official, employee or member.

On timing, the Acas Code of Practice recommends at least 10 days to consider a written offer and get advice, with reasonable adjustments where someone is disabled. Acas notes that if you are not given enough time, the settlement discussions can end up as evidence in a tribunal claim.

One point on eligibility. Unfair dismissal, constructive dismissal, breach of contract and redundancy pay claims require employee status. Discrimination claims are wider. What you are being asked to give up depends on what you could actually have claimed.

4. A worked example

Bethan, Swansea. Age 44, nine full years of service, gross pay £880 a week, three months of contractual notice. She is offered £27,500 to go, and told it is "well over the statutory".

Split it:

  • Statutory redundancy: three full years aged 41 or over at 1.5 weeks, six full years under 41 at 1 week, so 10.5 weeks. Her pay is above the cap, so it is 10.5 x £751 = £7,885.50. Tax free.
  • Notice: three months at £880 a week is about £11,440. Fully taxable as earnings, whether it is paid in lieu or worked.
  • Accrued holiday: say 8 days, about £1,408. Taxable.
  • What is left as real compensation: £27,500 less £7,885.50 less £11,440 less £1,408 = £6,766.50.

So the "well over statutory" offer adds about £6,767 above the legal minimum, and the tax free element, £14,652, sits well under £30,000. That room is the negotiation: every extra pound of compensation up to £30,000 reaches her untaxed, while an extra pound of notice pay does not.

Set out as four lines, the same £27,500 looks different:

Statutory redundancy, 10.5 weeks at the £751 cap£7,885.50Tax freeYes

The last cell is the number to negotiate on. Bethan has £15,348 of tax free room she is not using, and every pound of extra compensation inside it arrives whole. An extra pound of notice pay does not, because the notice element is taxed as earnings whatever the agreement calls it. So £3,000 more compensation is worth £3,000 to her, while £3,000 more notice is worth about £2,400 after basic rate income tax and less again after National Insurance.

When to ask for more, or walk

The same four lines show when an offer is close to worthless, which is the part most guides leave out.

Owen, Wrexham. Six full years of service, age 35, £560 a week, one month of contractual notice, and his job is going in a redundancy that was announced before any settlement was mentioned. He is offered £6,500 as a goodwill settlement. His pay is below the £751 cap, so his statutory redundancy uses his actual week's pay: six years all served between 22 and 40 is six weeks, so £3,360, tax free, and he gets it whether he signs or not. One month of notice is about 4.33 weeks, roughly £2,424, taxable, and he is owed that too. The agreement is therefore buying every claim he has for the remaining £715.

That is the test worth running on any offer. Subtract what you are getting anyway and look at what is left. If the remainder is four figures or less, the choice is to ask for more or to let the process run and keep your claims. Refusing is allowed: Acas describes a settlement agreement as voluntary, and refusing means the underlying redundancy, performance or dispute process simply continues on its own terms.

Two further cases where signing is the weaker move. If the money is mostly notice and owed holiday, the employer is handing you your own wages and calling it a settlement. And if the real complaint is discrimination, that claim does not depend on length of service, so what you are being asked to give up may be worth considerably more than the figure on the table.

The verdict

Never react to the headline figure. Write down four lines: owed pay, holiday, notice, compensation. Only the last is the employer buying something from you, and only that gets the £30,000 treatment.

Then use the 10 days. Take the independent advice, because without it the agreement is not binding anyway, and ask for the employer's fee contribution to be paid to the solicitor directly.

Frequently asked

Do I pay National Insurance on the amount above £30,000? GOV.UK says your employer pays employer Class 1A National Insurance on any amount over the combined £30,000, and the excess is subject to income tax.

Can I refuse a settlement agreement? Yes. Acas describes it as voluntary. Refusing means the underlying process, whether redundancy, performance or a dispute, continues on its own terms.

Who counts as an independent adviser? Acas lists a qualified lawyer, and a certified and authorised trade union officer, official, employee or member, among others. The adviser must not be employed by or acting on behalf of your employer.
