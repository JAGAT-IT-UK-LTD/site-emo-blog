The 8% Everyone Quotes for Workplace Pensions Is Not 8% of What You Earn

Ruhul Amin · Published 11 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The government confirmed that auto-enrolment thresholds stay frozen for 2026 to 2027. That is a routine-sounding decision with an unroutine effect, because contributions are calculated on a slice of your pay rather than all of it, and almost nobody is told which slice.

The short version

  • The thresholds are frozen for 2026 to 2027, confirmed by the Pensions Minister on 18 December 2025 and held at 2025 to 2026 levels.
  • Earnings trigger: GBP 10,000. Earn that or more from one job and your employer must enrol you automatically.
  • Qualifying earnings band: GBP 6,240 to GBP 50,270. This is the part almost nobody explains, and it is the part that decides what actually goes in.
  • Minimum total contribution is 8% of the band, not of your salary: 5% from you, 3% from your employer.
  • On a GBP 25,000 salary that 8% is really 6.0% of pay, because the first GBP 6,240 is excluded from the sum entirely.
  • The lower you earn, the wider the gap. On GBP 15,000 the headline 8% is worth about 4.7% of actual pay.
  • Freezing the trigger pulls more low earners in over time as wages rise, which is the one genuinely positive effect of standing still.
  • You can almost always pay in more than the minimum, and on most schemes the minimum was never meant to be a target.

1. What was actually decided, and when

On 18 December 2025 the Pensions Minister, Torsten Bell, confirmed in a written statement that the automatic enrolment thresholds for 2026 to 2027 would be held at their 2025 to 2026 levels. The stated reason was continued stability for employers and individuals while the Pensions Commission does its longer term work on whether people are saving enough at all.

Three numbers carry the whole system:

The earnings trigger, GBP 10,000. Earn at least that from a single job, and be aged between 22 and State Pension age, and your employer has to enrol you automatically.

The qualifying earnings band, GBP 6,240 at the bottom and GBP 50,270 at the top. This is not about who gets enrolled. It is about how much goes in once you are.

2. The band, and why 8% is not 8%

Every guide to workplace pensions repeats the same line: a minimum of 8% goes in, 5% from you and 3% from your employer. That is true, and it is also not what most people hear when they read it.

The 8% is not 8% of your salary. It is 8% of your qualifying earnings, which means only the part of your pay that falls between GBP 6,240 and GBP 50,270. The first GBP 6,240 you earn is invisible to the calculation. Nothing is taken from it and nothing is matched on it.

So on a salary of GBP 25,000, the sum is not 8% of GBP 25,000. It is 8% of GBP 25,000 minus GBP 6,240, which is GBP 18,760. That gives GBP 1,500.80 a year going into the pension. As a share of what the person is actually paid, GBP 1,500.80 on GBP 25,000 is 6.0%.

Two percentage points of a salary sounds small. Over a forty year career, with investment growth on top, it is not small at all.

Nobody is lying to you when they say 8%. They are just quoting the percentage of a number you have never been shown.

3. What the gap looks like at different salaries

The lower your pay, the bigger the distortion, because the excluded GBP 6,240 is a larger share of your earnings.

On GBP 15,000: qualifying earnings are GBP 8,760. The 8% minimum is GBP 700.80, which is 4.7% of actual pay. Your own 5% share is GBP 438, or 2.9% of what you earn.

On GBP 25,000: qualifying earnings are GBP 18,760. The 8% is GBP 1,500.80, or 6.0% of pay. Your 5% share is GBP 938.

On GBP 40,000: qualifying earnings are GBP 33,760. The 8% is GBP 2,700.80, or 6.8% of pay.

On GBP 50,270 or more: qualifying earnings top out at GBP 44,030. The 8% is GBP 3,522.40, and it stops growing however much more you earn, because the band is capped.

Note what that pattern means. The people for whom the workplace pension is furthest from the advertised 8% are the people on the lowest wages, who can least afford the shortfall and are least likely to have anything else.

One caveat worth stating plainly: many employers do not use the statutory band at all. Some calculate contributions on full basic pay, some on total earnings, and many pay more than the 3% minimum. If yours does, you are better off than this article describes, and your payslip is the place to find out.

4. What freezing the thresholds really does

A freeze is never neutral, because wages do not freeze with it.

Freezing the GBP 10,000 trigger widens coverage. As pay rises, more part time and lower paid workers cross a line that is standing still, and get enrolled who previously were not. That is a genuine gain, and it happens quietly without anyone legislating for it.

Freezing the GBP 6,240 floor slightly improves contributions. As your salary rises against a fixed floor, the excluded slice shrinks as a proportion, so the effective percentage of your pay creeps up. Small, but in the right direction.

Freezing the GBP 50,270 ceiling caps higher earners. Contributions on the statutory basis stop growing above that point regardless of salary.

So the freeze is mildly helpful at the bottom and mildly restrictive at the top. What it does not do is address the question the Pensions Commission was set up to look at, which is whether 8% of a band was ever going to be enough for anybody.

Worked example: Callum Beattie in Falkirk

Callum is 29, works in a warehouse and earns GBP 25,000. He is enrolled in his employer scheme on the statutory minimum basis and has never changed anything.

What he assumes: that 8% of GBP 25,000, so GBP 2,000 a year, is going into his pension.

What actually goes in: qualifying earnings of GBP 25,000 minus GBP 6,240 is GBP 18,760. Total 8% of that is GBP 1,500.80. His own 5% is GBP 938 and his employer 3% is GBP 562.80.

The gap: GBP 2,000 assumed against GBP 1,500.80 actual, so GBP 499.20 a year less than he thinks, before any growth. Over ten years that is nearly GBP 5,000 of contributions that were never going in, and the investment return he never earned on them.

What closing it would cost him. To get the total to GBP 2,000 he would need to add GBP 499.20 a year, which is GBP 41.60 a month gross. Because pension contributions attract tax relief, a basic rate taxpayer giving up GBP 41.60 of gross pay loses meaningfully less than that from take-home. Whether that is affordable is his call and nobody else can make it for him.

What costs him nothing at all: checking his payslip and his scheme documents to find out whether his employer uses the statutory band or full pay. If it uses full pay, the gap above does not exist and he is already where he thought he was. That is a five minute check with a materially different answer either way.

5. The honest verdict

Auto-enrolment is one of the most successful pieces of UK policy in decades. Millions of people are saving who were not, by default, without having to make a decision. None of what follows takes that away.

But the headline is misleading by omission, and the frozen thresholds are a reminder that the design has not really been revisited. If you take one thing from this: the minimum is a floor that was set by a compromise, not a recommendation of what you need. The 8% figure is 8% of a band, and on a below average salary that is closer to 6% or even under 5% of what you actually earn.

The useful action is not dramatic. Look at one payslip, find the pension line, and work out what percentage of your gross pay it actually represents. If the answer surprises you, you now know something true about your own money that the advertising never told you, and you can decide what to do about it. If money is tight and the answer is nothing right now, that is a legitimate answer too.

One thing to be careful of: opting out to free up cash means giving up the employer 3% as well as your own 5%. That is the part of the deal that has no equivalent anywhere else, and it is the last thing worth surrendering.

Frequently asked

Do these figures apply if I have two jobs? The GBP 10,000 trigger applies per job, not to your total income, so two jobs of GBP 8,000 each will not trigger automatic enrolment in either. You can usually ask to join a scheme voluntarily.

Can I pay in more than 5%? Almost always, yes. Ask your employer or scheme provider. Some employers will also increase their own contribution if you increase yours, which is the single best value change most people never ask about.

Does my employer have to use the band? No. The band is the statutory minimum basis. Employers may use a more generous basis, and many do. Your payslip and scheme documents will tell you.

Will the thresholds change next year? They are reviewed annually. This decision covers 2026 to 2027 only, and the Pensions Commission is separately looking at the bigger question of adequacy.
