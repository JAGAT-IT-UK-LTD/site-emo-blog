No Spend Challenge UK: Why the Month Fails and What Actually Saves You Money

1. What is a no spend challenge?

A no spend challenge is a fixed period, usually 30 days, when you buy nothing outside a list of essentials agreed in advance. In the UK it is often run as No Spend January after Christmas, or No Spend November before it.

Rent or mortgage, energy, council tax, food, medicine, travel to work and existing debt payments stay. Everything else stops.

That last sentence is where most people go wrong, because "everything else" is not obvious. Is a haircut essential? A birthday present for your mother? The train fare to a wedding you agreed to in April?

If you have not decided before day one, you will decide in the moment. And in the moment, you will say yes.

The challenge is popular because it is simple to explain and feels decisive. It is not a budgeting system. It is a short, blunt experiment that shows you where your money goes when you stop it moving.

2. How much can a no spend month save?

Start from real numbers rather than the ones in the videos. The ONS puts average UK household spending at £676.60 a week in the year to March 2025. That is about £2,930 a month, up 9% on the year before.

The biggest slice is housing, fuel and power at 18%, with transport next at 14%. Those, plus food and bills, are what a no spend month leaves alone.

So the honest ceiling is your discretionary spend. For many households that is a few hundred pounds a month. Nobody stops all of it. Realistically you keep somewhere between a third and a half.

Expect £200 to £400 for a typical household. Once. If a guide promises £1,000 from a single no spend month, it is talking to somebody on a much higher income, or it is not really talking about a month.

The month tells you where the money goes. It does not stop it going there. Only one of those jobs needs willpower.

3. The subscriptions nobody notices

Here is the part of your spending a no spend month never touches, because it happens without you doing anything.

The government says UK consumers spend an estimated £1.6 billion a year on subscriptions they do not want. Of around 155 million active subscriptions, nearly 10 million are believed to be unwanted.

More than 3.5 million people slide from a free or discounted trial into a full price contract each year, and 1.3 million are caught out by an unexpected auto renewal. The average unwanted subscription costs about £14 a month, nearly £170 a year.

Citizens Advice found that 26% of UK adults, over 13 million people, took out a subscription by accident in a year. Unused subscriptions cost consumers £688 million over that year.

Now compare the two. A no spend month is thirty days of saying no, for a one off £200 to £400. An evening with your bank statement is one sitting. Find three unwanted subscriptions at the average price and that is about £500 a year, every year, with no willpower needed afterwards.

The recurring payment audit, in order:

  • Open your banking app and list direct debits and recurring card payments. The card payments are the ones people miss, because they do not appear in the direct debit list.
  • Write down every one with the amount and the date. Do not judge yet, just list.
  • For each, ask one question: have I used this in the last 60 days? Not "would I", not "might I". Have I.
  • Cancel the contract with the company first, then cancel the payment. Cancelling only the direct debit leaves the contract running, and you can still owe the money.
  • Set a calendar reminder for any free trial you keep, two days before it converts.

Some apps bill through Apple or Google rather than your bank. Check the subscriptions page in your phone's settings too.

The rules are about to improve, but not for what you already have. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, new subscription rules are due on 1 January 2027, brought forward from spring 2027 by the Prime Minister in August 2026. They bring clearer reminders, easier exits and a new 14 day cooling off period after a trial or renewal.

The regulations were still in draft when we checked, and they cover contracts taken out from that date. Our guide to the subscription cancellation rules from 1 January 2027 explains what changes. Until then, the cancelling is on you.

4. The second leak is your fridge

WRAP, the UK waste charity, found that households wasted 6.0 million tonnes of food in 2022, in a report published in July 2025. Of that, 4.4 million tonnes, or 73%, was edible.

The edible food that was bought and then binned was worth £17 billion. WRAP puts the cost at £1,000 a year for an average household of four, which is roughly £250 per person.

Halve your waste and a family of four keeps about £500 a year. A single person keeps about £125.

This matters here because a no spend month often makes food waste worse, not better. People do a large panic shop on day one to avoid shopping later, then bin a third of it. If you do the month, plan five meals at a time, not thirty.

5. Why does a no spend challenge fail in week two?

Week one is easy because it is new. Week four is easy because the end is in sight. Week two is where it collapses, almost always for one of four reasons.

  1. The rules were never written down. An undefined rule is not a rule, it is a mood. The first awkward moment, a colleague's leaving lunch, ends it.
  2. There was no destination for the money. If the money you did not spend sits in your current account, your brain treats it as available. By week three it is gone and you cannot say where.
  3. It was all or nothing. One slip becomes proof that the whole thing failed. People abandon the remaining eighteen days, which would still have counted.
  4. It replaced spending with resentment. A month of denying yourself everything often produces a binge in week five that costs more than the month saved.

6. What are the rules of a no spend challenge?

There is no official rulebook. The rules are yours, and that is the point. These five keep the challenge alive past week two.

  1. Write the exception list before day one. On paper. Mine would be prescriptions, anything already promised to another person, and travel to work. Yours will differ. What matters is that it exists before you need it.
  2. Allow one spend day. Pick a date mid month, with a cash limit. A planned release valve prevents an unplanned blowout.
  3. Move the money the same day. When you decide not to buy the £34 thing, move £34 into a separate pot straight away. Money you can see is money you will spend.
  4. Give it a target with a name. "Saving money" fails. "£380 towards the car service in March" survives, because you can tell whether you are winning.
  5. Do the recurring payment audit in week one, not at the end. It is the single highest value hour of the month, and people leave it until they have lost interest.

A simple tracker helps. A calendar on the fridge with a cross for each no spend day, and a running total of the pot, is enough. You do not need an app or a printable pack.

For no spend activities, think free: walks, the library, free museums, a film night at home, a potluck dinner with friends. If coffee out is your habit, our sums on making coffee at home show what that one change is worth over a year.

7. A worked example with real numbers

Priya, 34, lives alone in Leeds. Take home pay £2,150 a month.

Fixed costs: rent £850, council tax £132, energy £96, water £34, phone £22, travel £78, insurance £41, minimum credit card payment £45, food £280. Total £1,578. That leaves £572 of discretionary spend.

What the no spend month did. Priya cut discretionary spending from £572 to £248 in January. Saved: £324, once.

What the subscription audit did. In week one she found three payments she had forgotten: a streaming service at £12.99, a fitness app at £9.99 and a magazine at £16.00. Total £38.98 a month, which is £467.76 a year. She had paid for the magazine for fourteen months without reading it, so that alone had already cost her £224.

What the food change did. Instead of one big shop she planned five meals at a time and shopped twice a week. Her bin waste roughly halved. On WRAP's figure of roughly £250 per person, that is about £125 a year.

The comparison that matters. The month she had to fight for produced £324 once. The two changes that needed no ongoing willpower produced £592.76 every year, nearly twice as much, and they keep paying in year two.

8. Who should skip this entirely

  • If you carry debt at 25% APR or more. Your money does more work paying that down than sitting in a no spend pot. Deal with the interest first.
  • If the problem is income, not spending. If your essentials already exceed what comes in, no amount of skipped coffee closes the gap. That needs a different answer, whether a benefits check, a pay rise conversation or extra earnings.
  • If you have done one before and binged afterwards. Do the subscription audit on its own and skip the month. You get most of the money and none of the rebound.
  • If you already track every pound. You already know where it goes. The diagnostic has nothing to tell you.

If the bills themselves are the problem, switching does more than refusing. Our guide to switching and saving on UK bills and our page on cutting energy bills cover the fixed costs a no spend month leaves alone.

The honest verdict

A no spend challenge is a good diagnostic and a poor cure. It shows you, quickly and uncomfortably, what you buy without deciding to. That is genuinely useful, and most people have never seen it laid out.

But the money that changes your year is not the money you refuse to spend in January. It is the subscriptions that leave your account whether you are paying attention or not, and the food you buy and then bin. Both are fixed with an evening and a habit, not with willpower.

If you only do one thing from this guide, open your banking app tonight and list your recurring payments. That single hour is worth more than the whole month.

Frequently asked questions

What can you spend money on during a no spend challenge?

Whatever you wrote down before day one. For most people that means rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, water, planned food shopping, medicine, travel to work and debt repayments. Many add one planned spend day. The list matters more than the exact definition, because an exception decided in the moment almost always becomes a yes.

How much money can you save with a no spend challenge?

For a typical UK household, roughly £200 to £400 in one month, depending on how much discretionary spending you had. That is a one off saving. Cancelling unwanted subscriptions, at about £14 a month each on government figures, keeps paying every year, so do both together for the best result.

Is a no spend January a good idea after Christmas?

It can be, because January bills land while pay feels stretched. Start by listing what Christmas already put on credit cards, and pay that first. Then run the month with written exceptions and one spend day. Do the subscription audit in the first week, before motivation fades.

Will a no spend month hurt my credit score?

No. Your spending patterns are not on your credit file. One caution: if you close a credit card during the month, your total available credit falls and your utilisation rises, which can nudge your score down. Keep paying at least the minimum on every card, on time, throughout the challenge.

Should I cancel the direct debit or contact the company?

Contact the company and cancel the contract first, then cancel the payment. If you only cancel the direct debit, the contract usually carries on and you can still owe the money, which may end up with a debt collector. Keep the cancellation email or screenshot as proof, with the date.
