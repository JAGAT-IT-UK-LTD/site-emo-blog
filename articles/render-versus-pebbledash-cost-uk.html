Rendering the House Will Not Make It Warmer, and the Quote Knows It

Ruhul Amin · Published 18 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Render quotes tend to arrive with a sentence about improving thermal performance. On a solid wall, a coat of render does almost nothing for heat loss, and the version that genuinely does is a different product at four times the price. Here are both finishes on cost per year, and the insulation arithmetic done properly.

The short version

  • Render typically costs £50 to £100 per square metre applied and lasts 20 to 30 years for sand and cement, longer for silicone and acrylic systems.
  • Pebbledash costs £40 to £80 per square metre and lasts 40 to 60 years, which is why so much of it is still on 1930s housing.
  • Neither finish is insulation. A solid 9-inch brick wall is around 2.1 W/m²K, and a render coat moves that by a rounding error.
  • External wall insulation with a render finish is around 0.30 W/m²K, which on a typical semi saves in the region of £350 a year of gas at 7.97p per kWh. It costs four times as much and is a different decision entirely.
  • Cracked render on a solid wall traps water behind it, so a failing render is worse than no render, and patching it rarely holds.

1. What the two finishes actually are

Render is a coating applied wet and finished smooth or textured. The traditional version is sand and cement, applied in two or three coats onto a scratch coat, and painted afterwards. Modern proprietary systems use silicone, acrylic or monocouche, which carry the colour through the material so there is nothing to repaint, and move more with the building.

Pebbledash, properly roughcast or dry dash, is a render base into which small stones are thrown while it is still green. The stones are the wearing surface. It is unfashionable, it is very difficult to repair invisibly, and it is extraordinarily durable, which is why so many houses built between the wars still carry the original.

Both are, in origin, weather protection rather than decoration. They were applied to walls built of materials that did not cope well with driving rain, which is why they cluster in exposed coastal and western districts and on cheaply built interwar housing.

2. The insulation claim, tested with numbers

Here is where the quotes overreach. Figures use the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: gas at 7.97p per kWh including VAT at 5% and electricity at 26.32p per kWh with no VAT, because the zero rate on electricity runs from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027.

A solid 9-inch brick wall has a U-value of roughly 2.1 W/m²K. Add 20mm of sand and cement render and the improvement is a few hundredths of a watt per square metre per kelvin. On a 90 m² wall area over a 2,000 degree day heating season, that is a saving measured in single-figure pounds a year.

So a straight render is not a thermal upgrade. It is weatherproofing and appearance, and both of those are legitimate reasons to buy it.

Now do the honest version. External wall insulation is 90 to 100mm of insulation board fixed to the wall and then rendered over. That takes the wall from about 2.1 to about 0.30 W/m²K, a difference of 1.8 W/m²K.

On 90 m² of wall: 1.8 multiplied by 90, multiplied by 2,000, multiplied by 24, divided by 1,000, is about 7,776 kWh a year of reduced heat demand. Allowing for boiler efficiency of around 88%, that is roughly 8,836 kWh of gas.

At 7.97p per kWh, that is about £704 a year in theory. Real installations routinely deliver considerably less than the calculation, because of thermal bridging at reveals, eaves and party walls, and because a warmer house tends to get used as a warmer house rather than a cheaper one. A defensible working figure is £350 to £500 a year.

At an installed cost of £14,000 to £22,000 for a semi, that is a payback of roughly 30 to 50 years unaided, and considerably better where a grant covers part of it. Which is the real conclusion: external wall insulation is a comfort and carbon decision with a long financial tail, and plain render is not an energy decision at all.

4. How each one fails, which decides the real cost

Render fails by cracking and debonding. Cement render is rigid and the wall behind it moves. Hairline cracks open, water gets behind the render, and because the render is less permeable than the wall the water cannot get back out. Tap along a rendered wall: a hollow note means the render has parted company with the wall behind, and that section is past repair.

The consequence matters. A wall with failing render is often damper than an unrendered one, because you have given the water a way in and no way out. This is the reason a patched render rarely holds for long, and why a whole-elevation strip and redo, despite the price, is frequently the correct answer.

Pebbledash fails by losing stones and by the base coat cracking. It fails slowly and locally rather than in sheets. The genuine problem with pebbledash is cosmetic: a repair never matches, because the aggregate, the base colour and forty years of weathering cannot be replicated. That is why people cover it rather than repair it.

One warning about covering. Applying a modern render over existing pebbledash requires the pebbledash to be firmly bonded to the wall, and a great deal of it is not. The correct preparation is to test it, hack off what is loose, and accept that on some walls the honest answer is full removal first, which changes the price materially.

Worked example: the Doran household, Port Talbot

1935 semi, original pebbledash, solid 9-inch walls, roughly 92 m² of wall area, west facing and fully exposed to weather off the sea.

Quote one, strip pebbledash and apply silicone render: £8,600 including scaffold. That is about £93 per m², and over a 30-year life, £3.11 per m² per year. Thermal saving, effectively nil.

Quote two, external wall insulation with a render finish: £18,400 including scaffold, reveals, and extending the roof verge. That is £200 per m², and over a 30-year life, £6.67 per m² per year.

The gas saving on quote two, taken at a realistic £420 a year rather than the theoretical £704, gives a payback of £18,400 divided by £420, or 44 years unaided.

The difference between the quotes is £9,800. The extra buys roughly £420 a year of gas and a house that is markedly more comfortable to sit in, which is a real benefit that does not appear in the payback.

They took quote two only because a grant scheme covered a substantial part of it, which changed the arithmetic entirely. Without the grant they said they would have taken quote one, and on these numbers that would have been reasonable.

5. Where the grant money actually sits

The external wall insulation arithmetic above changes completely with grant support, so it is worth knowing where to look rather than accepting a cold-call offer.

The Energy Company Obligation places a duty on larger energy suppliers to deliver efficiency measures to eligible households, and solid wall insulation is one of the measures it funds. Eligibility is generally tied to means-tested benefits or to a local authority referral scheme.

Local authority schemes vary enormously by area, and several run their own eligibility criteria that reach households not on benefits, often based on income, property EPC rating and council tax band. Your own council's website is the place to check, not a comparison site.

Two cautions that matter more here than almost anywhere else in home improvement. First, doorstep and cold-call offers of free insulation are a persistent problem in this sector, and a genuine scheme does not need you to decide on the day. Second, insist the installer is certified under the relevant quality scheme and that the work comes with the associated guarantee, because poorly detailed external wall insulation causes damp problems that are expensive and miserable to unpick.

Apply through the scheme or your council directly, and treat anyone who approaches you first with scepticism.

6. The honest verdict

Choose between render and pebbledash on appearance, exposure and cost per year, and do not let anyone price a thermal benefit into either. Pebbledash is the cheaper per year by a wide margin and the harder to love.

If warmth is what you actually want, the product is external wall insulation, not render, and it costs about three times as much per square metre. Check what grant support you qualify for before dismissing it, because that is usually the difference between a sensible decision and an unaffordable one.

And if existing render is hollow when tapped, budget for stripping it rather than patching it. Patching a debonded render is the most reliably wasted money in this whole comparison.

Frequently asked

Do I need planning permission to render my house? Usually not, because cladding or rendering the exterior is generally permitted development for a house, provided the materials are of similar appearance. Conservation areas, Article 4 directions, listed buildings and flats are the exceptions and need checking.

Can I render over pebbledash? Sometimes. The pebbledash must be sound and firmly bonded, and a competent contractor will test it rather than assume. Rendering over loose dash means both layers fail together.

Is lime render better on an old house? On a pre-1919 solid wall, generally yes, for the same reason lime mortar is better for pointing: it is permeable and lets the wall dry outwards. Cement render on a soft old wall traps moisture.

Does render stop damp? Sound render on an exposed solid wall reduces water penetration substantially. Failed render makes penetrating damp worse than no render at all, which is why condition matters more than presence.
