The British Standard Does Not Say You Need A Powerflush

The short version

  • BS 7593:2019 requires the system to be cleaned. It names powerflushing, mains pressure cleaning and gravity cleaning as recognised methods. A powerflush is one option, not the requirement.
  • The same standard is much firmer about the in-line filter, which it says should be permanently installed, and about dosing with inhibitor afterwards.
  • Powerflush route: roughly £575 plus £150 for the filter. Chemical clean route: roughly £150 plus the same £150 filter. A difference of about £425 on day one.
  • The only energy saving here you can honestly calculate is about £29.75 a year, and only if sludge was making you run the house a degree warmer. On a £300 clean and filter that is a 10 year payback, and the assumption behind it is doing a lot of work.
  • Get the filter regardless. It is a condition of most boiler manufacturers' warranties, it is the cheapest item on the quote, and it is the only part that keeps working after the engineer leaves.

1. What BS 7593:2019 actually says, in five parts

BS 7593:2019 is the British Standard code of practice for the preparation, commissioning and maintenance of domestic central heating and cooling water systems. It was published in May 2019 and it is the document your installer means when they say "it is a British Standard requirement". It structures the job in five stages: clean, guard, protect, maintain and test.

Clean. Before a system is dosed or a filter fitted, existing sludge has to be removed. The standard points installers at "a recognised cleaning method such as powerflushing, mains pressure or gravity cleaning". Three methods, listed together, no hierarchy between them. It also says the system should be cleaned before a new boiler goes on, so old sludge cannot be pushed into a new heat exchanger.

Guard. Once clean, an in-line filter "should be permanently installed". This is the part closest to a hard requirement, and the part discussed least.

Protect. Dose with the correct quantity of corrosion inhibitor, plus a biocide on underfloor circuits. Maintain. Service the filter as part of the annual boiler service, so captured debris is actually removed. Test. Check inhibitor levels over the system's life.

A necessary disclosure about the source. The clearest public summary of the 2019 revisions is published by Fernox, and we have used it above. Fernox manufactures heating chemicals and filters, and states on that page that it sat on the BEAMA water treatment committee that developed the revisions. We have quoted only its description of what the standard requires, not its product recommendations. BS 7593:2019 itself is a paid publication from BSI.

2. The three cleaning methods, and what each costs

Powerflushing connects a pump to the system and drives water and chemicals through it at high flow, reversing direction one radiator at a time. It is the most thorough method and the only one that will clear a genuinely blocked radiator.

Mains pressure flushing uses the incoming mains supply to push water through. Less aggressive, much quicker, no specialist pump. Gravity cleaning, often called a chemical clean, means dosing with a cleaner, running it hot for hours or days, then draining, refilling and dosing with inhibitor. That is what most competent installers do as part of a boiler swap.

Now the prices, with a warning attached. No national heating company publishes a fixed price for a powerflush, which is itself worth knowing, and the figures below come from UK trade pricing guides rather than a primary source. Treat them as the range to expect, not a tariff:

  • Powerflush, typical whole job: £400 to £700, with a commonly cited midpoint of about £575
  • Standard house up to about 10 radiators: £300 to £600, plus roughly £30 to £40 per extra radiator
  • Per radiator pricing, where it is used: quotes range enormously, from £20 to £40 in some guides to £80 to £120 in others. That spread is the clearest sign that this is a negotiable price rather than a fixed cost
  • Magnetic filter, supplied and fitted: £100 to £200

A chemical clean done as part of another job typically adds around £100 to £200 in chemicals and time, because the engineer is on site anyway.

Both of these routes are compliant with BS 7593:2019. That is the entire point of the table.

Look at the last two rows. A powerflush without a filter does not meet the standard. A filter without a clean does not meet the standard either. If someone quotes you £575 for a flush and treats the filter as an optional extra, they have the priorities the wrong way round, because the filter is the cheaper item and the one the standard is firmest about.

This is also why the filter matters commercially. Worcester Bosch, Vaillant, Ideal, Baxi and Viessmann all make filter and inhibitor presence a condition of their boiler warranties. If you are spending money to protect a new boiler, the £150 filter is protecting a £2,000 to £3,500 appliance and a warranty that can run to ten years. That is the strongest argument in the whole job, and it has nothing to do with energy.

4. The only saving here that can be calculated honestly

Every heating firm's website carries a claim that a powerflush cuts your heating bill by some percentage. We are not going to repeat one, because we cannot verify any of them from a source that does not sell flushes.

What can be calculated is the cost of the thing sludge makes you do: turn the thermostat up.

A typical three bedroom semi loses roughly 200 watts for every degree of difference between inside and outside. So raising the thermostat by one degree means the boiler has to supply an extra 200 watts continuously while the heating is on. Over a heating season of 210 days at 8 hours a day, that is 1,680 hours, and 0.2 kW × 1,680 hours is 336 kWh of extra heat.

A gas boiler at about 90 per cent efficiency burns 373 kWh of gas to deliver that. At the Ofgem price cap rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026 of 7.97p per kWh, that is £29.75 a year.

So the payback sum is this. £300 for a chemical clean and a fitted filter, divided by £29.75 a year, is a payback of 10.1 years, on the assumption that sludge was costing you exactly one degree. On the £725 powerflush route the same assumption gives a payback of 24.4 years.

Now the caveat, and it is a large one. That assumption is not measurable in your house. If two radiators are stone cold at the bottom, the effect might be more than a degree. If your system is fine and someone is selling a flush during an annual service, the effect is zero and the payback is infinite. There is no reliable way to know in advance, which is why this job is sold on fear rather than figures.

So: do not pay for a flush to save energy. Pay for it to fix a symptom you can point at. The same logic runs through boiler cover against paying for repairs yourself.

5. When a flush is genuinely the right answer

There are real cases. In roughly descending order of how clearly a full powerflush is justified:

  • Radiators cold at the bottom and hot at the top. That is sludge sitting in the bottom of the panel, and it is the textbook case. Bleeding will not fix it because it is not air.
  • Black water when you bleed or drain. Magnetite. The system is corroding internally and the debris is circulating.
  • One or two radiators that never heat while the rest are fine, and balancing has not fixed it. Something is physically blocking them.
  • The boiler is kettling, banging or cutting out on overheat. Debris or scale in the heat exchanger restricting flow. Fix this before it becomes a new boiler.
  • You are fitting a new boiler onto an old system. Here cleaning is not optional under the standard, though which method is appropriate depends on how bad the system is. A twenty year old system with black water deserves a proper flush. A well maintained ten year old system with an existing filter probably does not.

And the cases where the honest answer is no:

  • Every radiator heats evenly and the water runs clear. Nothing to clean.
  • It is being offered as an add-on during an annual service with no symptom named.
  • Air in the top of radiators only. That is bleeding and possibly a pressure or pump problem.
  • One cold radiator on a system that has never been balanced. Try balancing first, it is free and takes an afternoon. Our page on heating controls and TRVs covers how.

6. Worked example: Callum in Barnsley

Callum is having a combi boiler replaced on a 2003 system with nine radiators. The installer quotes £2,650 for the boiler, plus £595 for a powerflush, plus £165 for a magnetic filter. Total £3,410.

He drains a little water from a radiator valve into a jam jar. It is grey, not black, and no radiator in the house is cold at the bottom. So he asks the installer a specific question: will a chemical clean with a filter meet BS 7593:2019 on this system?

The installer, who is competent and not trying it on, says yes on a system in this condition, and requotes the cleaning at £170 for chemicals and the extra time. New total: £2,985.

Saved on day one: £425. That is the whole finding of this article in one number.

What Callum does not do is cut the filter. His new boiler carries a ten year warranty conditional on a filter and inhibitor being present, and £165 against a £2,650 appliance is the cheapest insurance in the quote. He also books the filter to be emptied at each annual service, because a filter full of magnetite that nobody has cleaned out is decoration.

7. The honest verdict

If you have no symptoms, do not buy a powerflush. There is no measurable saving to recover and the standard does not require that method.

If you are having a new boiler fitted, the system must be cleaned, but ask in writing whether a chemical clean is appropriate for your system's condition. On a reasonable system it usually is, and the difference is around £425.

Buy the filter every time. £100 to £200 fitted, close to mandatory under the standard, a condition of most boiler warranties, and the only part of this job that goes on working. Then have it emptied at every annual service.

If your radiators are cold at the bottom or the water runs black, pay for the full powerflush and stop reading cost guides. That is the case the machine exists for, and it is cheaper than the boiler it is protecting.

If you are buying

The work here is a trade job, not a product. What is worth owning is the means to check the claim before you accept it: a jar to catch a little water from a radiator valve and see its colour, and an infrared thermometer to check top and bottom of each radiator panel. Two minutes per radiator will tell you more than any sales visit.

Frequently asked

Can I refuse a powerflush and still get my boiler warranty? Generally yes, provided the system is cleaned by an acceptable method and a filter and inhibitor are fitted, because that is what the manufacturers' conditions actually ask for. Read the specific warranty terms for your boiler model before you decide, and make sure whatever is done is written on the Benchmark commissioning checklist, because that document is what you will be asked for if you ever claim.

Does a magnetic filter need emptying? Yes, and this is where most of the value is lost. BS 7593:2019 says filter servicing should be part of the re-commissioning process and ideally the annual boiler service. A filter that has never been opened has captured everything it is going to capture and is now just a fitting on a pipe.

Is an electronic or magnetic scale reducer worth adding in a hard water area? Different problem, different product. A filter catches magnetite from corrosion in the heating circuit. Limescale comes from hard mains water on the hot water side. If you are in a hard water area the question you are really asking is about water treatment for the whole house, and there the arithmetic is a good deal less flattering than the salespeople suggest.
