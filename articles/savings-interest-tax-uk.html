Your Savings Allowance Has Not Moved Since 2016. Your Interest Rate Has.

Ruhul Amin · Published 3 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The Personal Savings Allowance was set at £1,000 in April 2016, when the Bank of England base rate was 0.5%. It is still £1,000. Base rate is not still 0.5%. That gap is why people who have never thought about savings tax in their lives are now quietly paying it through their tax code.

The short version

  • Three allowances stack, in order: your Personal Allowance, then the starting rate for savings of up to £5,000, then the Personal Savings Allowance.
  • The Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 for basic rate taxpayers, £500 for higher rate, and £0 for additional rate. Unchanged since it was introduced on 6 April 2016.
  • The starting rate for savings disappears entirely if your other income is £17,570 or more, and shrinks by £1 for every £1 of other income above your Personal Allowance.
  • If you are employed or on a pension, HMRC changes your tax code and collects it automatically, estimating this year's interest from what you earned last year.
  • The deadline almost nobody knows: if you went over your allowance and have not received a tax calculation letter by 31 March of the following tax year, you must contact HMRC yourself or risk a penalty.
  • Joint accounts split interest equally for tax, which is the single most useful fact in this article for couples.

1. The three allowances, in the order HMRC applies them

Most explanations of savings tax give you one number, the £1,000 Personal Savings Allowance, and stop. There are actually three allowances and they apply in a specific order, which is why two people with identical savings can get completely different answers.

First, your Personal Allowance. Currently £12,570. If your wages, pension and other income do not use it all up, the unused part can shelter savings interest. Somebody with no other income at all can receive £12,570 of interest before anything else is even considered.

Second, the starting rate for savings. Up to £5,000 of interest, taxed at 0%. This is the one almost nobody has heard of, and it has a taper that makes it useless to most working people. Every £1 of other income above your Personal Allowance reduces it by £1. So at £13,570 of other income you have £4,000 left of it, and once your other income reaches £17,570 it is gone entirely. Whether you cross it at all depends on where rates go next, which is what the latest MPC signals mean for savers and borrowers turns on.

Third, the Personal Savings Allowance. This is the well-known one, and it depends on your Income Tax band, worked out after adding your interest to your other income:

Income Tax bandPersonal Savings Allowance
Basic rate£1,000
Higher rate£500
Additional rate£0

Two details worth holding on to. The allowance covers more than bank accounts: credit union accounts, government and company bonds, peer-to-peer lending, some life insurance contracts and interest paid on PPI compensation all count. And money in an ISA does not count towards it at all, which is the entire point of an ISA. Regular savers are the clearest case of a headline rate that does not deliver what it looks like, because the balance builds month by month rather than sitting there all year: what 8% on a regular saver actually pays.

2. What £1,000 actually protects now

The Personal Savings Allowance arrived on 6 April 2016. At that point the Bank of England base rate had been 0.5% for seven years, and the allowance was designed for a world in which savings paid almost nothing. Where that leaves the money now is covered in what your savings should do at a 3.75 percent Bank Rate.

Do the arithmetic on what the allowance shelters at different rates. The break-even balance is simply the allowance divided by the interest rate:

Interest rateBalance a basic rate taxpayer can hold tax freeSame for a higher rate taxpayer
0.5%£200,000£100,000
3%£33,333£16,667
4%£25,000£12,500
5%£20,000£10,000

These are our own calculations, and the rates in the left column are illustrative rather than a claim about what any account currently pays. The point is the shape of the table, not any one row.

At 0.5%, the rate that applied when the allowance was designed, £1,000 sheltered £200,000 of savings. At 4% it shelters £25,000. That is a reduction of about eight times in the balance protected, and it happened without a single word of the legislation changing.

This is why savings tax has stopped being a wealthy person's problem. A basic rate taxpayer with a house deposit sitting in an easy-access account is now a plausible candidate to pay tax on savings interest, and most of them have no idea.

3. How HMRC takes it without you filing anything

If you are employed or receiving a pension, you do not declare savings interest and you do not get a bill in the usual sense. Banks and building societies report the interest they paid you to HMRC after the end of the tax year. HMRC then changes your tax code so the tax comes out of your salary or pension automatically. Where HMRC decides tax is owed on interest it usually arrives as a Simple Assessment letter rather than a tax return, and there is a 60 day window to challenge the figure on it.

The part that confuses people is the timing. HMRC estimates how much interest you will get in the current year by looking at what you got in the previous year. So the code is always working from history.

That has two practical consequences. If your savings balance or the interest rate has fallen, your code may be collecting tax on interest you are not going to receive, and you can ask HMRC to update the estimate. If your balance has grown, the code is under-collecting and you will owe the difference later.

If you complete a Self Assessment return, you report the interest there instead. And note the separate trigger: you need to register for Self Assessment if your income from savings and investments is over £10,000, whether or not any tax is actually due.

4. Worked example: a £52 tax bill and the fix

Worked example: Nadia in Stevenage

Nadia earns £32,000 and has £30,000 saved in an easy-access account paying 4.2%, which is a house deposit she has been building for four years. She has never filed a tax return and has never thought about savings tax.

Her interest for the year is £1,260. That is £30,000 multiplied by 4.2%.

Starting rate for savings: nil. Her other income of £32,000 is well above £17,570, so it does not apply.

Personal Savings Allowance: £1,000. She is a basic rate taxpayer, including once the interest is added.

Taxable interest: £260. That is £1,260 minus £1,000. At the basic rate of 20%, the tax is £52, which HMRC will collect through her tax code without her ever seeing a bill.

Now the fix. Nadia is married to Tom, who works part time and earns £14,000. If the account were held jointly, the interest would be split equally for tax purposes: £630 each. Nadia's £630 sits inside her £1,000 allowance. Tom's £630 sits inside his, and he also has some starting rate for savings available.

Tax due: nil. The whole £52 disappears, on the same money, at the same rate, in the same account.

£52 is not life-changing, and we are not going to pretend it is. But it is the entire tax bill, it costs nothing to fix, and the same logic scales: on a larger balance or at a higher rate the number grows quickly, and the fix stays identical.

One caveat before anybody rushes to the bank. Moving money into a joint account means giving somebody else legal access to it, and HMRC's default 50-50 split can be varied if the true ownership is different, but that has to be told to HMRC. This is a tax point, not a relationship one, and it only makes sense if the joint ownership is genuine.

5. The 31 March deadline nobody mentions

Here is the sentence buried in HMRC's guidance that almost no consumer article repeats.

HMRC sends a tax calculation letter if you have overpaid or underpaid, and those letters go out between June and March of the following tax year. Then this:

If you go over your savings allowance and do not receive a letter by 31 March of the following tax year, you must contact HMRC. You should do this as soon as possible to avoid a penalty.

The obligation is on you. If HMRC's system does not catch your interest, perhaps because a smaller provider reported late or your details did not match, the responsibility to flag it does not disappear. And the consequence of not doing so is described as a penalty, not just the tax.

Working the other way, if you paid tax on interest that was inside your allowance, you can reclaim it, and you have four years from the end of the relevant tax year to do it. If you do not file a return, that is done on form R40.

6. The honest verdict

Nothing here is a loophole and there is nothing to buy. Savings tax has become a mainstream issue purely because a fixed allowance met a rising rate, and the number of people affected grows every time either side of that moves.

Three things are worth doing. Work out your actual interest for the year, which is one multiplication, and see whether it clears £1,000, or £500 if you are a higher rate taxpayer. If you are close and you have a partner in a lower band, look at whether joint ownership is genuinely appropriate, because the automatic 50-50 split is the simplest legitimate fix in the system. And diarise 31 March, because if you went over and no letter arrives, the obligation to speak up is yours. The same return is where relief gets claimed as well as tax paid, including higher rate relief on Gift Aid donations.

If your savings sit inside an ISA, none of this applies to you, and that is worth remembering before the next time somebody tells you cash ISA rates are slightly worse.

Frequently asked

Does interest from an ISA count towards the allowance? No. Interest in an ISA is outside Income Tax entirely and does not use up any part of your Personal Savings Allowance. Some National Savings and Investments products are also outside it. A Junior ISA works the same way, with a £9,000 a year allowance that sits outside Income Tax entirely, though whether it is the right home for money you are saving for a child is a separate question.

What if I have no other income at all? Then all three allowances are available in full. Your Personal Allowance of GBP 12,570, plus the full GBP 5,000 starting rate for savings, plus the GBP 1,000 Personal Savings Allowance, which is why a person with no employment income can receive a substantial amount of interest tax free. On a low income the best return is not interest at all: the 50 per cent government bonus paid on a Help to Save account sits outside all three allowances because it is a bonus rather than taxable interest.

Is the tax taken at source by my bank? No. Banks and building societies have paid interest gross, without deducting tax, since April 2016. They report it to HMRC instead, which is why the collection happens through your tax code rather than at the point the interest is paid.

What about interest from peer-to-peer lending? It counts. HMRC lists peer-to-peer lending among the sources covered by the allowance, alongside bank and building society accounts, credit union accounts, unit trusts, government and company bonds and some life insurance contracts.
