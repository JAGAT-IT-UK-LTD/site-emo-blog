Heating oil nearly doubled in a single month this year, and the usual advice did not protect anyone

For years the standard advice to anyone with an oil tank was simple: fill it in summer when demand is low. In 2026 the price went up 95 per cent in one month, in March, and it did it for reasons that had nothing to do with the season. Here is what the official figures actually show, and what to do with a tank now.

1. What actually happened to the price in 2026

The House of Commons Library briefing Households off the gas-grid and prices for alternative fuels, published on 6 August 2026 and read again on 9 November 2026, sets it out plainly. Average UK heating oil prices jumped following strikes by Israel and the United States against Iran on 28 February 2026. Prices went from 53 pence per litre in February 2026 to £1.04 per litre in March 2026, a 95 per cent increase in a single month, though the briefing adds that they have since decreased slightly.

The Library's companion briefing Domestic energy prices: In short, published on 21 September 2026, says the March 2026 spike was sharper than those in 2022, when prices jumped after the full scale invasion of Ukraine. Prices almost doubled in one month.

The same briefing says prices have stayed volatile since. Typical mid month prices were 90 pence per litre in August 2026, down from the spring peak but still well above August 2025.

For a live figure, BoilerJuice publishes a daily UK average built from the cheapest 1,000 litre kerosene price in each postcode district. On 9 November 2026 it was 108.73p a litre including VAT. That is a different measure from the official monthly series, so do not read the gap from 90p as a rise of exactly that size.

The March figures are the ones that matter for planning. For a household that heats with oil, that is not a line on a chart, it is the difference between a £530 delivery and a £1,040 delivery for the same 1,000 litres.

2. Why filling in summer did not help

The summer filling rule exists because heating oil demand is seasonal and, in a normal year, so is the price. Order in July when nobody else is ordering and you pay less than you would in December. It is decent advice in a quiet year and it is worth nothing in a year like this one.

The reason is in the Commons Library's own framing. Heating oil prices are closely linked to crude oil prices, so they are highly volatile and frequently linked to major global events. The briefing lists conflicts, the 2008 financial crisis and the pandemic as the drivers. Seasonality is a small effect sitting on top of a much larger one, and when the large one moves, the small one is invisible.

The Library also makes a point that matters more than the price itself: prices for alternative fuels are not regulated in the same way as gas and electricity prices, so they can vary day to day and between different local areas to a much greater extent. There is no cap. Nobody is standing between the world crude price and your tank.

That unregulated local variation is the part you can actually act on, and it is the reason the single most valuable habit for an oil household is not seasonal at all. It is ringing three suppliers on the same morning rather than one. Two quotes taken a week apart in a volatile month are not comparable and tell you nothing about who is cheap. Three taken within an hour of each other are a real comparison, and in a market with no cap and genuine local spread, that hour is usually worth more than the month you picked.

There is a harder point in the same briefing that is worth stating rather than skipping past. Households using alternative fuels are more likely than other households to be classified as being in fuel poverty, and more likely to experience deeper or more extreme levels of it, largely because properties off the gas grid tend to be less energy efficient. So the households most exposed to a 95 per cent move in one month are on average the ones least able to absorb it, and that is not a coincidence of the market, it is a feature of which houses ended up off the grid.

3. The comparison we are refusing to print

Many pages on this subject will give you a table comparing the running cost of an oil boiler with the running cost of a heat pump, in pence per kWh of heat delivered. We are not going to, and the reason is the whole reason this site exists.

To produce that table you need two numbers we did not verify from a primary source during this run. The first is the calorific value of kerosene in kWh per litre, which is what turns a price per litre into a price per unit of heat. The second is the seasonal performance factor of a heat pump in a real British house, which varies enormously with how the system was designed and installed. Guess either one and the answer moves by 30 per cent or more, in whichever direction the writer wanted it to move.

An omitted number costs you nothing. A wrong one on a page about a decision worth thousands of pounds costs you a great deal. So the honest position is this: we can tell you what oil costs per litre, because that is published and dated, and we cannot tell you what it costs per unit of heat, because we have not checked the conversion. If you want that comparison, get your installer to put their assumed seasonal performance factor in writing on the quote, then run it against your own delivery notes.

What we can give you is the capital side, because that is published by government. Under the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, read again on GOV.UK on 9 November 2026, you can get £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump, and until March 2027 an additional £1,500 if both your property is heated by oil or LPG and you do not have a mains gas connection. That is £9,000, one grant per property, and the uplift has a deadline attached to it.

4. A worked example

Rhodri, Llandeilo. Detached stone house, no mains gas in the lane, 2,500 litre tank, buys roughly 1,700 litres a year in two deliveries.

In February 2026 he was paying around half what he paid a month later. On the Commons Library figures, the same 1,700 litres cost £901 at February 2026 prices and £1,768 at March 2026 prices, a gap of £867 for exactly the same warmth. At the August 2026 price of 90p, the same oil would cost £1,530.

He gets a heat pump quote. Say it leaves £4,000 to find after the £7,500 grant and the £1,500 off grid uplift. Against his oil spend at March 2026 prices, £4,000 divided by £1,768 is 2.3 years of his current oil bill. That is a sizing exercise, not a payback, and we are labelling it as one: it tells him how big the remaining cost is in units he understands, and it does not claim the heat pump saves him £1,768 a year, because we have not verified what it would cost to run.

What the number does do is frame the decision properly. His real exposure is not the price of oil today, it is that he is holding an unhedged position in a commodity that moved 95 per cent in one month on news from the other side of the world, with no price cap and no regulator. The £1,500 uplift closing in March 2027 is the only part of this with a date on it.

The verdict

Keep filling in summer, because in an ordinary year it still helps a little, but stop treating it as protection, because in 2026 it was worth nothing against a move of this size.

If you heat with oil and have no mains gas connection, the one thing on this page with a deadline is the extra £1,500 on the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, which runs until March 2027. Get a quote while the uplift exists, and make the installer write their assumed performance figure on it so you can check the running cost yourself rather than taking anyone's table on trust.

Frequently asked

How much heating oil does a typical house use? It varies far more than mains gas, because off grid properties are on average larger and less energy efficient than the national average, a point the Commons Library makes directly. The 1,700 litres used above is our own stated assumption for illustration. Use your own delivery notes instead.

Is heating oil expensive compared with other countries? No. The Commons Library states that heating oil is cheaper in the UK than in every EU country apart from Malta. In mid August 2026 the UK figure was 90.0p a litre against an EU average of around 124p, mainly because taxes on heating oil, especially VAT, are higher across the EU.

How many households does this affect? An estimated 4.8 million households across Great Britain were not connected to the gas grid in 2024, which is 16.0 per cent of domestic properties. In Northern Ireland the 2021 census found around half of households, 49.5 per cent, used oil as their only central heating.

Is there any price protection for oil customers? Not of the kind gas and electricity customers have. The Ofgem price cap does not apply to heating oil, and the Commons Library notes prices can vary day to day and between local areas to a much greater extent as a result.
