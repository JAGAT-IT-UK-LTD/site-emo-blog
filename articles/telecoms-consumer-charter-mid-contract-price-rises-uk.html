1. Are mid contract price rises still allowed? The Ofcom rule and the charter

Yes. Mid contract price rises are still allowed in the UK. What changed is how they must be written, and who has promised what.

It helps to separate the regulation from the promise. News coverage often runs them together.

The regulation is Ofcom's. Since 17 January 2025, providers cannot write inflation-linked or percentage rises into new contracts. The old "CPI plus 3.9%" formula is gone for new customers.

Any scheduled rise must now be set out in pounds and pence. It must be shown prominently and clearly at the point of sale, with the date it applies. The rule covers phone, broadband and pay TV.

That rule is part of Ofcom's regulation, so Ofcom can investigate and act on breaches. Contracts signed before 17 January 2025 could keep their inflation-linked terms until they ended. That is why so many people still saw a CPI-plus rise in April 2025 and April 2026.

The promise is the government's. On 11 February 2026 the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology published the Telecoms Consumer Charter. Technology Secretary Liz Kendall and Chancellor Rachel Reeves hosted the providers that day.

The charter's own text calls its contents "additional voluntary commitments". It says it builds on Ofcom's rules, including the January 2025 pounds-and-pence approach, end-of-contract notifications and One Touch Switching. It adds commitments. It does not add powers.

2. What does the Telecoms Consumer Charter promise?

The charter has three headings. The ones that touch your bill sit under transparency.

The price you sign up to is the price you pay. Where a contract includes a mid contract price increase, signatories commit that the core subscription price you sign up to is the price you will pay.

Any exception is "limited strictly to unforeseeable and externally driven events that materially affect the cost of providing services". Read carefully, this is about the rise being the one disclosed at signing. It is not a promise of no rise.

April 2026 was the last inflation-linked rise. For any customer still on legacy inflation-linked terms for consumer services, April 2026 was the final increase expressed that way for the core subscription.

Those contracts then move to the pounds-and-pence system. The charter says some providers would tell customers the pound figure before that date.

Clearer information. Signatories commit to showing your full package in one place: headline price, contract length, key terms and add-ons. They also commit to explaining agreed changes, such as mid contract rises, in a simple format throughout the contract.

Under the third heading, help for people struggling to pay, signatories commit to signposting social tariffs. That means end-of-contract notices, websites, apps and customer service scripts. They also commit to options such as a move to a cheaper package without charge or penalty, or a manageable payment plan.

The signatories at the last update on 19 June 2026: BT Group, VMO2, VodafoneThree, Sky, TalkTalk, KCOM, Community Fibre, WightFibre, Hyperoptic and Olilo. The five large providers signed on 11 February. Community Fibre joined on 13 March, WightFibre on 8 April and Olilo on 19 June. The charter says providers can sign at any time.

3. What does the charter not stop?

It is not enforceable. The charter is voluntary. A signatory that breaks it faces the government's displeasure, not a fine. The enforceable protections remain Ofcom's rules.

It does not ban or cap mid contract rises. A new contract can still say "£3 a month more from April 2027 and April 2028". The charter says that figure must be the one you were shown. Ofcom's rule says it must be in pounds and pence. Neither says it must be zero, or small.

It does not end variable pricing. Some contracts do not fix a rise at all. They say the price may change, and give you notice and a right to leave instead. Sky works this way and is a signatory.

Under Ofcom's general rules, a change that was not set out upfront needs at least a month's notice. You can then leave without a penalty, normally within 30 days of the notice. Many people only notice when the higher bill lands, so read every price letter.

It covers the core subscription only. Add-ons, out-of-bundle charges and equipment are outside the commitment.

It binds signatories only. Providers that have not signed are bound by Ofcom's rules and nothing more. Check the list before assuming a promise applies to your provider.

The exception is open-ended. "Unforeseeable and externally driven events" is not defined. The charter gives no examples and no test.

MoneySavingExpert's Martin Lewis said the charter still leaves "two glaring gaps" in Ofcom's rules. The first is that transparency does not stop or limit price rises. The second is that variable pricing remains a loophole.

4. What has happened since the charter?

The April 2026 round of legacy CPI-plus rises was meant to be the last. From now on, signatories' rises on those older contracts should be stated in pounds and pence.

The bigger question is whether pound rises are better for customers at all. On 25 November 2025 the Chancellor and the Technology Secretary wrote to Ofcom about it.

They asked for an interim review of the impact of the January 2025 changes by spring 2026, and a full review in 2027. They also asked Ofcom to look at whether the 30 day notice period lets people leave when a rise takes effect.

Consumer groups have argued that fixed pound rises can cost more than the old formula when inflation is low. Treat that as a campaign view, not an Ofcom finding. The full review is the one to watch in 2027.

One change has already helped. The Communications Ombudsman cut its waiting time from eight weeks to six. It applies to complaints first raised with the provider on or after 8 April 2026.

5. How much does a mid contract rise really cost? A worked example in Croydon

Femi in Croydon signs a 24 month full-fibre broadband contract on 1 September 2026 at £29.99 a month. The contract summary, as Ofcom requires, states a £3 a month rise on 1 April 2027 and a further £3 on 1 April 2028.

Our arithmetic runs in three steps. Seven months at £29.99, September to March, is £209.93.

Twelve months at £32.99, April 2027 to March 2028, is £395.88. Five months at £35.99, April to August 2028, is £179.95.

Total: £785.76 over 24 months, an effective £32.74 a month. The headline £29.99 is what the comparison table shows. The £32.74 is what the contract costs.

What the charter gives him: certainty that £3 is £3, not £3 plus whatever inflation turns out to be, unless his provider invokes an unforeseeable external event.

What Ofcom's rule gives him: the right to have seen that £3 in pounds and pence before he signed. What neither gives him: a contract with no rise.

A rival's £31.49 with no scheduled rises would cost £755.76 over the same 24 months. That is £30 less, despite the higher headline.

Had Femi signed in 2024 on a "CPI plus 3.9%" contract, April 2026 would have been his last rise on that formula. Under the charter, a signatory should now state any later rise in pounds and pence.

6. What can you do now, before and during a contract?

Signing a new deal: find the mid contract price rise line in the contract summary. It must be there in pounds and pence, with dates. Multiply out the whole term as above and compare totals, not headlines.

Our guide to the true cost of broadband price rises over a contract walks through more examples.

If the rise is not the one you were shown: when your first bill after April arrives, check it against the contract summary. If a signatory charges more, quote the charter and the contract summary together, in writing.

Already on a legacy inflation-linked deal: April 2026 should have been the last CPI-plus rise from a signatory. If a later bill shows an inflation-linked increase from one of the ten, complain in writing.

Taking it further: if the complaint is unresolved after six weeks, or you get a deadlock letter, take it to the Communications Ombudsman or CISAS. Use whichever scheme your provider belongs to. Both are free. Complaints first raised before 8 April 2026 still needed the full eight weeks.

Given notice of a variable price change: if your contract did not set the rise out upfront, you can usually leave without a penalty. Act within the window in the notice, normally 30 days.

Struggling to pay: the charter commits signatories to point eligible customers to social tariffs and to allow a move to a cheaper package without penalty. Ask for both by name.

Ofcom lists social tariffs from £10 to £24 a month for people on Universal Credit and some other benefits. See our guide to who qualifies for broadband social tariffs in 2026.

Out of contract: none of this matters as much as the end-of-contract notification. Out-of-contract prices are where the real overpayment sits.

7. The honest verdict

The enforceable protection is Ofcom's: rises in pounds and pence, shown before you sign, since January 2025. Use it every time you compare a deal, by pricing the whole term.

The charter is a promise from ten providers that the pound figure they showed you is the one you pay, and that April 2026 ended CPI-plus rises on old contracts. It is worth quoting. It is not worth relying on as if it were law.

Neither stops a provider writing a fixed rise into a new contract, or using variable pricing. The only defence is arithmetic before you sign, a provider that does not do it, and leaving when a notice gives you the right.

Frequently asked questions about mid contract price rises

Are mid contract price rises banned now?

No. Since 17 January 2025, Ofcom has banned inflation-linked and percentage rises in new phone, broadband and pay TV contracts. Any rise must be shown in pounds and pence before you sign. Fixed pound rises are still allowed, and the Telecoms Consumer Charter does not change that. A full Ofcom review is due in 2027.

Which providers signed the Telecoms Consumer Charter?

At the last GOV.UK update on 19 June 2026, the signatories were BT Group, VMO2, VodafoneThree, Sky, TalkTalk, KCOM, Community Fibre, WightFibre, Hyperoptic and Olilo. The five largest signed on 11 February 2026 and the others joined later. Providers can sign at any time, so check the list before you rely on it.

My contract rose with CPI in April 2026. Will it rise again?

It can, but not on the inflation formula if your provider signed the charter. April 2026 was meant to be the last inflation-linked rise on legacy terms. Any later rise should be a pound figure told to you in advance. If it is not, complain in writing and escalate to the ombudsman after six weeks.

Can I leave my contract if the price goes up?

It depends on the contract. If the rise was set out in pounds and pence when you signed, you agreed to it and cannot leave free. If the contract allowed a variable change that was not stated upfront, you get at least a month's notice and can usually leave penalty free, normally within 30 days.

Can Ofcom fine a provider for breaking the charter?

No. The charter is a set of voluntary commitments published by the government, not an Ofcom rule. Ofcom enforces its own rules, including the pounds-and-pence requirement and notice rights. If a signatory breaks the charter, quote it in a written complaint, then use the free ombudsman scheme if the provider does not put it right.
