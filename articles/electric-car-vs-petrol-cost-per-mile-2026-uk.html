7.7p, 3.6p Or 20.5p A Mile: What An Electric Car Actually Costs To Run In 2026, And What April 2028 Does To It

Almost every electric car comparison prices the car. That is the number you cannot control and cannot generalise. The number you can check is the mile, and on the Energy Saving Trust's own figures the same 220 miles costs £8, £17 or £45 depending on nothing more than how you fill it.

The short version

  • 220 miles costs about £17 charged at home, about £8 on an off-peak EV tariff and up to £45 in petrol or diesel. Those are the Energy Saving Trust's figures, updated 21 August 2026. Per mile that is 7.7p, 3.6p and 20.5p.
  • At 10,000 miles a year the gap is £1,681. Petrol at 20.5p a mile is £2,045. An electric car on an off-peak tariff at 3.6p a mile is £364. That gap is the whole case, and it is entirely dependent on the tariff, not the car.
  • The off-peak rate needs a smart meter. The Energy Saving Trust says these tariffs typically require one. Without a smart meter you are on the £17 column, not the £8 column, and the gap halves.
  • From April 2028 electric cars pay 3p a mile on top. That takes the off-peak figure from 3.6p to 6.6p and the standard home figure from 7.7p to 10.7p. Plug-in hybrids pay 1.5p. Petrol and diesel pay nothing new.
  • Watch the £50,000 line. An electric car with a list price above £50,000 pays a £440 a year supplement on top of the £200 standard rate. For petrol and diesel that line is £40,000.

1. Why the mile is the only number worth comparing

The purchase price of an electric car against a petrol one is not a stable comparison. It depends on the model, the trim, the discount, whether it is new or three years old, and what the used market is doing that month. Any article that prints it is out of date within weeks.

Cost per mile is different. It is set by two things that are published and checkable: the price of the energy and the efficiency of the car. Both move, but both move slowly and both are on a government or public-body page you can read yourself.

So this page prices the mile. If you want the wallbox arithmetic, we did that separately in the home charger and off-peak tariff piece, and this page will not repeat it.

2. Cost per mile, every column, at today's rates

The Energy Saving Trust's electric vehicle guidance, updated 21 August 2026, gives three figures for the same journey. A full charge in a fully electric car gives a typical real-world range of around 220 miles and costs approximately £17 charging at home. On an off-peak EV tariff, which typically requires a smart meter, the same full charge costs approximately £8. Driving a petrol or diesel car 220 miles could cost up to £45.

Divide each by 220 and you have the per-mile figures. The annual columns are those figures at 10,000 miles a year, which is a little above the typical British car's mileage and makes the arithmetic easy to rescale.

Two honest caveats. The £45 is described by the Energy Saving Trust as an upper figure, so a frugal small petrol car will beat 20.5p a mile. And the Energy Saving Trust separately notes that charging can work out as low as 2p a mile in the best case, which is below even our off-peak column. What the table does show reliably is the ordering and the size of the gap. Off-peak electric is roughly a fifth to a sixth of the cost of petrol per mile today. That does not become false at the edges of the range.

3. The tax side: £10, £200 and the £440 trap

GOV.UK's vehicle tax rate tables for cars registered on or after 1 April 2017 set the first-year rate for a 0g/km car at £10. Every car then moves to the standard rate, which is £200 for a single 12 month payment, or £210 if you spread it over 12 monthly Direct Debits.

The part that catches people is the expensive car supplement. GOV.UK states that you pay an extra £440 a year if you have a car or motorhome with a list price of more than £40,000, or an electric car or motorhome with a list price of more than £50,000. So electric cars get a £10,000 higher threshold, but they are no longer exempt.

The supplement is charged on the list price, not what you paid. A car listed at £50,500 that you negotiated down to £47,000 still pays it. It runs for five years from the second licence, so it is £2,200 in total, not a one-off.

GOV.UK also notes the supplement applies to electric vehicles registered between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2026, and that you do not pay it if you have a zero emission vehicle registered before 1 April 2025. If your car is older than that, this section does not apply to you.

4. What April 2028 does to the arithmetic

The Energy Saving Trust's page states that from April 2028 a new road charge will be introduced for electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles, set at 3p per mile for electric cars and 1.5p per mile for plug-in hybrids.

Three pence a mile is a large number against a 3.6p fuel cost. It nearly doubles the running cost of an electric car on an off-peak tariff, from 3.6p to 6.6p a mile. At 10,000 miles a year that is £300 added, taking the annual figure from £364 to £664.

It does not close the gap. Petrol stays at 20.5p a mile and gains nothing, so the electric car still runs at about a third of the cost. But anyone doing a ten-year ownership sum on today's 3.6p is using a number with a known expiry date, and should run the second half of the decade at 6.6p.

It is worth saying what this charge does not do. It changes nothing before April 2028. It does not replace the £200 standard rate, which continues. And it does not touch petrol or diesel, which pay fuel duty instead.

5. One payback that does hold up

Most electric car payback sums are unreliable because they rest on a purchase price premium that varies wildly. Here is one that does not, because both numbers are published.

Suppose you are choosing between two cars and the electric one crosses the £50,000 list price line, so it picks up the £440 a year expensive car supplement for five years. That is £2,200 of extra tax you would not otherwise pay.

At 10,000 miles a year, the fuel saving against petrol on an off-peak tariff is £2,045 minus £364, which is £1,681 a year.

£2,200 divided by £1,681 a year is 1.31 years, or about 16 months. The supplement everyone complains about is paid off, in full, by sixteen months of fuel savings. It is a real cost and worth knowing about, but it is not the thing that decides this purchase.

Run the same sum from April 2028 and the saving falls to £2,045 minus £664, which is £1,381 a year, and the payback stretches to 19 months. Still inside two years.

6. Worked example: Marcus in Doncaster

Marcus does 14,000 miles a year, mostly a motorway commute. He has off-street parking and a smart meter already fitted, so the off-peak column is genuinely available to him rather than theoretical.

Petrol at 20.5p a mile: £2,870 a year. Electric on an off-peak tariff at 3.6p a mile: £504 a year. His saving is £2,366 a year.

If he never gets round to switching tariff and charges on his standard rate at 7.7p a mile, it is £1,078 a year, and the saving drops to £1,792. The tariff switch is worth £574 a year to him on its own. That is the single highest-value thing on this page and it costs him one phone call.

From April 2028 the 3p per mile charge adds £420 a year at his mileage. His off-peak running cost becomes £924 and the saving becomes £1,946. He should plan on that number, not on today's.

Marcus's car lists at £46,000, under the £50,000 line, so he pays £10 in year one and £200 a year afterwards with no supplement. Had he specified the trim above, at £51,200, he would have added £2,200 over five years for the sake of £5,200 of car.

7. The honest verdict

If you can charge at home and get onto an off-peak tariff, an electric car costs about 3.6p a mile against about 20.5p for petrol on the Energy Saving Trust's figures, and at 10,000 miles a year that is £1,681 saved. Even the £440 expensive car supplement, if your car crosses the £50,000 list price line, is paid off by sixteen months of that saving. On running cost the case is not close.

Two things should change how you plan. Without a smart meter and an off-peak tariff you are on 7.7p a mile, not 3.6p, and the tariff switch is worth more than most people's choice of car. And from April 2028 the 3p per mile road charge takes the off-peak figure to 6.6p, so any ownership sum running past 2028 should use that number and not today's. If you cannot charge at home at all, none of these figures describe your situation and we would not buy on them.

Frequently asked

Do I need a smart meter for the cheap rate? The Energy Saving Trust says off-peak EV tariffs typically require a smart meter. Without one you are on the standard-rate column at 7.7p a mile, which is still well under petrol but roughly double the off-peak figure.

What if I cannot charge at home? Then none of the columns on this page apply to you cleanly, and we are not going to invent a public charging number, because there is no primary source for a single national rate. Price your nearest chargers yourself before committing.

Does the 3p per mile charge start in April 2028 for certain? It is the figure published on the Energy Saving Trust's page as of 21 August 2026. Rates announced years ahead can move at a Budget, and the next Budget is 28 October 2026. Treat the date as firm and the rate as reviewable.

Is the £440 supplement charged on what I paid? No. GOV.UK charges it on the list price, so a discount does not get you under the line. It applies from the second licence and runs for five years.
