Self Assessment payment on 31 January 2027: how much will you pay, and can you cut it?

Ruhul Amin · Published 9 November 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked November 2026

If you file a Self Assessment return, 31 January 2027 is often the biggest tax day of your year. You may owe the balance for 2025 to 2026 and the first half of an advance payment for 2026 to 2027, both on the same day. That second part catches people out. Here is how the sum works, what happens if you pay late, and how to ask HMRC to lower it if your income has dropped.

The short version

  • Both your 2025 to 2026 tax bill and your first payment on account for 2026 to 2027 are due by midnight on 31 January 2027. Source: GOV.UK, read 9 November 2026.
  • Each payment on account is half of last year's tax bill. The two payments fall on 31 January and 31 July.
  • You skip payments on account if last year's bill was under £1,000, or if you paid more than 80% of it outside Self Assessment, for example through your tax code.
  • 31 January 2027 is a Sunday. GOV.UK says to make sure payment reaches HMRC on the last working day before, which is Friday 29 January, unless you pay by Faster Payments or by card.
  • Late payment interest is 8.00% from 24 November 2026 (7.75% before that), and a 5% penalty arrives at 30 days.
  • If your income has fallen, you can ask HMRC to reduce your payments on account, online or on form SA303.

1. What exactly is due on 31 January 2027?

Many people think 31 January is the day they pay last year's tax. For anyone who has filed Self Assessment before, it is often two bills in one.

The first is your balancing payment. That is the tax you owe for 2025 to 2026 (the year ending 5 April 2026), minus any payments on account you already made towards it. It also includes any Capital Gains Tax, and student loan repayments if you are self-employed.

The second is your first payment on account towards 2026 to 2027. GOV.UK explains that payments on account are payments towards your next tax bill, including Class 4 National Insurance if you are self-employed. They spread the cost into two instalments. Each is half of the tax you owed last year.

The deadlines are on GOV.UK: 31 January for the previous year's balancing payment and your first payment on account, and 31 July for your second payment on account.

If you are new to the system, our guide to registering for Self Assessment explains how you end up here. For the filing deadlines and the penalties for a late return, see our piece on the paper and online return dates. This article is only about the money.

2. Who has to make payments on account?

The rules on GOV.UK are short. You must make both payments on account unless either of these is true:

  • the tax you owed last year was less than £1,000, or
  • you paid more than 80% of last year's tax outside Self Assessment, for example through your PAYE tax code or because your bank already deducted tax on your savings interest.

Your Self Assessment statement or online account shows the amounts if you need to pay them. The table below shows what lands on 31 January depending on your history.

Your situationWhat is due by 31 January 2027
First year filing, no payments on account made last yearThe full 2025 to 2026 tax bill plus the first payment on account (half of that bill)
Made payments on account last year, bill was higher than they coveredA balancing payment (bill minus payments already made) plus the first payment on account
Made payments on account last year, they covered the bill exactlyOnly the first payment on account for 2026 to 2027
2025 to 2026 bill under £1,000, or over 80% paid outside Self AssessmentNo payments on account. Only any balance still owed

The first row is the one that surprises people. Add the two together and your first January can cost one and a half times the tax you actually owe for the year. GOV.UK gives the same warning in its own example: a £3,000 bill becomes £4,500 on the first January, because you also pay £1,500 towards next year.

3. A worked example in Leeds

Amira is a freelance designer in Leeds. This is our own arithmetic, using the method on GOV.UK, with made up figures.

Her first Self Assessment return was for 2024 to 2025, and the tax was £2,400. In January 2026 she paid that £2,400 plus a first payment on account of £1,200 (half). She paid the second £1,200 on 31 July 2026. So she has paid £2,400 towards 2025 to 2026 in advance.

Her 2025 to 2026 return, filed online, shows a tax bill of £3,000. Her payments on account covered £2,400, so she owes a balancing payment of £600.

The first payment on account for 2026 to 2027 is half of £3,000, which is £1,500.

Total due by 31 January 2027: £600 plus £1,500, which is £2,100. Then she pays another £1,500 on 31 July 2027.

Her actual tax for 2025 to 2026 is £3,000, but cash leaves her account in a different pattern. That is why a freelancer who only budgets the tax bill can come up short.

A simple fix is to put aside a share of each invoice every month. Treat the 31 January and 31 July dates as bills you already know about, and keep that money in a separate savings account.

4. Can you reduce payments on account?

Yes, if you know your tax will be lower than last year. GOV.UK says you can ask HMRC to reduce your payments on account, online or by post. Online, sign in to your account, view your latest Self Assessment return and select Reduce payments on account. By post, send form SA303 to your tax office. You must say what you expect your tax bill to be, so HMRC can work out the new amounts.

Take Amira again. Suppose she loses her biggest client and expects her 2026 to 2027 tax bill to be about £1,000. Her default payments on account would be £1,500 twice, £3,000 in total, against a real bill of around £1,000. Reduced payments would be about £500 each. That is our own arithmetic, and it leaves £2,000 in her account through 2027 rather than with HMRC.

There is a catch. GOV.UK warns that if you reduce your payments and your bill turns out higher than you expected, you will be charged interest on the difference. Do not reduce them on a hopeful guess. Use a realistic estimate, based on your actual income so far this tax year.

ChoiceWhat it doesRisk
Pay the standard amountsHalf of last year's tax, twiceYou may overpay if income fell
Reduce online or on form SA303Lower payments based on your estimateInterest on any shortfall if you guess too low

If your income has risen, there is nothing to reduce. Any extra tax comes as a balancing payment the following January. Our guide to side hustle tax rules shows how the £1,000 allowance affects that bill.

5. What happens if you pay late?

HMRC charges interest on late payment and adds penalties. According to the HMRC interest rates page, the late payment rate is 8.00% from 24 November 2026. Before that date it was 7.75%, in place since 9 January 2026. The rate is set at Bank Rate plus 4 percentage points. Repayment interest, which HMRC pays you, is 3.00% from 24 November 2026.

On the GOV.UK penalties page, late payment penalties are 5% of the unpaid tax at 30 days, then again at 6 months and 12 months.

Using our own arithmetic, suppose Amira's £2,100 is paid 45 days late and the rate stays at 8.00%. Interest is about £21 (£2,100 times 8% times 45 over 365). The 30 day penalty is 5% of the £2,100 still unpaid, which is £105. In total she would pay about £126 extra for the delay.

Cannot pay in full? GOV.UK says you may be able to set up a payment plan to pay in instalments. HMRC checks whether the plan is affordable. If it cannot agree one, it will ask for the full amount. Contact HMRC before the deadline, not after.

6. How to pay, and how early

The time you need to allow depends on how you pay. GOV.UK lists these:

MethodTime to allow
Online or telephone banking (Faster Payments), CHAPS, debit or corporate credit card online, at your bankSame or next day
Bacs Direct Debit, if you have set one up with HMRC before3 working days
New Direct Debit5 working days

You can no longer pay at the Post Office. You can also pay in instalments before the deadline, through weekly or monthly payments towards your bill.

Because 31 January 2027 is a Sunday, treat Friday 29 January as your real deadline if you pay by a slower method. Faster Payments and card payments are the exceptions. Allow a few days anyway, since online payment services can be slow at busy times.

If you use Making Tax Digital for Income Tax, GOV.UK says you must pay any amount listed as due in either your Self Assessment account or your Making Tax Digital account. Check both. Our Making Tax Digital guide explains who is in.

7. The honest verdict

Check your statement now, not on 28 January. Your online account shows the balancing payment and the first payment on account. If the total is more than you expected, you have time to plan.

Reduce payments on account only if you have evidence your income has fallen. Use a realistic estimate. Cut them too far and HMRC charges interest on the gap.

Save for both dates from the start. A freelancer who only sets aside the tax bill will feel the extra half in January and again in July.

Frequently asked

Do I have to make payments on account every year? Only if last year's bill was £1,000 or more and you paid 80% or less of it outside Self Assessment. Your statement shows whether they apply. If your bill falls under £1,000, or most of it is collected through your tax code, they stop for that year.

Is a payment on account an extra tax? No. It is an advance towards your next bill, not a penalty. Each is half of last year's tax. If you pay more than you end up owing, GOV.UK says you may be able to claim a tax refund once your actual tax for the year is worked out.

What is the difference between a balancing payment and a payment on account? A balancing payment settles what you still owe for a finished tax year, after deducting payments already made. A payment on account is an advance towards the next tax year. Both can fall due on 31 January, which is why the total looks bigger than expected.

How do I reduce my payments on account? Sign in to your HMRC online account, view your latest Self Assessment return and choose Reduce payments on account. Or send form SA303 to your tax office. You state the tax you expect to owe. If you guess too low, interest is charged on the difference.
