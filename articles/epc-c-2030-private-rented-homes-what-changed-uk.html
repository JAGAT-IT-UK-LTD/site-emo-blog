The Rented Homes Deadline Is 2030, but the Rating It Is Measured Against Changes Next Month

Ruhul Amin · Published 11 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The government response of 21 January 2026 settled the argument about dates: one deadline, 1 October 2030, covering new and existing tenancies alike. What it did not settle in most of the coverage is that the meaning of a C is being rebuilt at the same time, and the new certificates start arriving from October 2026.

The short version

  • One deadline, 1 October 2030, for all private tenancies. The earlier idea of an earlier date for new tenancies and a later one for existing tenancies is gone.
  • The standard rises from EPC E to EPC C. E is the requirement today.
  • The government response was published on 21 January 2026 as part of the Warm Homes Plan, which is funded at just under GBP 15 billion.
  • Legislation is targeted for 2027, so the duty is settled policy but not yet law, and the detail can still move.
  • New-style EPCs are due from October 2026, built on the Home Energy Model rather than the current method.
  • C will no longer mean one number. Compliance is to be measured on a primary Fabric Performance rating plus a choice of either Heating System or Smart Readiness as a second metric.
  • That means a property rated C today is not automatically a C under the new metrics, and a property rated D today is not automatically outside them.
  • The government position is that meeting this should not require rent rises. Landlords and their trade bodies dispute that, and it is fair to say the cost question is not settled.

1. What was actually settled in January

Private rented homes in England and Wales currently have to reach EPC E to be let. That standard has been in place for years and most of the sector has adjusted to it.

On 21 January 2026, as part of the Warm Homes Plan, the government published its response to the consultation on raising it. Two things were decided.

The standard rises from E to C. That is a substantial jump rather than a nudge, and it captures a large share of older stock.

There is one deadline, not two. 1 October 2030, applying to all tenancies, new and existing. Earlier proposals had floated an earlier date for new tenancies with existing ones following later, which would have created a two-tier market and a strong incentive to avoid re-letting. That has been dropped in favour of a single date.

Legislation is targeted for 2027. So this is firm policy with a published date, but it is not yet law, and anyone telling you the detail is immovable is overstating it.

2. The part that got almost no coverage

Nearly every write-up of this stops at "EPC C by 2030". The more consequential change is what a C is going to mean.

Today an EPC gives you a single Energy Efficiency Rating, a number from 1 to 100 mapped onto a letter from G to A. It is heavily influenced by fuel costs, which is why a property can move band when nothing physical about it has changed.

New-style EPCs, built on the Home Energy Model, are due to start from October 2026, and they carry several metrics rather than one. The confirmed approach is that compliance will be measured against two of them: a primary Fabric Performance rating, plus a choice of either Heating System or Smart Readiness as the secondary.

Fabric first is a defensible and arguably overdue principle. Insulation, draughts and glazing are what actually determine whether a house is cheap to heat, and they last decades. But it means the target has moved sideways as well as upwards.

A landlord who spends this winter chasing points on the current rating may be optimising a number that is being retired. The measures that count are the ones that improve the fabric.

3. Why the order of events matters

Line the dates up and the practical advice falls out of them.

October 2026: new-style EPCs begin. 2027: legislation targeted. 1 October 2030: compliance required.

That order is unusual and it is in the landlord favour if used properly. The measurement method arrives roughly four years before the deadline, which is enough time to get a new-style assessment, see where the property actually sits on the metrics that will count, and then spend money once rather than twice.

The mistake available here is spending in 2026 and 2027 on whatever lifts the old single rating fastest, then discovering in 2028 that the fabric score is where the shortfall is. Measures that improve the fabric are highly likely to help under either system. Measures that mainly game the old cost-based calculation are the risky ones.

There will be an exemptions framework, as there is under the current standard, and a cost cap has been part of every version of these rules so far. The detail of both is exactly what the 2027 legislation has to settle, so any number quoted for a cap today is a forecast rather than a rule. I am not going to put one in this article for that reason.

4. What this means if you rent rather than let

Most coverage of this is written for landlords, which leaves out the larger group.

You can look up the EPC for any property free on GOV.UK before you view it, let alone before you sign. A band D or E terrace is telling you something concrete about what it will cost to keep warm, and that is a running cost you will carry for the length of the tenancy.

Between now and 2030 the rating on a rented home is also a fair proxy for what the landlord is going to have to do to it. A property already at C is unlikely to be disrupted. A property at F or G is on a longer journey.

What this does not give you is a right to demand improvements before 2030 or to withhold rent over a rating. The duty is on the landlord and it bites on that date, not now.

Worked example: a two-bed terrace in Rotherham

Priya lets a two-bed Victorian terrace. Solid walls, gas combi boiler fitted four years ago, loft insulated to a decent depth, single glazed at the back. The current EPC says band D.

Where she stands today: compliant. The requirement is E and she is a D.

Where she has to be: C, by 1 October 2030. One band, which sounds modest until you notice which band.

The tempting move: the boiler is the newest thing in the house, so on a heating-weighted view she looks reasonably placed and might be tempted to do very little.

Why that is the risk: under the confirmed approach the primary metric is Fabric Performance. Solid walls and single glazing are fabric problems, and a four year old boiler does nothing for them. Her strongest-looking asset sits in the secondary metric, and the weakest part of the house sits in the primary one.

The sequence that avoids paying twice: wait for a new-style assessment once they are available from October 2026, get the property scored on the metrics that will actually be used, and only then decide between solid wall insulation, glazing, or a heating and smart readiness route. That is roughly four years of runway between the measurement changing and the deadline landing.

What she can sensibly do before then: the cheap fabric work that helps under any methodology and cuts her tenant bills immediately. Draughtproofing, loft top-up if it is thin, and sealing the obvious gaps. None of it is wasted whichever way the metrics land.

5. The honest verdict

The direction is right. Rented homes are on average colder and leakier than owner occupied ones, and the people living in them pay for that in heating bills they cannot fix. A fabric-first standard is the correct thing to measure.

Two honest caveats. First, this is not law yet. Legislation is targeted for 2027 and the exemptions, the cost cap and the precise thresholds are all still to be written. Anyone quoting you a definitive cost per property today is guessing. Second, the government position that this should not require rent rises is a position, not a finding, and landlord bodies contest it. On a tight rental market it is reasonable to expect some of the cost to reach tenants, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Cost is only half of what has moved under landlords this year; the other half is possession, and what the end of Section 21 means for a tenancy now is the part tenants feel first.

For landlords the single most useful takeaway is about sequencing rather than spending: the metric changes before the deadline, so measure on the new basis before you commit serious money. Cheap fabric work is safe to do now. Large capital decisions are better made against a new-style assessment.

For tenants, the useful action is free and available today: look up the certificate before you sign, and treat a low band as a running cost rather than a detail.

Frequently asked

Does this apply in Scotland and Northern Ireland? This consultation and response cover the private rented sector in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland set their own standards.

Is there a cost cap or an exemption? The current standard has both, and the new one is expected to. The detail is for the 2027 legislation, so no figure quoted today is reliable. Check the GOV.UK landlord guidance for the rules in force now.

My property is already a C. Am I finished? Not necessarily. The C you hold was calculated under the current single rating. Compliance in 2030 is to be measured on the new Fabric Performance metric plus one other, so it is worth getting a new-style assessment when they become available rather than assuming.

What happens if a property is not at C by the deadline? Under the current framework, letting a property below the minimum standard without a valid exemption exposes the landlord to enforcement by the local authority. The enforcement detail for the new standard is part of what the legislation has to set out.
