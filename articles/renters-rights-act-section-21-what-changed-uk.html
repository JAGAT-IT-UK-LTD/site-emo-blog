Renters' Rights Act 2026: Section 21 Is Gone, Your Tenancy Rolls On, and Your Landlord Needs a Reason

Ruhul Amin · Published 5 September 2026 · Updated 11 November 2026 · 11 min read · Figures checked November 2026

Since 1 May 2026, the Renters' Rights Act has turned almost every private tenancy in England into a rolling one, ended section 21 no fault evictions, and limited rent rises to once a year. The last window for old section 21 notices closed on 31 July 2026. If you rent or let a home in England, this is the rulebook you now live under, and it has limits the headlines skip.

The short version

  • Since 1 May 2026 most existing assured shorthold tenancies in England have been assured periodic tenancies, and every new private tenancy is one. Tenancies roll weekly or monthly with no end date. Source: GOV.UK, Renters' Rights Act overview for tenants; commencement by SI 2026/421.
  • Section 21 no fault evictions ended on 1 May 2026, even if your agreement says otherwise. A landlord now needs a ground for possession, the correct form and the correct notice, usually four months.
  • Old section 21 notices are now dead. A landlord who served one before 1 May had until 31 July 2026 at the latest to apply to court. Claims made in time carry on; nothing new can be started on an old notice.
  • Rent can rise once a year, not in the first 12 months, on Form 4A with at least two months' notice, and you can challenge a rise above market rent at the tribunal. Rent in advance is capped at one month once you have signed, and no rent can be taken before you sign.
  • Landlords must state a price in any advert and cannot accept bids above it, cannot refuse you for being on benefits or having children, and must give a reason if they refuse a pet. You can end your tenancy with two months' notice in writing.
  • What it does not do: it does not cap rents, does not stop a landlord selling or moving in (after 12 months), does not apply to lodgers, council or housing association tenants or student halls, and does not apply in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.
  • Existing tenants should have had the government Information Sheet by 31 May 2026. A landlord who did not give it can be fined up to £7,000. Shelter and Citizens Advice give free help; nothing here is legal advice.

1. What happened to your tenancy on 1 May?

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 27 October 2025. Its main renting rules switched on in England on 1 May 2026.

The switch was made by SI 2026/421, the Renters' Rights Act 2025 (Commencement No. 2 and Transitional and Saving Provisions) Regulations 2026. They were made on 16 April 2026 and apply to private assured tenancies, not social housing.

GOV.UK's guidance for tenants, from the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, puts the effect in one line. Most existing assured shorthold tenancies automatically became assured periodic tenancies on 1 May 2026.

An assured periodic tenancy rolls on, weekly or monthly, depending on how rent is paid. It can no longer have an end date. If your agreement has one, that date no longer applies.

You did not need to sign anything new. Instead your landlord or letting agent had to give you a government Information Sheet explaining the changes by 31 May 2026.

If you never had a written agreement, the landlord must now give you the key terms in writing. Anyone who signed a new private tenancy on or after 1 May 2026 has been on an assured periodic tenancy from day one.

So the words fixed term, six months, twelve months and break clause no longer have legal force in a private tenancy in England.

2. Is section 21 abolished, and how can a landlord evict now?

Yes. Section 21 was abolished for private tenancies in England on 1 May 2026. A landlord can no longer use the no fault route, even where the tenancy agreement says they can.

A landlord who wants the home back now needs a ground for possession. These are the reasons set out in law, and the old section 8 process is now the only route.

GOV.UK names the main grounds. The landlord wants to sell or move in, which cannot take effect in the first 12 months. A student HMO needs to be relet for the new academic year. Rent has not been paid. Or there has been antisocial behaviour by the tenant, someone living with them or a visitor.

The landlord must also use the correct form and give the correct notice. GOV.UK says that is usually four months, but shorter for some grounds, such as serious rent arrears or antisocial behaviour.

A notice is not an eviction. If you do not leave when it ends, the landlord must apply to court for a possession order. Only a court order, followed by bailiffs, can remove you.

Leaving early because a notice arrived is a choice, not a duty. It is worth getting free advice before you decide.

3. What are the notice periods under the Renters' Rights Act?

The notice period depends on the ground. These are the main ones from GOV.UK's grounds for possession guidance for tenants, updated 1 May 2026.

GroundReasonNoticeKey condition
Ground 1Landlord or family moving in4 monthsCannot be used to end the tenancy in the first 12 months
Ground 1ASelling the property4 monthsNotice can be given in year one, but cannot expire before 12 months
Ground 4AStudent HMO needed for new students4 monthsTenant must be told at the start that this ground may be used
Ground 8Serious rent arrears4 weeksAt least 3 months' rent owed (13 weeks if paid weekly or fortnightly), on the notice day and at the hearing
Grounds 10 and 11Any arrears, or rent paid late again and again4 weeksCourt decides if possession is reasonable
Ground 12Breach of a tenancy term2 weeksCourt decides if possession is reasonable
Grounds 7A and 14Antisocial or criminal behaviourNo set periodCourt cannot make an order until 14 days after notice

Two details matter for tenants in arrears. Arrears caused by a Universal Credit payment that has not arrived are not counted for Ground 8. And if you clear the arrears below the threshold by the hearing, Ground 8 falls away.

Grounds 8, 1, 1A and 4A are mandatory. If the landlord proves them, the court must usually give possession. Grounds 10, 11, 12 and 14 are discretionary, so the court weighs whether it is reasonable.

4. What if you got a section 21 notice before 1 May?

The window for old notices has now closed. GOV.UK's guidance for landlords on notices given before 1 May 2026 was updated on 1 August 2026 to say exactly that.

A landlord who served a valid section 21 notice before 1 May had to apply to court by the earlier of two dates. Either the deadline set by the notice itself, or 31 July 2026. GOV.UK calls 31 July 2026 "the last date" for an application.

If the court claim was made in time, the case carries on until it ends. If it was not, the old notice cannot be used. The landlord must start again under the new section 8 grounds.

If a claim is dismissed, or the notice runs out, the tenancy becomes an assured periodic tenancy. The landlord then has one month to give you the Information Sheet.

So if you received a section 21 notice in early 2026 and have heard nothing from a court, you are very likely now on a rolling tenancy with full protection. Check with Shelter or Citizens Advice to be sure.

5. How do rent increases work now?

Rent can now rise only once a year. It cannot rise at all in the first 12 months of a new tenancy.

The landlord must use the statutory Form 4A and give at least two months' notice. Rent review clauses in old agreements no longer work.

If you think the new rent is above the market rent for your home, you can challenge it at the First-tier Tribunal. GOV.UK says so in plain terms. The tribunal compares your rent with similar homes nearby, not with your income.

On the way in, two more changes. A landlord cannot ask for, encourage or accept any rent before you sign. Once you have signed, the most they can ask for in advance is one month's rent.

Deposits are separate. They are still capped by the Tenant Fees Act 2019 at five weeks' rent where the yearly rent is under £50,000. Our guide to tenancy deposit protection covers how to get yours back.

Rental bidding has also ended. Every written advert or offer must state a set price, and the landlord or agent cannot accept or encourage offers above it.

The tenant who offers £50 a month more to jump the queue can no longer be taken up on it, at least not lawfully.

6. Pets, discrimination and the Information Sheet

You can now ask to keep a pet. The landlord must consider the request and must give a reason if they refuse. A blanket no pets clause is no longer the end of the matter.

It is now unlawful for a landlord to refuse you because you receive benefits or have children. That sits alongside the existing Equality Act protections.

Ending a tenancy from your side is simpler. You give two months' notice in writing, by letter, email or text, on the day rent is due or the day before. You keep paying rent through the notice, then leave. You and the landlord can agree a shorter period in writing.

The Information Sheet is the government's own summary of the changes. Landlords had to give it to existing tenants by 31 May 2026, as a printed copy or the PDF itself. A link to the PDF does not count, and every named tenant needs a copy.

Failing to give it is a breach. GOV.UK's enforcement guidance says councils can fine landlords up to £7,000 for a breach, and up to £40,000 for an offence instead of prosecution.

Tenants also have the First-tier Tribunal. For some offences, such as letting an unlicensed property, you can apply for a Rent Repayment Order. The tribunal can order back some or all of the rent paid over up to two years.

7. A worked example in Nottingham

Aleksandra rents a two bedroom flat in Nottingham. She signed a 12 month agreement in September 2025 at £895 a month, with a rent review clause after six months.

On 1 May 2026 it became a monthly assured periodic tenancy with no end date. Her agent emailed the Information Sheet PDF on 19 May, inside the 31 May deadline.

In June the agent proposed £995 a month from July. That was inside the first 12 months, and it was not on Form 4A. She replied quoting the GOV.UK guidance, and the agent withdrew it.

In September, once 12 months had passed, the agent served Form 4A. It proposed £975 a month from November, giving two months' notice.

Similar flats in her block were letting at around £920. She applied to the tribunal. Before the hearing, the landlord agreed £930.

On our arithmetic, £975 less £930 is £45 a month, or £540 a year saved against the Form 4A figure. Her rent still went up by £35 a month, £420 a year. And it cannot move again for 12 months.

Her neighbour Tomasz started his tenancy in April 2026. In August his landlord said he wanted to sell. Under Ground 1A, notice can be given in the first year, but it cannot expire before the 12 months are up.

So with four months' notice, the earliest it can end is April 2027. If Tomasz has not left by then, the landlord must apply to court, which costs the landlord a £415 fee. The Act does not stop the sale. It fixes the timetable.

The names and sums are illustrative. The rules behind them are the ones on GOV.UK.

8. What the Act does not do

It does not control rents. A landlord can still propose a market rent once a year. The tribunal tests it against the market, not against what you can afford, so in a rising market rents still rise.

It does not stop evictions. It requires a ground, and the selling and moving in grounds are broad ones, delayed by 12 months rather than removed.

It does not write off arrears. It changes the notice and threshold rules, not the debt.

It covers private tenancies in England only. GOV.UK says different rules apply if you rent from a housing association or council, live in student halls, or are a lodger. That is why our lodger guide is unaffected. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own tenancy law.

And it does not enforce itself. A landlord can still serve an invalid notice. Your protection is knowing it is invalid and getting help. This article is general information, not legal advice.

9. What is still to come?

1 May 2026 was phase one. The government's roadmap, published on 13 November 2025, sets out what follows.

A Private Rented Sector Database, where landlords must register, is due to start rolling out "from late 2026". A landlord ombudsman, which every private landlord must join, is expected in 2028.

A Decent Homes Standard for private rented homes was proposed for 2035 or 2037, subject to consultation. Awaab's Law on damp and mould for private renters has no date yet.

Separate energy rules are also coming for rented homes. Our guide to the EPC C target for private rented homes explains what landlords face by 2030.

10. What should tenants and landlords do now?

Tenants. Check you have the Information Sheet. If not, ask for it in writing and keep a copy of the request.

Read any rent rise notice closely. It must be on Form 4A, give two months' notice, and come at least 12 months after the last rise or the start of your tenancy.

If you get a possession notice, check the ground and the dates against the table above. Do not move out before taking free advice.

When you leave, document the home's condition. Our guide to deposit deductions and fair wear and tear shows what a landlord can and cannot charge.

Landlords. Make sure every tenant got the Information Sheet PDF, or the written terms, and keep proof. Use Form 4A for any rise, once a year. Watch for the database registration opening, and budget for the ombudsman.

If a section 21 claim was not issued by 31 July 2026, it is gone. Any new possession claim needs a ground from the new list.

11. The honest verdict

Since 1 May 2026 your tenancy rolls, your landlord needs a ground to evict, rent can rise once a year on Form 4A with a tribunal challenge, and no more than one month's rent can be taken in advance. Those are the four things to know.

It is not rent control and it is not eviction control. Selling and moving in remain grounds after 12 months, and the tribunal tests a rise against the market, not your income.

Old section 21 notices are finished, and the next protections, a landlord database and an ombudsman, are still on the way. If a notice or a rent rise looks wrong, use Shelter or Citizens Advice before you act. A wrong notice protects you only if you know it is wrong.

Frequently asked questions about the Renters' Rights Act

Has section 21 been abolished?

Yes. Section 21 no fault evictions ended for private tenancies in England on 1 May 2026, under the Renters' Rights Act 2025 and SI 2026/421. A landlord now needs a ground for possession, the right form and the right notice. Old notices could only be used if a court claim was made by 31 July 2026.

What is the notice period under the Renters' Rights Act?

For a tenant leaving, two months in writing. For a landlord, it depends on the ground. Selling or moving in needs four months and cannot end the tenancy in the first year. Serious rent arrears need four weeks. Antisocial behaviour has no set period, but the court cannot make an order within 14 days.

Can a tenant refuse a rent increase?

You cannot simply refuse, but you can challenge it. A rise must be on Form 4A, with at least two months' notice, once a year and not in the first 12 months. If you think the new rent is above market rent, apply to the First-tier Tribunal before the rise starts.

What happens if my landlord did not give me the Information Sheet?

Ask for it in writing and keep the request. Landlords had to give it by 31 May 2026, and councils can fine them up to £7,000 for the breach. Your tenancy still became periodic on 1 May whether or not you received the sheet, so your new rights apply either way.

Does the Act apply to lodgers or council tenants?

No. GOV.UK says different rules apply to lodgers, council and housing association tenants, and students in halls. The Act also covers England only, so tenants in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland follow their own laws. If you are unsure which rules cover you, Shelter and Citizens Advice can tell you for free.
