Student loan interest rates from September 2026: what changed, and why the 6% cap missed most people

Student loan interest rose from 3.2% to 4.1% on 1 September 2026 for Plan 1, Plan 4 and Plan 5, with no cap. On Plan 2 the new 6% cap only helps people earning more than £44,268. Your monthly repayment did not change, because it depends on a threshold that is now frozen.

The short version

  • The new rates started on 1 September 2026 and run to 31 August 2027. They use March 2026's RPI of 4.1%, up from the 3.2% that applied the year before.
  • Plan 1, Plan 4 and Plan 5 all went from 3.2% to 4.1%. The 6% cap does not cover them. That is every Scottish and Northern Irish borrower, plus every English student who started from August 2023.
  • On Plan 2 the cap only bites above £44,268. Below that your rate rose the full 0.9 points. Only above £51,318 did a rate actually fall.
  • Postgraduate (Plan 3) borrowers are the clear winner, at 6% instead of the 7.1% the formula would have given.
  • The Bank Rate rise on 5 November changed nothing. Plan 1 and Plan 4 take the lower of RPI or Bank Rate plus 1%. With Bank Rate at 4%, that is 5%, so RPI's 4.1% still applies.
  • The 28 October 2026 Budget kept the Plan 2 threshold freeze. It stays at £29,385 for three years from April 2027. Your monthly repayment is set by that threshold, not by the interest rate.

1. What are student loan interest rates from September 2026?

Student loan interest rates are reset every 1 September using the Retail Prices Index from the previous March. March 2026's RPI was 4.1%. The Student Loans Company confirmed the new rates on 10 August 2026, and they took effect on 1 September 2026.

Here is the whole picture for the year to 31 August 2027, checked against GOV.UK today:

Plan 1 (England and Wales before 2012, all Northern Ireland): 3.2% to 4.1%. It is the lower of RPI or Bank Rate plus 1%.

Plan 2 (England 2012 to 2023, Wales 2012 onwards): was 3.2% to 6.2%, now 4.1% to 6.0% depending on income.

Plan 3 (postgraduate): RPI plus 3% would be 7.1%. It is capped at 6.0%.

Plan 4 (Scotland): 3.2% to 4.1%.

Plan 5 (England, courses from August 2023): 3.2% to 4.1%. RPI only.

Three of the five plans took the full 0.9 point rise with no protection at all. The 6% cap was announced on 7 April 2026 and covers Plan 2 and Plan 3 only. The Budget on 28 October 2026 listed it as a costed measure for 1 September 2026 to 31 August 2027.

The rate only matters through your balance. The figure that hits your payslip is the threshold, so what you actually repay on each plan in 2026/27 is the number to check first.

2. Why does the 6% student loan cap reach almost nobody?

Plan 2 interest is not a single rate. It slides with your income.

At or below £29,385 you pay RPI. At £52,885 or above you pay the top rate. In between, it rises in a straight line across that £23,500 band. That gives you a sum you can run yourself:

With RPI at 4.1%, the cap only matters once the sliding part passes 1.9 points. That is 1.9 divided by 3, or 63.3% of the way up the band.

£29,385 plus 63.3% of £23,500 = £44,268.

Earn less than £44,268 and your rate is already below 6%. The cap does nothing for you, and you pay 0.9 points more than you did in August.

Run the same sum on last year's 3.2% RPI and you get £51,318. That was the income at which borrowers were already paying more than 6% before September.

Between £44,268 and £51,318 your rate still rose, just by less. Only above £51,318 did it actually fall.

Plan 2 students still studying are the other winners. During study the rate is RPI plus 3%, which would have been 7.1%. They pay 6%. Parents face a different set of income bands on the way in, which is where the maintenance loan and the household income cut-offs matter.

3. What changed after September 2026?

Two things happened since this guide was first written. Neither moved your student loan rate.

First, Bank Rate. It stayed at 3.75% through September and October. Then the Bank of England raised it to 4% on 5 November 2026. You can read what the 5 November Bank Rate rise means for your money in our separate guide.

For Plan 1 and Plan 4 the test is simple. Bank Rate plus 1% is now 5%. RPI is 4.1%. The lower figure wins, so the rate stays at 4.1%.

Second, the Budget on 28 October 2026. It did not reverse the Plan 2 threshold freeze. The Budget's own tables record a revaluation of the student loan book in 2027/28 caused by that freeze. In plain terms, the government is counting on it.

The Student Loans Company also confirmed one new threshold. From 6 April 2027 the Plan 1 threshold rises to £28,005. Plan 1 is not frozen.

4. Why is the frozen threshold the number that moves your money?

Here is what gets lost every September. Your interest rate has no effect on what leaves your payslip.

Someone with £80,000 of debt and someone with £12,000 on the same salary pay the identical amount each month. Interest only moves the balance. Many Plan 2 borrowers will never clear that balance before it is written off.

What does move your repayment is the threshold. The 2026/27 figures on GOV.UK:

Plan 1: £26,900 · Plan 2: £29,385 · Plan 4: £33,795 · Plan 5: £25,000 · Postgraduate: £21,000

You repay 9% of income above the threshold on Plans 1, 2, 4 and 5, and 6% on the postgraduate loan.

At the Budget on 26 November 2025 the government set the Plan 2 threshold at £29,385 from April 2026. It then freezes there for three years from April 2027, with uprating due to resume in April 2030. The October 2026 Budget left that in place.

It works the same way as frozen income tax thresholds. Nothing moves, so more of your pay falls into the charge every year that wages rise.

5. Does the Plan 2 freeze apply in Wales?

No. The freeze applies to borrowers from England. In February 2026 the Welsh Government confirmed it would not follow the Plan 2 threshold freeze for Welsh borrowers.

That matters because Wales never moved to Plan 5. Welsh students starting this September were still issued Plan 2 loans.

So from April 2027 you get two graduates, both on Plan 2, both on the same salary, repaying different amounts every month. The difference is decided entirely by where they lived when they applied.

6. Worked example: Aisha in Luton

Aisha is 31, works in local government and earns £34,000. She started university in 2015, so she is on Plan 2 with about £48,000 outstanding.

Her repayment now. £34,000 minus £29,385 is £4,615. Nine per cent is £415.35 a year, or £34.61 a month.

Her interest since 1 September. She is well below £44,268, so the cap does nothing. Her income sits 19.6% of the way up the band, which adds 0.59 points. Her rate moved from about 3.8% to about 4.7%.

What that means in pounds. On £48,000, 4.7% is roughly £2,250 of interest a year against £415 of repayments. Her balance grows by around £1,800 a year. The September rise alone added about £430 of that.

The freeze, from April 2027. At 3% uprating the threshold would have reached £30,267, then £31,175, then £32,110. Frozen at £29,385, Aisha pays 9% of the gap: about £79 extra in 2027/28, £161 in 2028/29 and £245 in 2029/30. Roughly £486 over three years.

The 3% uprating figure is my own illustration, not an official forecast. Higher inflation makes the freeze cost more, lower inflation less. The direction does not change.

Notice the shape of it. Aisha is not a high earner, yet the freeze costs her the same in pounds as a graduate on £70,000. It is a flat 9% of the same frozen gap.

Her one legitimate lever is pension salary sacrifice, since repayments are worked out on pay after the sacrifice. It is not a trick, and it is not free, because the money is locked away until pension age.

The honest verdict

There is nothing to buy here and, for most people, nothing to do. Your monthly repayment did not move on 1 September. If you will never clear the balance, the rate change is a number on a statement that gets written off in the end.

What is worth ten minutes: check which plan your payroll has you on. Being put on the wrong one is common and expensive. If your income for a tax year came in below your annual threshold but repayments were taken in a busy month, you can claim that back from the Student Loans Company.

And do not overpay voluntarily unless you are confident you will clear the loan in full. This is one of the few debts where paying it down early is usually the wrong move.

Frequently asked

What is the student loan interest rate for Plan 2 now? From 1 September 2026 to 31 August 2027, Plan 2 is 4.1% if you earn £29,385 or less. It rises with income to a maximum of 6% at £52,885 or more. Without the cap the top rate would have been 7.1%. Students still studying pay the 6% top rate.

Does the 6% cap mean my repayments go down? No. Repayments are set only by your salary and your plan's threshold, at 9% of income above it. The cap changes how fast your balance grows, not what leaves your pay each month. Two people on the same salary repay the same amount, whatever their balances or interest rates.

Did the Bank Rate rise change Plan 1 or Plan 4 interest? No. Both plans charge the lower of RPI or Bank Rate plus 1%. Bank Rate rose to 4% on 5 November 2026, so Bank Rate plus 1% is 5%. That is above RPI's 4.1%, so the rate stays at 4.1%. The next Bank Rate decision is on 17 December 2026.

Will the 6% cap continue after August 2027? Nobody can say yet. The cap is set only for 1 September 2026 to 31 August 2027, and the October 2026 Budget did not extend it. The next rates will use March 2027's RPI. Check GOV.UK next summer, when the Student Loans Company announces the September 2027 rates.

Is the Plan 2 threshold freeze definitely happening? It is firm policy. It was set at the Budget on 26 November 2025, and the 28 October 2026 Budget left it in place, counting the effect in its 2027/28 figures. The threshold stays at £29,385 from April 2027 for three years. Wales has said it will not follow it.
