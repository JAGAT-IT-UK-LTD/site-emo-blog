The 6% Cap Was Sold as Protection. On Tuesday, Most Graduates Get a Rate Rise Instead

Ruhul Amin · Published 27 August 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked August 2026

Student loan interest resets on 1 September 2026, and the government's new 6% cap is the headline everyone remembers. Run the numbers and the cap only reaches Plan 2 borrowers earning more than £44,268. Everyone on Plan 1, Plan 4 and Plan 5 gets no cap at all and a rise of 0.9 percentage points.

The short version

  • Rates reset on Tuesday 1 September 2026 using March 2026's RPI of 4.1%, up from the 3.2% in force since September 2025.
  • Plan 1, Plan 4 and Plan 5 all go 3.2% to 4.1%. The 6% cap does not cover them: that is all Scottish and Northern Irish borrowers, plus every English student who started from August 2023.
  • On Plan 2 the cap only bites above £44,268. Below that your rate rises the full 0.9 points and the cap is irrelevant. Only above £51,318 does a rate actually fall.
  • Plan 3 postgraduate borrowers are the one clear winner, dropping from 6.2% to 6%.
  • None of it changes your monthly repayment. Interest only moves the balance. The threshold moves your payslip, and the Plan 2 threshold is frozen at £29,385 for three years from April 2027.
  • That freeze does not apply in Wales. The Welsh Government has said it will not freeze the threshold, so from April 2027 two identical graduates repay different amounts.

1. What actually changes on 1 September

Student loan interest is set each 1 September using the Retail Prices Index from the previous March. March 2026's RPI was published on 22 April 2026 at 4.1%, up from the 3.2% that has applied since September 2025. Here is the whole picture for the year to 31 August 2027:

Plan 1 (England and Wales pre 2012, all Northern Ireland): 3.2% to 4.1%. It is the lower of RPI or base rate plus 1%, and base rate is 3.75%, so RPI is the lower figure.

Plan 2 (England 2012 to 2023, Wales 2012 onwards): 3.2% to 6.2% today, becoming 4.1% to 6.0%.

Plan 3 (postgraduate): 6.2% falling to 6.0%.

Plan 4 (Scotland): 3.2% to 4.1%.

Plan 5 (England, courses from August 2023): 3.2% to 4.1%. RPI only.

Three of the five plans take the full 0.9 point rise with no protection at all. The 6% cap, announced on 7 April 2026 by the Minister for Skills, Jacqui Smith, covers Plan 2 and Plan 3 only. The rate only bites through the repayment threshold, so what you actually repay on each plan in 2026-27 is the number to check first.

2. Why the 6% cap reaches almost nobody

Plan 2 interest is not a single rate, it slides. At or below £29,385 you pay RPI. At £52,885 or above you pay RPI plus the full 3%. In between it rises in a straight line across that £23,500 band, which gives you a sum you can run yourself:

With RPI at 4.1%, the cap needs the sliding part to reach 1.9 points. That is 1.9 divided by 3, which is 63.3% of the way up the band. Parents face the same banding on the way in, which is where the maintenance loan and the household income cut-offs matter.

£29,385 plus 63.3% of £23,500 = £44,268.

Earn less than £44,268 and your uncapped rate is already below 6%, so the cap does nothing for you. You pay 0.9 points more than you did in August. Run the same sum on today's 3.2% RPI and you get £51,318, the income at which you are already paying more than 6% now.

Between £44,268 and £51,318 your rate still rises, just by less than it would have. Only above £51,318 does it actually fall.

Plan 2 students still studying are the other winners. During study the rate is RPI plus the full 3%, which would have been 7.1%. They get 6%.

3. The number that moves your money is frozen

Here is what gets lost every September. Your interest rate has no effect on what leaves your payslip. Someone with £80,000 of debt and someone with £12,000 on the same salary pay the identical amount each month. Interest only moves the balance, and only about a third of Plan 2 borrowers who started in 2022/23 are projected to clear theirs anyway.

What does move your repayment is the threshold. The 2026/27 figures:

Plan 1: £26,900 · Plan 2: £29,385 · Plan 3: £21,000 · Plan 4: £33,795 · Plan 5: £25,000

You repay 9% of income above the threshold on Plans 1, 2, 4 and 5, and 6% on Plan 3.

At the Budget on 26 November 2025 the government confirmed the Plan 2 threshold rises to £29,385 from April 2026 and is then frozen there for three years from April 2027, with uprating resuming in April 2030. It is the same mechanism as frozen income tax thresholds: nothing moves, so more of your pay falls into the charge every year wages rise.

4. The Welsh exception nobody is reporting

The freeze applies to borrowers from England. The Welsh Government has stated in the Senedd that it will not freeze the threshold in this way for Welsh borrowers.

That matters more than it sounds, because Wales never moved to Plan 5. English students starting from August 2023 are on Plan 5, but Welsh students starting this September are still issued Plan 2 loans. So from April 2027 you get two graduates, both on Plan 2, both on the same salary, repaying different amounts every month for at least three years, decided entirely by where they lived when they applied. If you are a Welsh borrower, none of the freeze applies to you.

5. Worked example: Aisha in Luton

Aisha is 31, works in local government and earns £34,000. She started university in 2015, so she is on Plan 2 with about £48,000 outstanding.

Her repayment now. £34,000 minus £29,385 is £4,615. Nine per cent is £415.35 a year, or £34.61 a month.

Her interest from 1 September. Well below £44,268, so the cap does nothing. Her sliding rate moves from about 4.1% to about 5.0%. On £48,000 that is roughly £432 of interest against £415 of repayments, so her balance still grows.

The freeze, from April 2027. At 3% uprating the threshold would have reached £30,267, then £31,175, then £32,110. Frozen at £29,385, Aisha pays 9% of the gap: about £79 extra in 2027/28, £161 in 2028/29 and £245 in 2029/30. Roughly £486 over three years, reaching £20 a month.

The 3% uprating assumption is mine and illustrative, not official. Higher inflation makes the freeze cost more, lower inflation less, but the direction does not change.

Notice the shape of it. Aisha is not a high earner, yet the freeze costs her the same in pounds as it costs a graduate on £70,000, because it is a flat 9% of the same frozen gap. Her one legitimate lever is pension salary sacrifice, since repayments are calculated on post sacrifice pay. Not a trick, and not free, because the money is locked until pension age.

The honest verdict

There is nothing to buy here and, for most people, nothing to do on Tuesday. Your monthly repayment does not move. If you are among the two thirds who will never clear the balance, the rate change is a number on a statement that gets written off in your fifties.

What is worth ten minutes: check which plan your payroll has you on, because being put on the wrong one is common and expensive. If your income for a tax year came in below your annual threshold but repayments were deducted in a busy month, you can claim that back from the Student Loans Company.

And do not overpay voluntarily unless you are confident you will clear the loan in full. This is one of the few debts where paying it down early is usually the wrong move.

Frequently asked

Does the 6% cap mean my repayments go down? No. Repayments are set by salary and threshold only. The cap changes how fast the balance grows, not what leaves your pay.

Could Plan 1 and Plan 4 rates change again this year? Yes, and it is the one date worth diarising. Both take the lower of RPI or base rate plus 1%. Base rate is 3.75%, so base plus 1% is 4.75%, above RPI's 4.1%. The Bank of England next decides on 17 September 2026, and it would take cuts totalling 0.75 points to pull those rates below 4.1%. Plans 2, 3 and 5 are unaffected by base rate.

I have both an undergraduate and a postgraduate loan. They run separately and both are deducted: 6% above £21,000 on the postgraduate loan, 9% above your undergraduate threshold, at the same time.

Is the threshold freeze definitely happening? It was confirmed at the Budget on 26 November 2025 for April 2027, 2028 and 2029. Budgets can revisit it, so treat it as firm policy rather than settled law and recheck after this autumn's Budget.
