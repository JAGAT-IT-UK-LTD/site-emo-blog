The Professional Rate Is £31.92 an Hour. The Agencies Pay About £12

Proofreading is sold as the ideal side hustle for anyone with good English: flexible, remote, no equipment. The work is real and the rates are published. The gap between the professional rate and what a beginner is actually offered is the part nobody puts in the advert.

The short version

  • The CIEP suggested minimum for proofreading is £31.92 an hour for 2026. Copy editing is £37.11, substantial editing £42.66, project management £46.19.
  • Academic proofreading agencies typically pay £6 to £10 per 1,000 words. At a realistic 1,500 to 2,000 words an hour, that is about £9 to £20 an hour.
  • On Fiverr, beginners are competing at £3 to £15 per 1,000 words. The bottom of that range is under £6 an hour and below the minimum wage of £12.71.
  • The CIEP rate assumes you run a business, covering your own tax, software, insurance and unpaid admin. It is not comparable to an employed hourly wage.
  • The route to the professional rate is direct clients, not platforms. That takes months, and it is the part the training course adverts skip.

1. The published rates, and what they assume

The Chartered Institute of Editing and Proofreading publishes suggested minimum rates, which is unusually useful because most freelance work has no published benchmark at all.

Work typeCIEP suggested minimum, 2026
Proofreading£31.92 an hour
Copy editing£37.11 an hour
Substantial editing, rewriting, development editing£42.66 an hour
Project management£46.19 an hour

Read the assumption carefully. The CIEP is explicit that these rates are for a freelance professional running their own business, covering income tax, National Insurance, software subscriptions, professional membership, insurance, and all the unpaid time spent quoting, invoicing and chasing payment.

So £31.92 an hour is not equivalent to £31.92 an hour employed. Take off tax and the unbilled admin and the comparable employed figure is closer to £20. Still good. Just not what it looks like at first glance.

2. What agencies actually pay

Almost nobody starts with direct clients. Most people start with an academic proofreading company, which pays £6 to £10 per 1,000 words.

Convert it, because per word rates are designed to be hard to compare:

Your speedAt £6 per 1,000 wordsAt £10 per 1,000 words
1,000 words an hour, a careful beginner£6.00 an hour£10.00 an hour
1,500 words an hour£9.00 an hour£15.00 an hour
2,000 words an hour, experienced£12.00 an hour£20.00 an hour

On the gig platforms it is worse. Fiverr beginners compete at £3 to £15 per 1,000 words, and the low end of that is not a business, it is a loss. Upwork runs £8 to £25 an hour depending on your profile and reviews, which is better, but the reviews take months to build.

A per 1,000 words rate hides your hourly rate, which is exactly why the work is quoted that way. Convert every offer before you accept it.

3. Speed, which decides your hourly rate

The industry expectation is at least 1,500 to 2,000 words an hour for proofreading. That number is the single biggest lever on your earnings, and beginners are almost always far below it.

Two things slow people down. The first is doing the wrong job: proofreading is a final check for errors, not rewriting clumsy sentences. If you find yourself restructuring paragraphs, you are doing copy editing at proofreading rates, which is the most common way new proofreaders lose money.

The second is not using the tools. Style sheets, find and replace, PerfectIt or similar consistency checkers, and macros for repeated fixes are how experienced proofreaders hit 2,000 words an hour without lowering quality.

4. Does it pass the £12.71 test?

The benchmark for any UK side hustle is the National Living Wage, £12.71 an hour from April 2026. Against that:

  • Fiverr at the bottom of the range: fails badly. £3 per 1,000 words at 1,500 words an hour is £4.50 an hour, roughly a third of minimum wage.
  • Academic agency work, beginner speed: fails. £6 to £8 per 1,000 at 1,200 words an hour is £7 to £10.
  • Academic agency work, experienced: passes, just. £10 per 1,000 at 2,000 words an hour is £20, and that is before tax and unpaid admin.
  • Direct clients at anything near CIEP rates: passes comfortably. This is the only version that is properly worth your evenings.

The honest summary: proofreading is a poor side hustle for the first six months and a good one after that, if you get through the first six months. Most people do not, which is why the field is not saturated at the top.

5. Starting without paying for a course

The proofreading course industry is larger than the proofreading industry, and that should tell you something. Courses can be genuinely useful, but you do not need one to find out whether you can do the work or whether you enjoy it.

  • Test yourself first, free. Take a real document, proofread 2,000 words, and time it. If you are at 600 words an hour and still missing errors, a course will not fix that quickly.
  • Learn the standard marks and the difference between proofreading and copy editing. Free CIEP resources cover this.
  • Build three samples. A student essay, a business document and a piece of web copy, each with a before and after. This is what a client actually wants to see.
  • Apply to academic agencies for the volume, accept that the rate is poor, and treat it as paid practice while your speed rises.
  • Move to direct clients as soon as you can. Local businesses, self publishing authors, PhD students, small charities. This is where the rate doubles.
  • Then consider a recognised course, once you know the work suits you. Doing it in this order means the course is an investment rather than a gamble.

6. What AI changed, honestly

Pretending nothing changed would be dishonest. Free grammar tools now catch a large share of basic typos and agreement errors, which has compressed the bottom of the market. Simple typo hunting on straightforward text is worth less than it was.

What has not been compressed: work where being wrong is expensive or where judgement is required. Academic work with strict style guides and citation formats. Legal and medical documents. Anything going to print. Text where a machine suggestion would change the meaning. Non native English writing where the fix requires understanding the intent, not just the grammar.

The practical adjustment is to stop competing on catching typos and compete on the judgement part, because that is where the £31.92 lives and where a tool cannot follow.

7. A worked first six months

Nadia, 38, teaching assistant in Coventry. Ten hours a week available.

Month one. No spend. Reads the free CIEP guidance, times herself on three documents. Starting speed 900 words an hour. Builds three before and after samples.

Months two and three. Accepted by an academic agency at £8 per 1,000 words. Speed rises from 900 to 1,400 words an hour. She proofreads roughly 60,000 words across the two months. Earned £480, over about 48 hours, so £10 an hour. Below minimum wage.

Month four. Speed now 1,800 words an hour. Same agency, 45,000 words. £360 over 25 hours, so £14.40 an hour. Now above the benchmark.

Months five and six. She approaches nine local businesses and two self publishing authors with her samples. Two say yes. She charges £28 an hour, below CIEP but well above agency rates, and bills 22 hours across the two months alongside continuing agency work. £616 direct plus £400 agency.

Six month total: about £1,856, roughly 130 hours, an average of £14.30 an hour. The average hides the shape: £10 an hour at the start, £28 by the end. Her earnings did not rise because she got better at English. They rose because her speed doubled and she stopped selling through a middleman.

Tax note: she passed the £1,000 trading allowance in month four, on gross income, so she needs to register for Self Assessment.

The honest verdict

Proofreading is a real profession with published rates, and £31.92 an hour is genuinely achievable. It is also a poor first six months, because the entry route is agency work at £6 to £10 per 1,000 words and your beginner speed makes that under minimum wage.

Do it if you have patience and can treat the first months as paid training. Do not do it if you need the money now, because the honest answer is that the rate only becomes good once your speed doubles and you find direct clients.

Test yourself for free before you pay anyone for a course. Two thousand words and a stopwatch will tell you more about whether this suits you than any sales page will.

Frequently asked

Do I need a qualification? No, there is no licence. Clients want evidence you can do it, which means samples and testimonials. A recognised course helps with credibility, particularly for academic work, but it is not a legal requirement.

Proofreading or copy editing, which pays better? Copy editing, at a suggested £37.11 against £31.92. It is also harder and slower. The mistake to avoid is doing copy editing while being paid for proofreading.

Where do beginners find work? Academic proofreading agencies for volume, then direct clients for rate. Upwork sits between the two. Fiverr at the bottom of its range is not worth your time.

Do I need insurance? Professional indemnity insurance is worth considering once you take direct commercial clients, and some contracts require it. It is not needed to proofread student essays through an agency.
