Savings Tax Rises To 24% In April 2027: What To Do Before Then

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 November 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked November 2026

From 6 April 2027, interest above your tax free allowances is taxed at 24%, 44% or 49%, up from 20%, 40% and 45%. Most savers will pay nothing extra, but higher rate taxpayers with savings outside an ISA should act before then, and the cash ISA limit is staying at £20,000.

The short version

  • From 6 April 2027, tax on savings interest rises to 24%, 44% and 49%. Today it is 20%, 40% and 45%. The new rates apply across the whole UK.
  • The tax free allowances do not move. The Personal Savings Allowance stays at £1,000 for basic rate and £500 for higher rate taxpayers, and the starting rate for savings stays at £5,000.
  • The cash ISA cut to £12,000 has been scrapped. Budget 2026 keeps the cash ISA limit at £20,000 for all savers over 18 from April 2027.
  • Most savers will pay nothing extra. The average instant access rate was 2.08% in October 2026, so £20,000 earns about £416, well inside the £1,000 allowance.
  • Higher rate taxpayers with savings outside an ISA are the ones who pay. For them, the move is to fill an ISA before 5 April 2027 and again after 6 April 2027.

1. What exactly changes in April 2027?

HMRC published the policy paper on 29 October 2026, the day after the Budget. It raises the three savings rates of Income Tax for the 2027 to 2028 tax year and later years.

Savings tax band2026 to 2027 (now)2027 to 2028 (from 6 April 2027)
Starting rate for savings0% on up to £5,0000% on up to £5,000
Savings basic rate20%24%
Savings higher rate40%44%
Savings additional rate45%49%
Personal Savings Allowance, basic rate£1,000£1,000
Personal Savings Allowance, higher rate£500£500

All of these figures come from HMRC's Budget 2026 rates and allowances annex. Additional rate taxpayers get no Personal Savings Allowance at all.

A note on the size of the rise. HMRC's paper costs it as 2 percentage points, because the Finance Act 2026 had already set higher rates for 2027 to 2028. Measured against what you pay today, it is 4 points on every band.

Your wages are not affected. These are rates on savings interest only, the slice of your income that comes from bank, building society and similar accounts. Property income gets the same new rates in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Dividends are a separate set of rates, which our dividend tax guide covers.

2. Will I actually pay any of it?

Probably not. HMRC estimates about 12% of taxpayers will pay more in 2027 to 2028, by £113 a year on average. For everyone else, the allowances still cover all their interest.

Interest goes through three tax free layers before any tax applies. GOV.UK sets them out like this:

  1. Your Personal Allowance of £12,570. If your wages or pension do not use it all, the rest covers interest.
  2. The starting rate for savings, up to £5,000. You get it if your other taxable income is under £17,570. Every £1 of other income above £12,570 cuts it by £1.
  3. The Personal Savings Allowance. £1,000 for basic rate, £500 for higher rate, nothing for additional rate. Your band is worked out with your interest added to your other income.

So a retired person with a £15,000 pension keeps £2,570 of the starting rate, plus the £1,000 allowance. That is £3,570 of interest tax free, our own arithmetic using GOV.UK's method. HMRC's paper says a basic rate taxpayer using both in full would need over £150,000 at 4%, outside an ISA, before paying any tax.

The people who will pay are mostly higher rate taxpayers. Their allowance is only £500, and from April 2027 every pound above it costs 44p.

What does £500 of interest look like? At the Bank of England's average instant access rate of 2.08% in October 2026, it is the interest on about £24,000. At a better easy access rate of 3.75%, it is the interest on about £13,300. Both are our own arithmetic.

That gap is the real story. Many people earn far less than the best rates, and the rate difference is often worth more than the tax rise. Moving £20,000 from 2.08% to 3.75% adds £334 a year, before any tax. Our savings interest tax guide explains how HMRC collects any tax that is due.

3. Is the cash ISA still being cut to £12,000?

No. This is the change most people missed. The Budget 2026 overview of tax legislation says the government will keep the cash ISA limit at £20,000 for all savers over 18 from April 2027.

That reverses the cut announced at Budget 2025, which would have limited cash ISAs to £12,000 a year for under 65s. HMRC's September 2026 policy paper describing that cut is still on GOV.UK, so you may come across it. The later Budget document is the one that says £20,000. If you read our earlier guide to the cash ISA cut, the £12,000 limit is no longer the plan. The overall ISA limit is £20,000 in both 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028.

This makes the ISA the cleanest answer to the tax rise. GOV.UK says you pay no tax on ISA interest, and you do not need to declare it on a tax return. The allowance belongs to each tax year, which runs from 6 April to 5 April.

Do ISAs pay less? Not by much right now. The Bank of England's quoted rates for October 2026 put a two year fixed cash ISA at 4.60%, against 4.69% for an ordinary two year fixed bond. NS&I, as one named example, pays 3.80% on its Direct ISA and 3.75% on its taxable Direct Saver, both variable.

For a higher rate taxpayer from April 2027, 3.75% taxed at 44% leaves 2.10% once the allowance is used up. The 3.80% in the ISA stays 3.80%. That is our own arithmetic on NS&I's published rates.

4. Worked example: the Clarkes in Bristol

Helen and Mark, one higher rate, one basic rate

Helen earns £58,000, so she is a higher rate taxpayer. She has £40,000 in an easy access account paying 3.75%, which earns her £1,500 a year. None of it is in an ISA.

Mark earns £26,000. He has £6,000 in an account paying the October 2026 average of 2.08%, which earns £124.80.

All the sums below are our own arithmetic, assuming rates stay where they are.

If they do nothing. Helen's allowance is £500, so £1,000 of her interest is taxed. This year that costs £400 at 40%. From 2027 to 2028 it costs £440 at 44%. Mark pays nothing, because £124.80 is inside his £1,000 allowance.

Move one, before 5 April 2027. Helen puts £20,000 into a cash ISA using this year's allowance. At 3.80% it earns £760 tax free. Her remaining £20,000 earns £750, and only £250 is above her allowance. In 2027 to 2028 that costs £110.

Move two, on or after 6 April 2027. She uses next year's fresh £20,000 allowance for the rest. Now all £40,000 earns £1,520 a year, with no tax at all.

Doing nothing leaves her £1,060 after tax in 2027 to 2028. Doing both moves leaves £1,520 in a full year. That is £460 a year more.

Mark's own move. Switching his £6,000 from 2.08% to 3.75% adds £100.20 a year. The tax rise does not touch him, but the poor rate does.

There was a second route for Helen's other £20,000. She could give it to Mark. At 3.75% his interest would then be £975 a year in total, still inside his £1,000 allowance. But the money becomes his, legally and fully. Only do that if you are both happy with it.

5. Premium Bonds, spouses, pensions and fixed bonds

Premium Bonds. NS&I's prize fund rate is 4.35%, variable, with odds of 21,000 to 1 for each £1 Bond in each monthly draw. Prizes are free of Income Tax, and you can hold up to £50,000.

For a higher rate taxpayer from April 2027, a taxable account would need to pay 7.77% to match 4.35% tax free. For a basic rate taxpayer it is 5.72%. Both are our own arithmetic. But the prize fund rate is an average across all Bonds, not a promise. Your own Bonds can win less, or nothing, in any year. Our Premium Bonds versus savings guide goes through that risk.

Spouses and joint accounts. Each person has their own allowances. GOV.UK says HMRC splits interest on a joint account equally between the holders. Moving savings to the partner who pays less tax uses both sets of allowances, as in the Clarkes' example.

Pensions. Workplace pension contributions taken from pay before tax lower your taxable income. If that brings your income, with interest added, below the higher rate threshold, your allowance doubles from £500 to £1,000. Pension money is locked away until pension age, so this only suits money you will not need. Our salary sacrifice guide shows how the numbers work.

Fixed rate bonds. Timing matters here. HMRC's manual says interest arises when it is paid or credited to an account you can draw on. If a bond only lets you reach the interest at maturity, all of it is taxed in the year it matures.

So a two year bond that pays everything at maturity in 2028 lands its whole interest in a year taxed at 24% or 44%. A bond that pays interest each year, or one that matures before 6 April 2027, puts more interest into the cheaper year. Check the payment option before you fix.

6. What should I do before April 2027?

  1. Add up your interest for this tax year. Look at every account outside an ISA. If the total sits under your allowance, the rate rise means nothing to you.
  2. Fix the rate first. If you are earning around 2%, switching to a better easy access rate usually beats any tax move.
  3. Use this year's ISA allowance by 5 April 2027. The £20,000 for 2026 to 2027 belongs to the tax year that ends on 5 April 2027. Then use the new one from 6 April 2027.
  4. Check how your fixed bonds pay interest. Annual interest spreads the tax. Interest at maturity piles it into one year.
  5. Talk to your partner. If one of you is a higher rate taxpayer, holding savings in the other name can use a full £1,000 allowance.

Bank Rate rose to 4% at the Bank of England's November 2026 decision, so savings rates may move again. Our Bank Rate guide covers what usually happens to savings rates when it moves.

7. The honest verdict

For most households, the savings tax rise is not worth losing sleep over. If your interest stays under £1,000 a year, or £500 as a higher rate taxpayer, you pay nothing before or after April 2027.

The people it does hit are higher rate taxpayers with tens of thousands outside an ISA. For them, the fix is plain: use the £20,000 ISA allowance before 5 April 2027, then again after 6 April 2027. The cash ISA cut that would have made this harder has been scrapped.

For everyone else, the bigger win is the rate. Money sitting at around 2% is costing far more than the tax rise ever will. This guide is general information, not financial advice.

Frequently asked

What is the tax on savings interest from April 2027? From 6 April 2027, interest above your allowances is taxed at 24% for basic rate, 44% for higher rate and 49% for additional rate taxpayers. Today the rates are 20%, 40% and 45%. HMRC confirmed the change in a policy paper on 29 October 2026, and the rates apply across the whole UK.

Is the Personal Savings Allowance changing in 2027? No. HMRC's Budget 2026 rates annex shows the Personal Savings Allowance at £1,000 for basic rate and £500 for higher rate taxpayers in both 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028. Additional rate taxpayers still get none. The £5,000 starting rate for savings is also unchanged in both years.

Is the cash ISA limit still being cut to £12,000? No. The Budget 2026 overview of tax legislation says the cash ISA limit stays at £20,000 for all savers over 18 from April 2027. The plan to cut it to £12,000 for under 65s has been dropped. The overall ISA limit also stays at £20,000 a year.

Do I need to tell HMRC about savings interest? Usually not. GOV.UK says banks and building societies report the interest they pay to HMRC after 5 April each year. If tax is due, HMRC normally sends you a calculation or collects it through your tax code. Interest inside an ISA does not need declaring at all.
