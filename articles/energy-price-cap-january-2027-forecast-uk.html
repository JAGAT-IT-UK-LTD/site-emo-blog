£1,872 in January: Cornwall Insight's Price Cap Forecast, and the Two Things That Number Hides

On the morning Ofgem confirmed the October cap at £1,723, the consultancy whose forecasts the industry actually uses published its estimate for the quarter after: £1,872 for January to March 2027, a further 9%. That number is a forecast, not a cap, and Ofgem will not confirm the real one until late November. But it carries two quiet assumptions that change what it means for your house. Here they are, with the arithmetic, and the three things worth doing in the eight weeks before the confirmation.

The short version

  • The forecast is £1,872 a year for January to March 2027, up 9% or £149 on the confirmed October cap of £1,723. Source: Cornwall Insight press release, 26 August 2026. It is a forecast by a named consultancy, not an Ofgem figure.
  • Ofgem confirms the January cap in late November 2026. Cornwall Insight itself says there is "a lot of time for wholesale market conditions to shift"; wholesale energy is over 40% of the cap and has been driven by the conflict in the Middle East.
  • Hidden thing one: the yardstick shrank in July 2026. £1,872 is for a typical household now defined as roughly 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas. On the old 2023 definition (2,700 kWh and 11,500 kWh) the same rates come to about £2,100 by our arithmetic.
  • Hidden thing two: electricity carries 0% VAT from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. The January cap includes that zero rate. If 5% VAT returns on 1 April 2027 as currently scheduled, the April cap rises by that much on the electricity half before wholesale prices move at all.
  • What to do before November: compare any fixed deal against £1,872 not £1,723; take a meter reading on 31 December; check Warm Home Discount eligibility, because the £150 credit lands on winter bills.
  • Our verdict: treat £1,872 as the planning number and expect a real 2023-style household to be nearer £2,100. A fix that beats the forecast by 5% or more is worth taking; one that only beats today's cap is not.

1. What Cornwall Insight actually forecast

Cornwall Insight's press release of 26 August 2026 says bills are "currently forecast to rise a further 9% in the new year", which "would put an average January bill up to £1,872 a year, £149 higher than the £1,723 October price cap announced today". It adds, in the same paragraph, that "the January figure will not be confirmed until November, and there remains a lot of time for wholesale market conditions to shift", and that wholesale energy prices "currently make up over 40% of the cap". Its principal consultant, Dr Craig Lowrey, puts the cause outside Britain: European gas markets reacting to the conflict in the Middle East.

So three things are certain and one is not. The October cap of £1,723 is confirmed and runs to 31 December; we set out its unit rates in our October price cap guide. The January cap will be set by Ofgem in late November on wholesale prices between now and then. Cornwall Insight's forecasts have tracked Ofgem's confirmed figures closely at recent announcements, which is why it is the number to plan on. And the 9% could still move either way.

2. Hidden thing one: the smaller typical household

In July 2026 Ofgem cut its definition of a typical dual fuel household to roughly 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year, down from 2,700 kWh and 11,500 kWh, because people are using less. Every headline cap figure since, including £1,723 and £1,872, is priced on the smaller household. Ofgem's own notes for October put the identical rates at £1,935 on the old yardstick, 12.3% higher than £1,723.

Apply the same ratio to the January forecast and a household still using the 2023 amounts is looking at about £2,100 a year, by our arithmetic, not £1,872. That is not a criticism of the forecast; it is priced correctly for the new typical household. It is a warning about comparing it with the numbers you remember from 2023 and 2024, which were on the bigger yardstick, and about comparing a fixed-tariff quote, which will be worked out on your actual usage, with a cap headline that is not.

3. Hidden thing two: zero VAT that ends on 31 March

VAT on domestic electricity is zero from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, and gas stays at 5%. The October cap was the first to include the zero rate, which is why the electricity unit rate looked almost flat at 26.32p while the pre-tax price rose 5.8%; our VAT cut guide has the full working. The January cap will include the zero rate too, so £1,872 is an after-VAT-cut number. If the 5% returns on 1 April 2027 as currently scheduled, roughly £40 a year goes back onto a typical electricity bill from April before anything else changes. The Budget on 28 October is the obvious moment for a decision on extending or ending the zero rate, and it will land a month before Ofgem's January announcement.

4. Three things to do before Ofgem confirms

Compare fixes against £1,872, not £1,723. A fixed deal is a bet on the next 12 months, and for at least three of those months the cap is forecast at £1,872 on the standard household. A fix priced 5% or more below that forecast, on your own usage, is worth taking; a fix that merely undercuts today's cap by a few pounds is not, because it locks you in through any fall. Check exit fees, and remember the cap changes again on 1 April.

Take a meter reading on 31 December. Unit rates change on 1 January. Without a reading the supplier estimates how much of your usage fell in each period, and in a cold week that estimate can cost real money. A smart meter does it for you.

Check the Warm Home Discount. The £150 credit is applied to winter bills and the eligibility rules widened for 2026. Our Warm Home Discount guide covers who gets it automatically and who needs to act. If you are on a prepayment meter, the capped rate is about £45 a year below direct debit, and vouchers for the discount arrive by post or app.

5. A worked example in Preston

The Okafor family in Preston live in a three-bedroom semi with gas heating and use about the old typical amounts, 2,700 kWh of electricity and 11,500 kWh of gas. On the October rates their bill is about £1,935 a year, roughly £161 a month by direct debit. If the January cap lands where Cornwall Insight forecasts, their January to March bill runs at about £2,100 a year, £175 a month, so the three winter months cost them around £525 rather than £484, a difference of about £41 across the quarter, and more in a cold January because winter months carry a bigger share of the year's gas.

Their supplier offers a 12-month fix at the equivalent of £1,990 a year on their usage. Against today's cap, £1,935, that looks like a £55 loss. Against the forecast, £2,100 for at least one quarter and an April cap that carries 5% VAT back onto electricity, it looks closer to break-even with the bonus of certainty. On those numbers the fix is defensible but not compelling, and the right move is to wait for Ofgem's confirmation in late November, then decide with a real January figure and a Budget decision on VAT in hand, unless the fix is withdrawn first.

6. The honest verdict

£1,872 is Cornwall Insight's forecast for January to March 2027, 9% above the confirmed October cap, and it will be confirmed or corrected by Ofgem in late November. It is priced on Ofgem's smaller July 2026 typical household, so a house using the 2023 amounts should expect nearer £2,100, and it includes zero VAT on electricity that is scheduled to end on 31 March.

Plan on the forecast, compare any fix against it rather than against today's cap, take a reading on 31 December, and check the Warm Home Discount now. Nothing about the January figure is certain until Ofgem says it, and the Budget on 28 October may change the VAT picture first.

Frequently asked

Is £1,872 the confirmed January price cap? No. It is Cornwall Insight's forecast, published 26 August 2026. Ofgem confirms the January to March 2027 cap in late November 2026.

Why is the forecast lower than bills I remember from 2023? Because Ofgem shrank its typical household in July 2026 to about 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas. On the old 2,700 and 11,500 kWh yardstick the same rates come to about £2,100.

Does the January cap include the electricity VAT cut? Yes. VAT on domestic electricity is 0% from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027, so the January cap is priced with zero VAT on electricity. Gas remains at 5%.

Should I fix now? Only if a fix on your actual usage is around 5% or more below the £1,872 forecast equivalent, with low or no exit fees. A fix that only beats today's £1,723 cap is not worth locking into.
