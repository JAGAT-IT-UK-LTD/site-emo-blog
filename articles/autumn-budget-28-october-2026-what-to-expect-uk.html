Six weeks to the Budget: what is actually confirmed, and what is still just noise

The short version

  • The date is confirmed. The Budget is Wednesday 28 October 2026, announced by the Chancellor in a letter to the Treasury Select Committee on 31 July 2026.
  • The OBR forecast lands the same day. The Office for Budget Responsibility has been asked to publish an economic and fiscal forecast on 28 October to accompany the Budget.
  • Most tax changes would not start on 28 October. Income tax changes normally take effect from the start of the next tax year, 6 April 2027, not on Budget day.

  • Nothing about rates or thresholds is policy until it is announced. Everything circulating now is briefing, modelling or guesswork.

1. What has actually been confirmed

Two things, and only two things, are settled.

First, the date. The Chancellor wrote to the Chair of the Treasury Select Committee on Friday 31 July 2026 to confirm that the Budget will be held on Wednesday 28 October 2026. That letter is published on GOV.UK and is linked at the bottom of this page. It is the primary source. Any date you see that is not 28 October is wrong.

Second, the forecast. The same letter confirms the Office for Budget Responsibility has been commissioned to prepare an economic and fiscal forecast for publication on 28 October, to accompany the Budget. That matters more than it sounds. The OBR forecast is the scorecard the Chancellor has to work against, and it is usually the reason a Budget contains what it contains.

That is the confirmed list. It is short on purpose.

2. What the Budget does, and does not do, on the day

A Budget is an announcement, not a switch. On 28 October the Chancellor stands up and says what the government intends to do. Very little of it takes effect that afternoon.

The general pattern in the UK is this. Duties on things like fuel, alcohol and tobacco can change at or near midnight on Budget day, because they are collected at the point of sale and can be moved quickly. Income tax rates, allowances and thresholds normally change at the start of the next tax year, which is 6 April 2027. National Insurance thresholds follow the same tax year rhythm. Anything requiring new legislation waits for the Finance Bill to pass, which takes months.

So if you read that a Budget measure will "cost you £400 a year", check when it is supposed to start. A change announced on 28 October 2026 that begins on 6 April 2027 costs you nothing in this tax year.

3. The things people think are decided at the Budget but are not

This is the part that causes the most confusion, and it is worth being blunt about.

Benefit rates are not set at the Budget. Working age benefit uprating runs off the Consumer Prices Index figure for September, which the ONS publishes on 21 October 2026 at 7am, a week before the Budget. That number, not the Chancellor's speech, is what drives the April 2027 rates for most working age benefits. We have written that up separately.

The state pension triple lock is not decided at the Budget either. It runs off average weekly earnings growth, CPI and the 2.5% floor, whichever is highest, using data published on its own timetable.

The Bank Rate has nothing to do with the Budget. The Monetary Policy Committee is independent. Its next decision is Thursday 5 November 2026, eight days after the Budget, and it is made by the Bank of England, not the Treasury.

Making Tax Digital dates are already legislated. The April 2027 and April 2028 thresholds are set out in published HMRC policy papers. A Budget could change them, but as things stand they do not need a Budget to happen.

4. Why so much speculation circulates, and how to read it

Between the date announcement and Budget day there is a three month gap, and a lot of people are paid to fill it. Think tanks publish costed options. Newspapers report what somebody in the Treasury is said to be considering. Firms that sell financial products publish "act before the Budget" guides, because urgency sells.

None of that is policy. A useful test: ask who would gain if you believed it. If a piece tells you to move money, buy a product or lock something in before 28 October, and the source of the claim is "reports suggest", treat it as marketing until the Chancellor says otherwise.

The honest position six weeks out is that nobody outside a small Treasury circle knows what is in the Budget, and the people who do know are not briefing the internet.

5. A worked example: what a household can sensibly do before 28 October

Priya and Tom live in Stevenage. Between them they earn about £64,000. They have read four separate articles this month telling them to do something before the Budget, and they are anxious.

Here is the arithmetic that actually applies to them right now, none of which depends on the Budget.

  • Their energy bill. They are on a standard variable tariff, so the price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 applies to them. It is £1,723 a year for typical dual fuel use paying by direct debit, up £60 a year from £1,663. That is already announced and already certain.
  • Their savings. Bank Rate is 3.75% and was held there on 17 September 2026. The next decision is 5 November. Moving cash from an account paying well under the base rate to one paying near it is worth doing regardless of what the Chancellor says.
  • Tom does some freelance work on the side. If his qualifying income is over £30,000 he will be pulled into Making Tax Digital from 6 April 2027. That is already published policy and worth preparing for.

What they should not do is make an irreversible decision, such as crystallising a pension or selling an asset, on the strength of a rumour about a tax that does not exist yet. If a rumoured change does arrive on 28 October, it will almost certainly start on 6 April 2027, which leaves over five months to respond.

The sensible pre-Budget move for most households is not a move at all. It is tidying up the things that are already certain, and reading the actual Budget documents on 28 October rather than the preview coverage before it.

Frequently asked

Is the Budget definitely on 28 October 2026? Yes. The Chancellor confirmed it in a letter to the Treasury Select Committee published on GOV.UK, and the OBR has been commissioned to publish its forecast the same day.

Will income tax go up on Budget day? Nobody outside the Treasury knows what is in the Budget. Even if income tax changes are announced, they would normally take effect from the start of the next tax year on 6 April 2027, not on 28 October.

Does the Budget set my benefit rates for next April? No. Working age benefit uprating follows the September CPI figure the ONS publishes on 21 October 2026, a week before the Budget.

Should I move money before the Budget? There is no confirmed change to move money ahead of. Doing something irreversible on the strength of a rumour is a risk you are taking for no confirmed benefit.
