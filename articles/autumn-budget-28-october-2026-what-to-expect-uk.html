Autumn Budget 2026: what was announced on 28 October, and what it means for you

The Autumn Budget 2026 went ahead on Wednesday 28 October. Tax on wages stayed the same, tax on savings and rental income goes up from April 2027, and there is a £100 energy payment for millions of homes. Here is what changed, and when each change starts.

The short version

  • It happened on the day. Chancellor John Healey presented Budget 2026 on Wednesday 28 October 2026, and the OBR published its forecast the same afternoon.
  • Tax on wages did not go up. Income tax stays at 20%, 40% and 45%, the personal allowance stays £12,570 in 2027 to 2028, and employee National Insurance stays at 8% and 2%.
  • Tax on savings and rental income did go up. From 6 April 2027 savings interest and property income are taxed at 24%, 44% and 49%, up from 20%, 40% and 45% now.
  • Help with bills. A £100 Household Energy Payment from January 2027 for homes in council tax bands A to D in England, 0% VAT on home electricity to 1 July 2027, and the 5p fuel duty cut kept to 1 July 2027.
  • The cash ISA limit stays at £20,000. The planned cut is not going ahead.
  • Benefits were set the same day, separately. The full new State Pension rises 3.9% and most other benefits 3.5%, mainly from 12 April 2027.

1. Did the Budget happen on 28 October?

Yes. When we first wrote this guide in September, only two things were certain: the date and the forecast. Both happened as planned.

Chancellor John Healey presented Budget 2026 to Parliament on Wednesday 28 October 2026. It was his first Budget. The full Budget document, the speech and the tax papers are all on GOV.UK, and we have linked each one at the bottom of this page.

The Office for Budget Responsibility published its Economic and fiscal outlook, October 2026 the same day. It now expects growth of 1.2% in 2026 and 1.0% in 2027. It expects inflation to peak at 3.8% in the first quarter of 2027, then return to the 2% target by the third quarter of 2028.

That inflation outlook explains a lot of what follows. Most of this Budget is about bills, not big tax rises.

2. Did income tax or National Insurance go up?

Not on wages. The Budget document says there is no increase to the headline rates of income tax, National Insurance or VAT. Before the day, this was the question most readers asked us, so here is the plain answer.

  • Income tax rates stay at 20% basic, 40% higher and 45% additional in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • The personal allowance stays at £12,570 in 2027 to 2028. The higher rate still starts above £50,270, and the additional rate above £125,140.
  • Employee National Insurance stays at 8% on earnings between £242 and £967 a week, and 2% above that.
  • Dividend tax stays at 10.75%, 35.75% and 39.35% in 2027 to 2028.
  • Capital Gains Tax stays at 18% and 24%, with a £3,000 tax free allowance.
  • Inheritance Tax bands stay at £325,000, plus £175,000 for a home passed to children or grandchildren.

Thresholds that do not move are still a quiet tax rise when wages grow. We explain how that works in what frozen tax thresholds really cost you.

There is one new income tax break for older people. From 6 April 2027 to 5 April 2030, a Pension Tax Allowance means people over State Pension age whose total income is at or below the full new State Pension pay no income tax. Just above that level, the allowance tapers away.

3. Savings, ISAs and property income

This is where the Budget raised money from households. The tax rates on savings interest and on property income, such as rent, rise by a further 2 percentage points on top of the 2 points announced in Budget 2025.

From 6 April 2027, savings income above your tax free allowances will be taxed at 24%, 44% and 49%. Today it is 20%, 40% and 45%. Property income gets its own rates of 24%, 44% and 49% from the same date. The Treasury says 84% of income tax payers will not be affected.

The Personal Savings Allowance does not change. Basic rate taxpayers can still earn £1,000 of interest tax free, and higher rate taxpayers £500. Our guide to how tax on savings interest works sets out who actually pays.

The good news for savers is the ISA. The cash ISA limit stays at £20,000 from 6 April 2027. The cut that had been planned is not going ahead. If you were weighing up the changes, see our cash ISA 2027 guide.

For first time buyers, two changes. From 29 October 2026, Lifetime ISA savers buying a first home above the £450,000 price cap pay a 20% withdrawal charge instead of 25%. From April 2029, a new First Time Buyer ISA replaces the Lifetime ISA for new savers, with up to £4,000 a year and a 25% government bonus.

4. Energy bills, fuel and driving

Most of the help in this Budget is aimed at bills.

  • £100 Household Energy Payment. Up to 20 million homes in council tax bands A to D in England get a £100 payment from January 2027, paid through the council tax system. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland get funding to offer similar help.
  • 0% VAT on home electricity is extended to 1 July 2027. The Treasury expects this to take around £50 off the annual Ofgem price cap in April 2027.
  • Gas standing charges. The government has asked Ofgem to abolish them, aiming for the end of 2027. The cost moves into the price per unit instead.
  • Fuel duty. The plan to add 5p a litre back in January 2027 is cancelled. The 5p cut now runs to 1 July 2027, and the planned inflation rise is deferred to the same date.
  • Mileage rates. The 55p a mile tax free rate for the first 10,000 business miles is now permanent, and the rate above 10,000 miles rises from 25p to 35p from 6 April 2027.

If you are on a standard variable tariff, the next price cap matters more than any single Budget measure. We track it in the January 2027 price cap forecast.

5. Wages, benefits and the State Pension

The National Living Wage rises 3.9% to £13.21 an hour from 1 April 2027. The rate for 18 to 20 year olds rises to £11.27, and for 16 and 17 year olds and apprentices to £8.31. More detail is in our National Living Wage April 2027 guide.

Benefit rates were not set in the Budget speech. They were set by a separate statement from the Work and Pensions Secretary, Pat McFadden, on the same day.

  • The full new and basic State Pension rise by 3.9%, in line with earnings growth, mainly from 12 April 2027. DWP says that is around £490 a year more on the full new State Pension.
  • Most other benefits, including disability benefits, Statutory Sick Pay and Statutory Maternity Pay, rise by 3.5%, the September 2026 CPI figure.
  • The Universal Credit standard allowance rises by more than inflation, under the Universal Credit Act 2025.
  • The benefit cap and Local Housing Allowance rates stay frozen for 2027 to 2028.

How the September number feeds into those rates is in our guide to the September CPI figure. For pensioners, the State Pension April 2027 guide covers the triple lock.

The triple lock itself is changing, but not yet. It stays as it is through April 2029. From April 2030, the earnings part is replaced by whatever rise keeps the State Pension at the same value relative to earnings as in April 2029.

7. A worked example: one Stevenage household

Priya and Tom live in Stevenage. Between them they earn about £64,000, so both are basic rate taxpayers. Before the Budget they read four articles telling them to act fast. They did nothing, which turned out to be right.

Here is what the Budget actually does to them. The household and its numbers are an illustration. The tax rates are the official ones.

  • Tom's weekend work. He earns £2,400 a year from freelance jobs with almost no costs. In 2026 to 2027 he can take off the £1,000 trading allowance, leaving £1,400 taxed at 20%, which is £280. From 6 April 2027 the allowance is £3,000, so the whole £2,400 is tax free. He saves £280 a year. Our side hustle tax guide explains the allowance.
  • Priya's savings interest. She earns £1,600 a year in interest outside an ISA. The first £1,000 is tax free. On the other £600 she pays 20% now, which is £120. From 6 April 2027 she pays 24%, which is £144. That is £24 a year more. Moving the money into a cash ISA, still allowed up to £20,000 a year, would make all of that interest tax free.
  • Their energy bill. Their home is in council tax band C in England, so they should receive the £100 Household Energy Payment from January 2027.

Add it up. They gain £280 from the trading allowance and £100 from the energy payment, and lose £24 on savings tax. That is £280 plus £100 minus £24, so £356 better off over the year, before they move a penny into an ISA.

The lesson from before the Budget still holds. The changes that affect most people start on 6 April 2027, so there is time to read the real documents and then act.

Frequently asked

Did income tax go up in the Autumn Budget 2026? Not on wages. The basic, higher and additional rates stay at 20%, 40% and 45%, and the personal allowance stays at £12,570 in 2027 to 2028. What did go up is the tax on savings interest and property income, to 24%, 44% and 49% from 6 April 2027.

When do the new savings tax rates start? On 6 April 2027, the start of the 2027 to 2028 tax year. Until then, savings interest above your allowances is taxed at 20%, 40% or 45%. The £1,000 and £500 Personal Savings Allowances do not change, and interest inside an ISA stays tax free.

Who gets the £100 Household Energy Payment? Households in England whose home is in council tax bands A to D, up to 20 million homes, from January 2027. It is paid through the council tax system. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland receive funding to provide similar support, so the details there depend on each devolved government.

How much will the State Pension rise in April 2027? The full new and basic State Pension rise by 3.9%, in line with earnings growth, mainly from 12 April 2027. DWP puts that at around £490 a year more on the full new State Pension and around £375 on the full basic State Pension. Most other benefits rise by 3.5%.

Is the cash ISA allowance still £20,000? Yes. The Budget confirmed the cash ISA limit stays at £20,000 a year from 6 April 2027, and the cut that had been planned will not happen. The overall ISA limit is also £20,000, and the Junior ISA limit stays at £9,000.
