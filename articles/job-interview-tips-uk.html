Job Interview Tips UK 2026: How to Prepare in 90 Minutes

With 2.4 unemployed people for every vacancy, getting the interview was the hard part. This is how you stop the most common, most preventable rejections.

1. How hard is it to get a job interview in the UK right now?

Hard, and getting harder. The ONS estimates 690,000 vacancies for August to October 2026, down 22,000 on the quarter before.

Over July to September 2026 there were 2.4 unemployed people for every vacancy. That is before you count people who already have a job and want a better one.

At graduate level the squeeze is sharper. The Institute of Student Employers found an average of 140 applications per graduate vacancy in 2025, up 14% on the year.

Two things follow. First, if you have an interview, you are not there by accident. Stop treating it as a plea and start treating it as a conversation between two parties who might suit each other.

Second, the process is slow because volume is high. Many employers run two or three rounds. A week or two of silence after a first round is ordinary, not a signal.

2. What happens in the first fifteen minutes?

Interviewers form a view early. That is not fair, but it is human, and you can prepare for it.

The first quarter of an hour is almost always taken by three things: how you arrive and settle, "tell me about yourself", and "why this role". So you can rehearse the exact material that carries the most weight.

Tell me about yourself. This is not an invitation to narrate your life. Ninety seconds, three parts: where you are now, one concrete thing you have done that matches this job, and why you are sitting here.

Write it out. Say it aloud six times. Then stop rehearsing, so it does not sound recited.

Why this role. The weak answer is about you: "I am looking for a new challenge." The strong answer links something specific about them to something specific about you.

That needs research, which is the next section and the one that fails most often.

You cannot control how early they make up their mind. You can control that those minutes hold your best material, not your warm up.

3. How do I research a company before an interview?

Every serious UK careers source puts this first. The National Careers Service, Reed and university careers teams all tell you to read the job description closely and research the employer.

It is also the easiest failure to prevent. Thirty minutes the night before is enough. Here is what to find, in order of usefulness.

  • What they sell and to whom. Say it in one plain sentence, without the marketing words from their homepage.
  • Something that changed recently. A new product, a new site, a funding round, a takeover, or a change in the law that affects them. Their news page and Companies House filings take ten minutes.
  • Who you would be working for. Look up the interviewer on LinkedIn. Not to flatter them, but so you know whether you face a specialist or a generalist.
  • Their competitors. Naming one, and saying what makes this company different, puts you ahead of most of the pile.
  • The job advert itself. Read it three times and mark the words that repeat. Those are the things they care about, and they will be tested.

4. What is the STAR method, and how many answers do I need?

Competency questions, the "tell me about a time when" ones, are best answered with STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. The National Careers Service recommends it by name.

It works because it forces you to finish with an outcome, which is the part most people skip.

  • Situation. One sentence of context. Where, when, what was going on.
  • Task. What was your job in it. Not the team's, yours.
  • Action. What you did, step by step. This should be the longest part.
  • Result. What happened, with a number wherever one exists, and what you would do differently.

The efficient way to prepare. Do not write twenty answers. Write three strong stories and learn them well.

One where you solved a hard problem. One where you handled a difficult person. One where something went wrong and you dealt with it. Between them, they cover most competency questions. You reshape the same story to fit the question you get.

Some employers now use software to screen recorded video interviews. Whether or not you like that, a clearly structured STAR answer comes across better than a rambling one, to a person or to a machine.

5. What questions should I ask at the end?

Most people treat the end of the interview as the bit after the assessment. It is part of the assessment.

The National Careers Service advises writing your questions down beforehand. Prepare five, because two or three will get answered during the conversation.

  • What does good look like in this role after six months?
  • What is the biggest challenge facing the team right now?
  • Why is this role open? Growth, replacement or restructure. The answer tells you a lot.
  • How is performance reviewed here, and how often?
  • What would you change about how the team works, if you could?

Do not ask anything their website already answers. And do not lead with holiday, hours or working from home in a first interview. Those are fair questions. They belong at offer stage, when you have leverage.

6. How do I answer salary questions?

If asked what you want before you have an offer, you have two decent options.

Give a researched range with a reason: "Based on similar roles in this area I would expect between £32,000 and £36,000, though I am more interested in the fit." Or redirect once: "I would rather understand the role first, but what range has been budgeted?"

Can they ask what you earn now? Yes. No UK law currently bars an employer from asking about your salary history. You are not obliged to answer, and you can say you would rather talk about the value of this role.

What is changing. On 5 November 2026 the government announced a "say what you pay" law that will make employers publish pay in job adverts. Legislation is promised as soon as possible, with details to follow. Until then, it is a plan, not a right.

The real negotiation comes after "we would like to offer you the role", not before. Our guides to salary bands and pay transparency and asking for a pay rise cover what to say then.

7. A worked preparation session, ninety minutes

Leila, 34, interviewing for an operations manager role in Cardiff on Thursday. The advert says £34,000 to £38,000. She has one evening.

0 to 20 minutes: the company. Their site, their news page, one Companies House filing. She learns they opened a second site in Bristol four months ago. That one fact will appear in three of her answers.

20 to 35 minutes: the advert. Read three times. The words that repeat are "process improvement", "stakeholder" and "cost control". Those are the three things that will be tested.

35 to 65 minutes: three STAR stories. One on a process she rebuilt, with the number: handling time down from 9 minutes to 5. One on a supplier dispute she settled. One on a project that overran and how she pulled it back. Bullets, not scripts.

65 to 80 minutes: the opening ninety seconds. Where she is now, the process story in one line, and why this company, which is the Bristol expansion and what it means for operations.

80 to 90 minutes: five questions and the practical bits. Route, arrival time, who to ask for, and a charged phone. She also writes one salary line: the top half of the advertised range, £36,000 to £38,000, backed by the 4 minutes a case she saved.

What ninety minutes bought her. If she gets the role at £37,000 rather than the £34,000 floor, that is £3,000 a year before tax. Spread over the evening, her preparation was worth about £2,000 an hour in the first year alone.

More to the point, her strongest material now sits in the first fifteen minutes, rather than coming out at minute forty when the interviewer has already formed a view.

8. What mistakes cost UK candidates offers?

  • Talking too much. Answer, then stop. If they want more, they will ask. Silence after your answer is their thinking time, not your cue to keep going.
  • Seeming uninterested. It is rarely real. It is usually nerves showing as flatness. Asking questions of your own is the most reliable fix.
  • Criticising a previous employer. Even when it is deserved. Say what you are moving towards, not what you are escaping.
  • No examples. "I am good at problem solving" is worth nothing. The story with a number is the evidence.
  • Not testing the video set up. The National Careers Service says check your computer and connection, and be ready 5 to 10 minutes early. Camera at eye level, light in front of you rather than behind.
  • Not asking for adjustments. If you are disabled or have a health condition, you can ask for reasonable adjustments, such as extra time or the questions in writing. Acas tells employers they must ask interviewees whether they need adjustments, and must get your permission before recording an online interview.
  • Skipping the follow up. A short thank you email the same day, mentioning one thing you discussed, costs five minutes. Most candidates do not send one.

The honest verdict

Interview preparation has poor returns beyond a point, and most people put their effort in the wrong place. Rehearsing forty answers is worse than knowing three well.

Learning the company properly, and having your best material ready for the first fifteen minutes, is where the outcome is mostly decided.

Do the ninety minutes. Company research, the repeated words in the advert, three STAR stories, a ninety second opener, five questions. That covers the most common and most preventable failures.

And when it goes badly, remember the base rate. Several rounds, weeks of waiting, and many applicants for each role. Good candidates lose to a slightly better fit all the time. That is not a verdict on you.

Frequently asked

How should I prepare for a job interview in the UK? Read the job advert three times and mark the words that repeat. Research the company, its recent news and its competitors. Prepare three STAR stories, a ninety second answer to "tell me about yourself", and five questions to ask. Then plan the route or test your video set up the day before.

What should I wear to a UK interview? Go one notch smarter than the team's normal dress. If you cannot tell, smart trousers and a plain shirt or blouse is never wrong. The simplest fix is to ask the recruiter or the person who booked the interview. They expect the question and will usually give you a straight answer.

What are the most common interview questions in the UK? Expect "tell me about yourself", "why do you want this job", "what are your strengths and weaknesses" and several "tell me about a time when" questions. The last kind is where STAR earns its keep. Three well practised stories, reshaped to fit, will answer most of them clearly and with a result.

Is it acceptable to ask why I did not get the job? Yes. The National Careers Service suggests asking for feedback if you are not offered the role. Ask once, briefly, and without arguing. Not every employer will reply, but those who do often give you one point you can fix before the next interview, and it costs nothing to ask.

How long should I wait before chasing? Employers usually tell you when to expect news. If they do not, a week after the date they gave, or about a week after the interview, is reasonable. A short, polite email is enough. Chasing after two or three days tends to work against you, because hiring with several rounds is slow.
