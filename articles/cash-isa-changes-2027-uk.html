The Cash ISA Is Being Cut by £8,000: What That Actually Means for You

Ruhul Amin · Updated 3 September 2026 · 10 min read · Rules checked September 2026

From 6 April 2027 the cash ISA allowance drops from £20,000 to £12,000 for anyone under 65. Over-65s keep the full amount. Here is precisely what is changing, the transfer restriction almost nobody has noticed, and what your options are in the seven months before it starts.

The short version

  • From 6 April 2027, under-65s can put a maximum of £12,000 a year into a cash ISA, down from £20,000.
  • The total ISA allowance stays at £20,000. The remaining £8,000 has to go into a different type of ISA, such as stocks and shares, or not be used at all.
  • If you are 65 or over you keep the full £20,000 cash allowance, and in the tax year you turn 65 you get the full amount too. The government has said this applies at least initially. For under-65s pushed out of cash, the live question becomes how much interest you can earn before tax bites outside an ISA.
  • The rule most people miss: under-65s will not be able to transfer money from a stocks and shares ISA or an Innovative Finance ISA into a cash ISA.
  • You have one tax year left at the current rules, the 2026/27 year that ends on 5 April 2027. That is seven months from September 2026.

What changed since this was first published

  • Time left corrected. An earlier version said two tax years remained. Only the current 2026/27 tax year is left before the £12,000 cash cap starts on 6 April 2027.
  • New Chancellor. John Healey replaced Rachel Reeves on 20 July 2026. The 28 October 2026 Budget is his first, and the ISA rules could be revisited there.
  • Core figures rechecked against the GOV.UK ISA reform 2027 factsheet in September 2026: £12,000 cash cap for under-65s, £20,000 total, over-65 exemption and the transfer ban all unchanged.

1. Exactly what is changing

Now, until 5 April 2027From 6 April 2027
Total ISA allowance£20,000£20,000 (unchanged)
Maximum into cash ISA, under 65£20,000£12,000
Maximum into cash ISA, 65 and over£20,000£20,000 (unchanged)
Remaining allowance for under-65sn/a£8,000, usable only in non-cash ISAs

The headline everyone hears is "the ISA allowance is being cut". It is not. The total stays at £20,000. What changes is how much of it you are allowed to hold in cash.

That distinction matters, because the policy intent is to push money out of savings accounts and into investments. Whether that is right for any individual is a completely separate question, and the answer is often no.

The allowance is not shrinking. The cash part of it is. Those are very different problems, and only one of them affects you if you were never using the full £20,000 anyway.

2. Who is affected and who is not

Not affected at all: anyone who saves less than £12,000 a year into a cash ISA. That is the overwhelming majority of UK savers. If you are putting away a few hundred pounds a month, nothing in this changes anything for you.

Affected: under-65s who use, or intend to use, more than £12,000 of cash ISA allowance in a year. Typically people with a lump sum from a house sale, an inheritance, a bonus, or a large emergency fund they want kept in cash.

Exempt: savers aged 65 and over keep the full £20,000 cash allowance. Helpfully, in the tax year you turn 65 you also get the full amount, so there is no awkward part-year split. The government has described this exemption as applying at least initially, which is not the same as forever. Savers in that age group are also the most likely to have money they have lost track of altogether, and the free service for tracing an old pension pot is worth an hour before worrying about allowances.

3. The transfer rule almost nobody has noticed

This is the detail buried in the reform, and it is the one that will catch people out.

From the same date, under-65s will not be able to transfer money from a stocks and shares ISA, or an Innovative Finance ISA, into a cash ISA. The route only runs one way.

Why it matters: today, someone can hold investments in an ISA, decide they want to de-risk before a house purchase or retirement, and move that money into a cash ISA while keeping the tax wrapper. After April 2027 an under-65 will not be able to do that. Anyone planning that kind of de-risking should first know what they actually hold, and the 31 October 2026 dashboard connection deadline is what will eventually put every pot on one screen.

Anyone planning to shift from investments to cash within an ISA in the next few years should at least be aware that the window for doing so freely is time-limited. That is information, not a recommendation, and the right move depends entirely on your circumstances.

4. The £8,000 question

If you are under 65 and want to use the whole £20,000, the remaining £8,000 has to go somewhere other than cash. Realistically there are three honest answers.

Use a stocks and shares ISA. This is what the policy is designed to encourage. Over long periods, investments have historically outperformed cash, but they can and do fall, sometimes for years. This is only sensible for money you genuinely will not need for at least five years, and it is not appropriate for an emergency fund.

Use only £12,000 and keep the rest outside an ISA. Perfectly reasonable. Remember the Personal Savings Allowance already lets many basic-rate taxpayers earn a chunk of savings interest tax free outside an ISA, so for a lot of people the tax difference is small or nil. And if part of that money is being put aside for a child, it does not touch the adult allowance at all, which is the case for the separate 9,000 pound Junior ISA allowance and whether it is worth using. Savings held outside an ISA still count when help is means tested, which is the point of the 16,000 pound capital limit that stops a Housing Benefit claim.

Put it somewhere else entirely, such as a pension, or against a mortgage or expensive debt. Clearing a debt charging 20%-plus is mathematically better than almost any savings rate, as covered in our cashback card guide.

An important caution

Nothing in this article is advice about what you personally should do. Moving money from cash into investments changes your risk, and money you might need within a few years generally does not belong in the stock market. If the sums are significant, this is exactly the situation where free guidance from MoneyHelper, or a conversation with a regulated adviser, is worth the effort. A wedding is the clearest example of money with a date on it, and the size of the sum is the thing worth settling first, because what a UK wedding actually costs once the quiet extras are counted decides whether you are saving for two years or five, and that horizon, not the ISA rules, is what tells you to stay in cash.

Worked example: three different savers

Aisha, 34, saves £300 a month. That is £3,600 a year, comfortably inside the new £12,000 cash limit. The change affects her not at all. She should ignore the headlines.

Tom, 41, has just sold a flat and wants £20,000 in cash for a purchase in two years. Under the new rules he could only shelter £12,000 in a cash ISA. The other £8,000 would either sit outside an ISA, where his Personal Savings Allowance may cover the interest anyway, or go into investments, which for a two year horizon carries real risk of being worth less when he needs it. For him, using this tax year at the current rules, before 5 April 2027, is worth knowing about.

Margaret, 67, has £20,000 to place. Nothing changes. She keeps the full cash allowance.

5. What you can do in the meantime

  1. Check whether this affects you at all. If your annual cash ISA saving is under £12,000, stop worrying and go back to comparing interest rates.
  2. Know that you have one tax year at the current rules, the one you are in now, up to 5 April 2027. Anything you want inside a cash ISA under the £20,000 rules has to be subscribed by then.
  3. If you hold investments in an ISA and expect to want them in cash before retirement, understand that the transfer route closes for under-65s. Think about the timing, and take advice if the amount is meaningful.
  4. Do not rush into investing simply because of a rule change. A policy nudge is not a reason to take risk you would not otherwise take. The right answer for money you need soon is still cash, ISA or not.
  5. Keep chasing the rate. Whatever the wrapper, an uncompetitive savings rate costs you more each year than this reform will for most people.

6. Four misunderstandings worth clearing up

"The ISA allowance is dropping to £12,000." No. The total stays £20,000. Only the cash portion is limited for under-65s.

"I will have to move my existing cash ISA money." No. The change applies to new subscriptions from 2027 onwards. Money already in a cash ISA stays there and keeps its tax-free status. Money held outside an ISA is reported in its own way, and the crypto reporting rules HMRC now applies are the sharpest example of that.

"It starts this April." No. It starts on 6 April 2027. You have the rest of the current tax year, to 5 April 2027, under existing rules.

"I am being forced to invest." No. You are free to use only £12,000, or none at all. Nobody has to use the remaining £8,000.

7. What else may change on 28 October

The Autumn Budget is confirmed for Wednesday 28 October 2026. It is the first Budget from John Healey, who became Chancellor on 20 July 2026 under Prime Minister Andy Burnham, so the direction of travel on savings and tax is less predictable than usual. Two things already announced are worth having in mind, because they affect more people than the ISA reform does. A separate £20,000 line is worth not confusing with this one: the Making Tax Digital threshold from 2028 and who it catches applies to income, not to savings. We set out the likely measures in what to expect from the Autumn Budget on 28 October 2026.

Income tax thresholds stay frozen to 2030/31. This is the quiet one. As wages rise against fixed thresholds, more income is taxed and more people cross into the 40% band without any rate ever going up. For anyone approaching that boundary, pension contributions and salary sacrifice become more valuable.

Salary sacrifice pension contributions above £2,000 a year will attract National Insurance from 2029. Distant, but relevant if salary sacrifice is a large part of how you save.

Beyond that, property taxes, pension rules and further ISA changes are all being speculated about. Speculation is not policy, and we will update this page once the Budget is actually delivered.

8. The honest verdict

For most UK savers this reform will make no practical difference, because most people do not put £12,000 a year into a cash ISA. If that is you, the sensible response is to ignore the headlines and spend the energy on getting a better interest rate instead.

If you do use large cash ISA amounts, you have one tax year at the current rules and one genuine planning point: the ability to move ISA money from investments back into cash disappears for under-65s in April 2027.

What this reform is not, is a reason to put money you need soon into the stock market. A rule change does not alter your own time horizon, and that is what should decide where the money sits.

Frequently asked

Does this apply in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland? Yes, ISA rules are set UK-wide.

What about Lifetime ISAs and Junior ISAs? They have their own separate limits and rules. The change described here concerns the cash ISA element of the main £20,000 adult allowance.

Can I still transfer between cash ISAs? Cash-to-cash ISA transfers are not what is being restricted. The restriction for under-65s is on moving from stocks and shares or Innovative Finance ISAs into cash. Always use the provider's transfer process rather than withdrawing, or you lose the tax wrapper.

Will the over-65 exemption last? The government has said it applies at least initially and has not confirmed whether it will be phased out. Treat it as current policy rather than a permanent guarantee.
