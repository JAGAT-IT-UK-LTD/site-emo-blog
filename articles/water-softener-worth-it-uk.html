A Water Softener Has No Payback. It Has A Running Cost

The short version

  • Installed cost is £800 to £2,000, and then it costs you money every year for as long as you own it. There is no year in which it goes into profit on anything we could verify.
  • Salt costs somewhere between £40 and £180 a year. Published figures from people who sell salt disagree by more than a factor of two, which is itself the most useful fact in this article.
  • Every claimed saving comes from the industry. Boiler efficiency, soap use, appliance life: we could not find one of these quantified by a body with no product to sell.
  • For a £1,400 unit to pay back in ten years it would need to save £250 a year in soap, descaler and appliance life. Look at what you actually spend on those and decide whether that is believable.
  • Buy one because you are tired of limescale, not because of a payback. That is an honest reason and it is worth real money to some households. It is just not a return on investment.

1. Do you even live in a hard water area

This is free to check and it decides everything. Every water company in England and Wales publishes the hardness of the supply to each postcode, usually as a number in parts per million of calcium carbonate or in degrees Clark, on a page you can find by searching your company's name and "water hardness". Put your own postcode in before you read any further.

Broadly, the south and east of England are hard to very hard, the west and north are soft to moderately soft, and Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland are mostly soft. If you are in a soft or moderately soft area, stop here. A softener will do almost nothing for you and the rest of this page is academic.

If you are in a hard area, the symptoms are familiar: white crust round the taps and kettle element, scum on the bath, shower screens that never look clean, and appliances that develop scale on their heating elements. Those are real and a softener genuinely fixes them. The question is what fixing them is worth, and that is where the arithmetic starts to fall apart.

2. The salt cost, and why nobody agrees on it

An ion exchange softener works by passing water through resin, then periodically flushing that resin with brine to regenerate it. So it consumes salt forever, and it consumes a little water to do the flushing.

Here is where it gets interesting. We looked for a consistent published figure for annual salt consumption and could not find one. The published numbers, all from companies selling softeners or salt, include:

  • A trade rule of thumb of one 4 kg block of salt per person per month, which for two people is 96 kg a year
  • A stated typical hard water household usage of 40 to 70 kg a year
  • A two person household figure of 15 packs of 8 kg block salt a year, which is 120 kg
  • An annual salt spend variously given as £40 to £80, £80 to £180, and about £89 for two people

Those are not small differences. The top figure is three times the bottom one. Some of the spread is genuine, because consumption depends on hardness, household size and whether the unit meters water or regenerates on a timer. But when the people who make the product cannot agree within a factor of three on the one cost that is easy to measure, you should hold their savings claims very loosely indeed.

For everything below we use £110 a year, roughly the middle of the published range, and you should substitute a quote from your own supplier.

So the payback calculation, in the usual shape: £2,900 of ten year cost divided by the annual saving gives the payback in years. If the saving is the £75 a year that the two checkable claims add up to at their most generous, the payback is 38.7 years. If it is £290 a year, it breaks even at ten. Nothing we found lets us choose between those two numbers with any confidence.

Compare that with things on this site where the saving is a measurable quantity of energy at a published unit rate: a cylinder jacket, draught proofing, a low flow shower head. Those pay back in months or a few years and you can check every step. This one cannot be checked at all.

4. The four savings claims, checked one by one

Claim one: you use less soap, shampoo and detergent. This is real physics. Hard water ions react with soap to form scum rather than lather, so you use more product to get the same result. But how much more, in pounds, for your household? The figures quoted are always manufacturer estimates. Our own view is that £30 to £50 a year is plausible for a family and close to nothing for a single person who buys supermarket own brand.

Claim two: no more descaler. Also real, and easy to sanity check yourself. Add up what you spend a year on limescale remover, kettle descaler and shower screen cleaner. For most households it is under £25.

Claim three: your boiler runs more efficiently. Here we have to be careful. Scale forms on hot surfaces, and a combi boiler's plate heat exchanger is a hot surface in contact with mains water, so scale there is a genuine problem and it is why manufacturers in hard areas often specify scale protection. But a figure for how much gas that costs you a year, from a body that does not sell softeners or scale reducers, is not something we could find. A magnetic filter and inhibitor deal with the separate problem of sludge in the heating circuit, which is not the same thing.

Claim four: your appliances last longer. Very likely true, entirely unquantified. A washing machine that lasts eleven years instead of ten is worth about £45 a year in amortised replacement cost, which would be a meaningful contribution. A washing machine that lasts ten years and three months is worth almost nothing. Nobody has published which of those it is.

The point is not that softener companies are lying. It is that this is a product sold on a return that has never been measured outside the industry, and the honest response to that is to stop pretending you are making an investment.

5. Worked example: Bethan in Colchester

Bethan lives in one of the hardest water areas in the country with her partner and two children. The kettle scales up in three weeks and the shower screen is permanently cloudy. She is quoted £1,450 supplied and fitted, with block salt at roughly £120 a year for four people.

She adds up what she actually spends on the things a softener replaces: descaler and limescale spray, about £28 a year. Extra washing powder and shampoo over what a soft water household would use, which she estimates generously at £45 a year. Total £73.

Against that she will spend £120 a year on salt. So on the checkable numbers the softener leaves her £47 a year worse off, before the £1,450 she spent to buy it.

She buys it anyway, and this article thinks she is right to. What she is actually buying is not having to descale anything ever again, a shower screen that stays clear, and hair and skin that stop feeling like they have been washed in cement. Across ten years that costs her about £2,900, which is £290 a year, or £24 a month.

The useful thing the arithmetic did was change how she shopped. Knowing the salt was the real long term cost, she chose a metered unit that regenerates on demand rather than a timer model that regenerates whether it needs to or not, and she priced block salt in bulk rather than by the pack. Those two decisions are worth more over ten years than the difference between any two units on her shortlist.

6. The honest verdict

Do not buy a water softener as a money saving measure. There is no payback and nobody has ever demonstrated one. Over ten years it costs about £2,900 all in, and the savings you can actually check come to about £75 a year.

Do buy one if you live in a genuinely hard area and limescale is making you miserable. That is a quality of life purchase, it is a perfectly good reason, and £24 a month is not a bad price for it.

If you buy, buy a metered unit, not a timer. A timer regenerates on a schedule whether the resin needs it or not, and the salt is the whole long term cost.

And check your postcode first. If your water is soft or moderately soft, this entire product category is not for you, and that check takes thirty seconds and costs nothing.

If you are buying

Two things are worth comparing across quotes and neither is the headline price: the stated salt consumption for your household size in writing, and whether the unit is metered or on a timer. Ask for both. Culligan Harvey, one of the larger UK names, offers a 90 day money back guarantee on its own site, which is a useful benchmark to hold other suppliers against, because a company confident in the product should be willing to take it back.

Frequently asked

What about electronic or magnetic descalers instead? They are far cheaper and there is no ion exchange, so no salt and no running cost, which is genuinely attractive. The evidence that they work is weaker still than the evidence for softeners, and because they do not remove hardness minerals the water remains hard in every measurable sense. If a softener's savings are unproven, a magnetic descaler's are unproven twice over. The one honest thing in their favour is that at £60 you are risking very little to find out.

Can I drink softened water? Softening exchanges calcium and magnesium for sodium, so softened water contains more sodium than the original supply. Most installers therefore leave the kitchen cold tap on the hard mains supply for drinking and cooking, and this is worth confirming is included in your quote rather than assumed.

Does it need a drain and a lot of space? Yes to both. It needs a mains connection, a drain for the regeneration discharge and usually an electrical supply, and it takes up meaningful room under a kitchen sink. If the only viable location is a garage or outbuilding, factor in frost protection and the pipe run, which can add several hundred pounds to the fit.
