Run the Kitchen Tap for Ten Seconds. That Tells You Which Boiler You Need

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

A combi is cheaper to fit and costs nothing to keep hot water ready, because it does not keep any. A system boiler and cylinder cost more and lose about 51 pounds a year standing still. Which is right for you comes down to a measurement you can take in your own kitchen in under a minute.

The short version

  • Combi fitted: 1,800 to 3,000 pounds. System boiler with an unvented cylinder: 2,600 to 4,000, sometimes more if the cylinder needs a new location.
  • A modern cylinder loses about 1.5 kWh a day standing still. At the Ofgem capped gas rate of 7.97p and 85 per cent boiler efficiency that is about 51 pounds a year, or 770 over fifteen years.
  • The test is mains flow rate. Time how long a bucket takes to fill. Below about 12 litres a minute, a combi will disappoint you and no amount of boiler output fixes it.
  • Two bathrooms used at the same time means a cylinder. A combi heats water as it passes and cannot serve two outlets properly at once.
  • A cylinder is also the future proof answer if a heat pump or solar diverter is ever on the cards, because both need somewhere to put the heat.

1. The bucket test, in under a minute

Before any installer tells you what you need, measure this yourself. Take a bucket of known volume, ten litres is ideal, put it under the kitchen cold tap, open the tap fully and time how long it takes to fill.

Ten litres in thirty seconds is 20 litres a minute, which is a good mains supply. Ten litres in sixty seconds is 10 litres a minute, which is poor. Do it in the evening if you can, because pressure often drops when the street is busy.

Why it matters: a combi boiler heats water as it flows through, so the flow rate of your incoming mains is a hard ceiling on the shower you can have. A 30kW combi cannot deliver more litres per minute than the mains delivers to it, regardless of what the brochure claims, and the brochure figure is quoted at a temperature rise that British winters do not oblige.

Below about 12 litres a minute, a combi will produce a shower most people describe as weak, and the fix is not a bigger boiler. It is a stored system, where the cylinder fills slowly overnight and then delivers fast when you want it.

Above about 18 litres a minute with good pressure, a combi will give an excellent single shower and there is much less reason to store anything.

2. What the two systems actually are

A combi is one box. It heats the radiators and, when you open a hot tap, diverts to heating water on demand. No cylinder, no loft tanks, no airing cupboard needed. It is compact, it is cheaper to fit, and it wastes nothing keeping water hot that nobody uses.

A system boiler heats a cylinder of stored hot water and the radiators. The cylinder is normally unvented, meaning it runs at mains pressure and needs no cold water tank in the loft. You get mains pressure hot water at every outlet simultaneously, limited by the size of the cylinder rather than by flow rate.

A heat only or regular boiler is the older arrangement with a cylinder plus a cold water tank and a feed and expansion tank in the loft. It is still the right answer in some older properties, particularly where mains pressure is poor, but it is rarely chosen new.

The trade is simple to state. The combi has no standing loss and no space requirement. The cylinder has both, and in exchange it gives you hot water that does not care how many taps are open.

3. The comparison table: fitted cost and running cost

The standing loss figure is worked openly. A modern factory insulated cylinder loses roughly 1.5 kWh a day, which is 547 kWh a year of heat. Divided by 0.85 for boiler efficiency that is 644 kWh of gas, and at the Ofgem capped rate of 7.97p per kWh for 1 October to 31 December 2026 that is about 51 pounds a year.

The last row is there because it is often the right answer and nobody sells it. A boiler that works, is under about twelve years old and passes a service does not need replacing, and a separate guide on this site deals with that decision properly rather than repeating it here.

4. The future proofing argument, which is real

There is one argument for a cylinder that has nothing to do with showers, and it deserves weight because it is the only part of this decision that gets harder to reverse over time.

A heat pump cannot heat water on demand the way a combi does. It heats water slowly to a lower temperature, which means it needs a cylinder. Solar panels with a diverter need somewhere to dump surplus generation, which means a cylinder. An off peak electricity tariff with a cheap overnight window is far more useful if you have somewhere to store the heat you buy cheaply, which again means a cylinder.

If you take the combi now, and in eight years you want any of those three, you are not just buying the new equipment. You are finding space for a cylinder in a house that was replumbed to not need one, and that is a considerably bigger job than fitting one while the boiler is already out.

The counter argument is equally honest: eight years is a long time, technology and grants both move, and paying 1,665 pounds today to keep an option open is real money. If you have no intention of moving away from gas and no room for a cylinder anyway, the combi is not a mistake.

One practical note. An unvented cylinder falls under Approved Document G and must be installed and annually checked by someone qualified for unvented systems specifically, which is a separate qualification from being Gas Safe registered. Ask to see both.

5. Worked example: the Ridley household in Morpeth

Joanne Ridley has a four bedroom 1990s house in Morpeth with two bathrooms, three teenagers and a 2006 heat only boiler with a vented cylinder and loft tanks. The boiler has failed.

Two quotes. A 30kW combi at 2,750 pounds, which frees up the airing cupboard and removes the loft tanks. A system boiler with a 210 litre unvented cylinder at 3,850 pounds.

She does the bucket test. Ten litres in 41 seconds, which is about 14.6 litres a minute. That is adequate rather than good, and it is above the 12 litre threshold, so a combi would work for one shower.

The problem is that it is never one shower. Two teenagers shower at the same time most school mornings, and a combi would halve the flow to both.

She takes the system boiler. It costs 1,100 pounds more to fit and about 51 pounds a year in standing loss, so roughly 1,865 pounds more over fifteen years. For a house where two showers run simultaneously five days a week, that is a straightforward purchase.

The detail worth copying: she asked both installers what size cylinder and why, and only one gave a reasoned answer based on the number of people and the peak demand rather than a rule of thumb. She used that one. An undersized cylinder in a house of five is the commonest complaint about stored systems, and it is a specification failure rather than a technology failure.

Frequently asked

Will a bigger combi give a better shower? Only up to the limit of your mains flow. Beyond that, extra kilowatts change nothing at the shower head, and you are paying for output you cannot use. This is the single most common upsell in the trade.

Can I add a cylinder to a combi later? Technically yes, and it is expensive and disruptive, because the pipework layout for a combi is not the layout a stored system wants. Decide now if you can.

Does the cylinder standing loss go to waste? Not entirely. A cylinder in the middle of the house gives its heat to the house, which in winter is heat you would otherwise be paying for anyway. In summer it is pure loss and it makes the airing cupboard warm. The 51 pounds a year is the honest annual figure, and about half of it is arguably useful for half of the year.

What about hydrogen ready boilers? Most new gas boilers are now sold as hydrogen blend ready. That is a low bar and it is not a reason to pay a premium. There is no domestic hydrogen supply and no confirmed date for one.

The honest verdict

One bathroom, mains flow above about 12 litres a minute, no plans to leave gas: fit the combi. It is 1,665 pounds cheaper over fifteen years and it loses nothing standing still.

Two bathrooms used at the same time, poor mains flow, or any real prospect of a heat pump or solar diverter: fit the system boiler and the cylinder, and accept the 51 pounds a year as the price of hot water that does not run out and of an option you can still take later.
