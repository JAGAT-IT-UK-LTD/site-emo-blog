Your Subscriber Count Is Not the Number Anyone Is Paying For

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Newsletter sponsorship is quoted as a cost per thousand, and almost everyone assumes the thousand means subscribers. It does not. Getting this one definition right changes what your list is worth, what you should be working on, and what you can honestly quote a sponsor.

The short version

  • CPM is per 1,000 unique opens per send, not per 1,000 subscribers. beehiiv's own example: with a $5 CPM you earn $5 for every 1,000 unique opens you deliver in a single send.
  • So open rate is half your rate card. Two newsletters with 10,000 subscribers and open rates of 50% and 25% are worth double and half of each other on the same CPM.
  • CPC is the other model, and it carries the risk. beehiiv's example: a $2 CPC with 100 payable clicks pays $200. It also states plainly that with CPC there is no guarantee you will be paid.
  • Money arrives slowly. Clicks are tracked for 72 hours, the report lands 96 hours after the send, and payouts go out on the 20th of each month for the previous month's ads.
  • Read the labels. On beehiiv's marketplace, Estimated earnings shows a projection only when it is $10 or more. Below that it says Unlimited earnings instead.
  • There are hard gates. Its Ad Network needs the Scale plan or above, direct sponsorships need Max or Enterprise, and payouts require a Stripe Express account, with no PayPal or Wise alternative.

1. The unit is opens, not subscribers

CPM means cost per mille, cost per thousand. The argument is entirely about what is being counted in the thousand, and the platforms are explicit about it even though the advice around them usually is not.

From beehiiv's Ad Network FAQ, updated 12 August 2026: "CPM (cost per mille) is a payment method where you earn based on the number of unique opens per send for an advertiser. For example, with a $5 CPM, you'll earn $5 for every 1,000 unique opens you deliver in a single send."

And, in case there is any doubt: "You will only receive earnings from unique opens in email sends."

The consequence is large and immediate. Your subscriber count is not what is being bought. Two newsletters, both with 10,000 subscribers:

Subscribers10,00010,000
Unique opens per send5,0002,500
Paid units at $5 CPM5 thousands2.5 thousands
Earned per send$25$12.50

Same list size, double the income. Every hour spent cleaning dead subscribers, improving subject lines and sending to people who actually want the email is worth as much as an hour spent growing the list, and often more, because dead subscribers cost you deliverability as well as contributing nothing.

There is a second implication people miss. A large list with a poor open rate is worth less than its owner thinks, and the owner is usually quoting sponsors on the wrong number. If you have been pricing on subscribers, you have either been underpaid or been overcharging, and neither is a good position.

2. Building a number you can defend

This page is not going to print a rate card, because there is no published UK newsletter sponsorship rate and anyone quoting one is quoting an average of other people's deals. What you can do instead is build a figure from things you can actually evidence.

  1. Take your unique opens per send, averaged over your last several sends rather than your best one.
  2. Divide by 1,000. That is how many paid units you deliver per send.
  3. Multiply by the CPM. On a network, the CPM is set by the advertiser and offered to you. Selling directly, it is the number you are negotiating.
  4. Sanity check against the network. If you are on a platform with an ad marketplace, the offers you are shown are real prices real advertisers are paying for audiences like yours. That is better market information than any blog post.

The honest framing for a sponsor is the same arithmetic: you are selling a specific, measurable number of opens from a described audience, not a subscriber count. It is a stronger pitch precisely because it is checkable.

3. CPM against CPC, and who carries the risk

The other model is cost per click. beehiiv's example: "if the CPC for a campaign is $2 and you include an ad in your newsletter that receives 100 payable clicks, your payout would be 100 x $2, or $200."

On the surface CPC looks better, and sometimes it is. But the same FAQ says which way the risk runs, and it does not dress it up: "With CPC, there's no guarantee that you will receive payment."

On CPM, delivering the opens is enough. On CPC, you deliver the opens, run the ad, and then find out whether your readers happened to click on somebody else's creative, which you did not write and cannot change. beehiiv also explains that payout is on payable clicks rather than raw clicks, with bot traffic, non-unique clicks and immediate bounces filtered out, and says openly that it does not publish the exact weighting.

The practical reading: CPM for predictable income, CPC only where the offer is high enough to be worth the variance. And expect the click count in your post analytics to be higher than the number you are paid on, because it is measuring something different.

4. How slowly the money actually arrives

Newsletter sponsorship has the same lag problem as every other form of media income, and it is documented.

  • 72 hours. Clicks on the ad are tracked for 72 hours after the send.
  • 96 hours. The performance report, with confirmed earnings, becomes available four days after sending. Checking before that shows nothing, which is expected rather than broken.
  • The 20th of the following month. Payouts are processed on the 20th for the previous month's ads. An ad in January pays out on 20 February.
  • Then a withdrawal. On beehiiv the money lands in a wallet and has to be manually withdrawn to your bank.

There are also access gates worth knowing before you plan around any of this. beehiiv's Ad Network requires the Scale plan or above, direct sponsorships require Max or Enterprise, and payouts require a Stripe Express account. It states that publishers in countries Stripe does not support cannot receive Ad Network earnings, and that it does not support PayPal, Wise or other processors as an alternative.

5. Two labels worth translating

The marketplace uses two phrases that mean more than they appear to.

"Estimated earnings" shows a projected payout based on your historical open and click-through rates, and beehiiv says it appears "when that projection is $10 or more".

"Unlimited earnings" appears, in beehiiv's own words, "when the projected payout is below $10".

That is worth reading twice. The more optimistic-sounding of the two labels is the one that means the projection did not reach ten dollars. It is disclosed, to the company's credit, on the same help page. But a publisher who reads "unlimited" as upside rather than as a floor disclosure is misreading their own dashboard.

The other label to handle carefully is the estimate itself, which beehiiv says "is not a guaranteed amount, but rather an estimate based on historical email performance". Treat it the way you would treat an affiliate dashboard figure, which our piece on how long a first affiliate sale actually takes covers in more detail: a forecast, not an invoice.

Worked example: Yusuf in Bradford

Yusuf has 8,000 subscribers and sends weekly. His average unique opens per send are 2,800, a 35% open rate.

What he was quoting. He had been pitching on 8,000 subscribers, which is not the number anyone pays on.

What he is actually selling. 2.8 thousands of unique opens per send. At a $5 CPM that is $14 a send, or roughly $56 a month at one ad slot a week.

The lever he had not seen. Cleaning 1,500 subscribers who have not opened anything in six months costs him nothing in revenue, because they were never opening and so were never being paid for. If removing them lifts his open rate on the remaining 6,500 to 45%, unique opens go to about 2,925. Smaller list, more income.

The second lever. Up to 3 ads per post are permitted on beehiiv, with a maximum of one carrying a logo. Going from one slot to two roughly doubles the per-send figure, at the cost of a busier email, which is a real trade against reader goodwill rather than a free win.

The timing. An ad he runs in March is reported four days later and paid on 20 April, then withdrawn from the wallet by hand. He should not plan March spending around it.

The honest verdict

The single fact worth taking from this page is the definition. CPM is paid per thousand unique opens per send, not per thousand subscribers. beehiiv states it plainly and it reframes the whole business: open rate is not a vanity metric here, it is directly half of your rate card.

So the work that raises sponsorship income is not only list growth. It is sending to people who open, removing people who do not, and being able to show a sponsor an honest average of unique opens over several sends rather than a subscriber number.

Take CPM over CPC unless the CPC offer is clearly worth the variance, because on CPC the platform says outright there is no guarantee you will be paid. Expect the money about seven weeks behind the send. And if your dashboard says "Unlimited earnings", read it as what it is: a projection under ten dollars.

Where this does not help you: these are one platform's published mechanics, and they are the clearest available, not universal. If you sell directly or use another platform, the counting method and the payment terms are things to establish in writing before you agree a rate, because the definition of the thousand is the entire deal.

Frequently asked

Am I paid on subscribers or opens? On CPM campaigns, unique opens per send. beehiiv's own example is that a $5 CPM earns $5 for every 1,000 unique opens delivered in a single send, and it says earnings come only from unique opens in email sends. Subscriber count is not the billed unit.

Is CPC better than CPM? It can pay more, and it carries the risk. beehiiv's FAQ says directly that with CPC there is no guarantee you will receive payment, whereas CPM pays once the opens are delivered. CPC also pays on payable clicks rather than raw clicks, after bot and low-quality traffic is filtered out.

How long until I get paid? Longer than you would expect. Clicks are tracked for 72 hours, the performance report appears 96 hours after the send, and payouts are processed on the 20th of each month for the previous month's ads. On beehiiv the money then sits in a wallet until you withdraw it.

What does Unlimited earnings mean on an offer? That the projected payout is below $10. beehiiv says Estimated earnings is shown when the projection is $10 or more, and Unlimited earnings when it is below that. Some of those offers do outperform the projection, but the label is a disclosure rather than an upside signal.
