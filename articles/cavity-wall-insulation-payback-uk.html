Cavity Wall Insulation Pays Back In About Ten Years Whether You Live In A Flat Or A Detached House. So Stop Doing The Sum And Check The Cavity.

Ruhul Amin · Published 5 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Every insulation article on this site divides the cost by the annual saving and tells you the payback. This one does that too, five times, and gets almost the same answer five times: between eight and a half and eleven years. That is unusual. It means the money is not what should decide it. Whether your walls have a cavity wide enough to fill, whether the house is exposed to driving rain, and whether the installer is the kind who walks away when the answer is no: those are the decision.

The short version

  • A typical cavity wall installation in Great Britain costs around £2,200 and saves around £200 a year in a semi, on Energy Saving Trust figures updated 8 May 2026 at July 2026 fuel prices. Payback 11 years.
  • The payback is flat across house types: detached 10.3 years, semi 11, mid-terrace 10, bungalow 10.6, mid-floor flat 8.6. Bigger houses cost more and save more in almost exact proportion.
  • The same Energy Saving Trust page says it will likely take five years or less to make the cost back. Its own table does not support that at current install prices. We use the table.
  • Three things kill the case regardless of the sum: a cavity narrower than 50 mm, a wall exposed to driving rain or flooding, and brickwork or pointing in poor condition. A proper survey checks all three before quoting.
  • Grants exist and can change the arithmetic completely. ECO and the Great British Insulation Scheme run now; the Warm Homes Plan promises fully funded insulation for low-income homes and low or zero-interest loans for everyone else. Check before you pay full price.
  • Insist on a 25-year CIGA or insurance-backed guarantee and a TrustMark installer. The guarantee is worth more than a £200 discount, because removing failed insulation costs more than fitting it did.

1. The Energy Saving Trust table and why every row says ten years

The Energy Saving Trust's cavity wall insulation page, updated 8 May 2026 and read again on 5 September 2026, gives one headline cost, around £2,200 for a typical home in Great Britain and around £1,000 in Northern Ireland, and an embedded table of costs and savings by house type at July 2026 fuel prices for a gas-heated home. The table is the useful part, because it prices the job for five different houses rather than one average.

The pattern in that table is the whole point of this article. A detached house costs £3,900 to insulate and saves £380 a year. A semi costs £2,200 and saves £200. A mid-terrace costs £1,100 and saves £110. Divide each cost by each saving and the answers are 10.3, 11 and 10 years. The bungalow is 10.6, the flat 8.6. The cost of the job scales with the area of external wall, and so does the heat lost through it, so the ratio barely moves.

That is why this is a different kind of decision from double glazing, where the payback is 56 years and the honest advice is to buy it for other reasons, or from draught proofing, where it is four and a half years and the advice is just do it. Cavity wall insulation sits at ten years for everyone, and a ten year payback on a job guaranteed for 25 is worth doing if, and only if, the house is suitable. The sum will not tell you whether it is.

One caution: the saving is for a gas-heated home at typical use. An all-electric home saves roughly three times as much, because electricity under the October 2026 cap is 26.32p a kWh against 7.97p for gas, and its payback drops to three or four years.

If your loft has less than 100 mm, do that first; it is cheaper and faster. If the loft is done, the Energy Saving Trust puts about a third of the heat lost from an uninsulated home through its walls, so they are next.

3. The five year claim on the same page, and why we do not use it

Read the Energy Saving Trust page closely and there is a sentence in the cost section saying the job will likely take only five years or less to make back the cost through energy savings. That sentence sits a few lines above a table that says ten to eleven years for every house type. The two cannot both be right at the same prices, and the table is the one with the arithmetic attached.

The likeliest explanation is that the sentence dates from an earlier version of the page, when install costs were lower relative to gas prices, and was not rewritten when the table was refreshed. Older editions of the same advice quoted a semi at around £1,000 to fill, which at £200 a year is indeed five years. At £2,200 it is not.

The five year figure is the one that gets copied into sales leaflets. Ten years is the honest number, and it is still a good one.

4. What decides it instead: cavity width, exposure, condition

Because the payback is the same for everyone, the decision comes down to whether your house is suitable, and the Energy Saving Trust is unusually specific about what unsuitable means. A home is a candidate for standard cavity fill if the external walls are unfilled cavity walls, the cavity is at least 50 mm wide and clear of rubble, the brickwork and pointing are in good condition, the walls are not exposed to driving rain, and the house is not at risk of flooding. Fail any one of those and the advice is not to fill.

Do you have a cavity at all? Houses built after about 1920 usually do; before 1920 they are usually solid. Look at the brick pattern: a cavity wall shows bricks laid lengthways in an even pattern, a solid wall shows an alternating pattern with some brick ends visible. Measure the wall at a door or window reveal: 260 to 350 mm suggests a cavity, less suggests solid. Rendered or clad walls need a professional to look. Some older houses have a cavity too narrow to fill, and the Energy Saving Trust says to treat those as solid walls, which is a different and much more expensive job: £12,000 to £15,000 on the same Energy Saving Trust figures, with a payback measured in decades.

Is it already filled? Anything built in the last 20 years or so almost certainly is. For older homes, check the EPC, ask building control, or have an installer drill a small inspection hole and look with a borescope. Paying to fill a filled wall is the one outcome with a payback of never.

Exposure and damp. Insulation that bridges the cavity on a wall that takes driving rain can carry water to the inner leaf. That is the damp problem cavity fill is blamed for, and it is a problem of wrong houses rather than wrong insulation. A west-facing gable on a hillside in Cumbria is a different proposition from a sheltered semi in a Hertfordshire cul-de-sac. The surveyor should say so. On a wall that exposed the outside finish matters as much as the fill, and we price the options in rendering against pebbledash, cost and upkeep compared.

Repairs first. Damaged pointing, cracked render, leaking gutters and overhangs all have to be fixed before the cavity is filled, or the insulation traps the damp those defects let in. If the quote does not mention them, the survey was not thorough enough.

Ventilation left alone. Airbricks, wall vents and trickle vents must not be blocked, and a party wall needs a cavity barrier so the beads stay on your side.

5. Grants, guarantees and what to ask the installer

The £2,200 in the table is the unsubsidised price, and the Energy Saving Trust says so. Two schemes run now: the Energy Company Obligation (ECO4) and the Great British Insulation Scheme, both administered by Ofgem and delivered through energy suppliers, both aimed at lower-income households and homes with poor EPC ratings. The Warm Homes Plan, announced with close to £15 billion behind it, promises fully funded insulation for people on low incomes and low or zero-interest loans for other homeowners; the Energy Saving Trust says it will update its guide when the detail lands, and so will we. A £2,200 job at zero interest is the same 11 year payback with no cash up front, which for most households is the difference between doing it and not.

Whatever you pay, ask for three things in writing. A TrustMark-registered installer, which is the government-endorsed quality mark. A 25-year guarantee from the Cavity Insulation Guarantee Agency or an equivalent insurance-backed guarantee, because if damp appears in year six the guarantee is what pays for the investigation and, if needed, the removal. And a written survey that states the cavity width, the exposure assessment and any repairs required, so that if the installer says yes to a house another installer would refuse, you have it on paper.

There is no Amazon product in this article because there is nothing to buy. The purchase is a surveyor's judgement and a guarantee, and neither comes in a box.

6. Worked example: Hannah in Stockport

Three-bed 1960s semi, gas central heating, loft topped up to 270 mm last winter, brick walls with an even stretcher-bond pattern and no record of fill on the EPC

Hannah's loft is done, her windows are double glazed, and the front bedroom wall is still cold to the touch in January. Two installers survey the house. Both measure a 60 mm cavity, both find the pointing sound, both note the house is sheltered by the terrace opposite. One quotes £2,300 with a CIGA guarantee; the other £1,950 with no guarantee named on the quote. She takes the first.

The sum. Energy Saving Trust saving for a semi: £200 a year at July 2026 fuel prices. £2,300 divided by £200 is 11.5 years. Over the 25-year guarantee, at today's prices, she keeps about £5,000 for a £2,300 outlay.

What would have changed it. A 45 mm cavity: no fill, treat as solid. A west-facing gable end on an exposed site: no fill. A £0 quote under the Great British Insulation Scheme because her EPC was D and her council tax band low: fill, and the payback is immediate. She checks eligibility first, is told her band is one too high, and pays.

The result she notices. The bedroom wall stops being cold, the radiator in that room comes off its maximum setting, and the house holds its temperature for an hour longer after the heating goes off. The £200 arrives as a slightly smaller direct debit; the comfort arrives the first week.

7. The honest verdict

If your house has an unfilled cavity at least 50 mm wide, sound brickwork, and is not on an exposed or flood-prone site, fill it. On the Energy Saving Trust's own figures it pays back in eight and a half to eleven years whatever the house type, is guaranteed for 25, costs nothing to run and cuts about a third of the heat you were losing through the walls. Do the loft first if it is thin, and check ECO, the Great British Insulation Scheme and the Warm Homes Plan before paying the full £2,200.

If the cavity is narrow, the wall is exposed, or the pointing is shot, do not fill it, whatever the quote says and however low. The damp bill is bigger than the saving, and a good installer will tell you that before you ask.

Frequently asked

How much does cavity wall insulation cost in the UK in 2026? The Energy Saving Trust puts a typical installation in Great Britain at around £2,200 and around £1,000 in Northern Ireland, unsubsidised. Its table by house type runs from £950 for a mid-floor flat to £3,900 for a detached house. Grants under ECO4 and the Great British Insulation Scheme can reduce that to nothing for eligible households.

How long does cavity wall insulation take to pay for itself? Dividing the Energy Saving Trust's July 2026 costs by its savings gives 10.3 years for a detached house, 11 for a semi, 10 for a mid-terrace, 10.6 for a bungalow and 8.6 for a flat. The five-year figure quoted on the same page and in many sales leaflets is not supported by the current table.

Does cavity wall insulation cause damp? Not if the house is suitable and the job is done properly. Damp problems come from filling walls that are exposed to driving rain, cavities narrower than 50 mm, or walls with defective pointing, render or gutters. A proper survey rules those out first, and a 25-year CIGA or insurance-backed guarantee pays for investigation and removal if a problem appears.

Can I insulate the cavity walls of a flat? Not on your own. The whole block has to be insulated together, so the building owner or management company has to commission it with the agreement of the other leaseholders. The payback for a mid-floor flat is the shortest in the table at 8.6 years, but it is the hardest to arrange.
