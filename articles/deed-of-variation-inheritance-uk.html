You Can Change Who Inherits After Someone Has Died. The Window Is Two Years and the Name Is Misleading

Ruhul Amin · Published 14 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026
data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg' viewBox='0 0 100 100'%3E%3Crect width='100' height='100' fill='%230fae7e'/%3E%3Ctext x='50' y='63' font-family='Segoe UI,Arial' font-size='38' font-weight='700' fill='%23ffffff' text-anchor='middle'%3ERA%3C/text%3E%3C/svg%3E

A deed of variation is the tool families use to redirect an inheritance after a death, usually to skip a generation or to fix something the will got wrong. Two things about it are widely misunderstood: it does not have to be a deed, and the two year clock runs from the death, not from probate.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The window is 2 years from the date of death. GOV.UK: "Any changes to the will must be completed within 2 years of the death." It does not run from the grant of probate.
  • Every beneficiary left worse off by the change has to agree. You cannot vary away someone else's inheritance without their consent.
  • You do not need a formal deed. GOV.UK says a letter will do, as long as it meets the conditions in HMRC's instrument of variation checklist, form IOV2.
  • It works where there is no will at all. The intestacy outcome can be varied in exactly the same way.
  • Tell HMRC within 6 months only if the variation increases the Inheritance Tax due. If the tax does not change, no copy needs to go in.
  • It is not a way to give away your own money tax-free. It redirects an inheritance, and it is not a substitute for the 7 year gifting rules.

1. What a variation actually is

When someone dies, the will decides who gets what. If there is no will, the intestacy rules decide instead. A variation is the mechanism by which the people who inherit can agree to redirect some or all of what they receive to somebody else, and have that redirection treated, for Inheritance Tax and Capital Gains Tax purposes, as though the deceased had written it that way in the first place.

That last clause is the entire point. Anyone can simply give away money they inherit. What a variation adds is that the gift is read back to the death, so it is not treated as a gift from the beneficiary, and therefore does not sit on the beneficiary's own seven year clock.

GOV.UK's own framing is unusually direct: "You can change a person's will after their death, as long as any beneficiaries left worse off by the changes agree." And it adds, in the same breath, that where there is no will, "You can make changes to the inheritance in the same way as if there's a will."

The common name for the document, deed of variation, is therefore doing two misleading jobs at once. It is not necessarily a deed, and it is not varying the will in the sense of altering the dead person's instructions. It is the living beneficiaries agreeing among themselves, with a specific tax consequence attached.

2. The conditions, straight from GOV.UK

Two years, from the death. GOV.UK: "Any changes to the will must be completed within 2 years of the death." This is the hard edge of the whole subject. It is not two years from probate, and probate in a complicated estate can take a long time, which means the useful window can be much shorter than it sounds. If a variation is being considered at all, the date of death should be written at the top of the file on day one.

Agreement from anyone left worse off. If a change reduces what a beneficiary receives, that beneficiary has to agree to it. There is no majority vote and no executor override. A beneficiary who is a minor cannot give that agreement, which is a common reason a variation becomes impossible without a court application.

No formal document required. GOV.UK: "You do not need a formal document or deed - you can write a letter as long as it meets these conditions", linking to HMRC's instrument of variation checklist, form IOV2. This is genuinely useful and almost universally unmentioned in the marketing material of firms selling deeds of variation. The checklist is the thing to satisfy, not the format.

HMRC only needs telling if the tax goes up. GOV.UK: "If the variation means there's more Inheritance Tax to pay, you must send a copy to HM Revenue and Customs within 6 months of making it." And immediately after: "You do not need to send a copy to HMRC if the variation does not change the amount of Inheritance Tax due." Two different six month and two year clocks running at once is exactly the sort of thing that goes wrong, so keep them separate in your head.

3. The four reasons people use one

GOV.UK lists the purposes, and they are worth taking one at a time because they are not equally common.

To reduce Inheritance Tax or Capital Gains Tax. The classic case is generation skipping. A beneficiary who does not need the money and whose own estate is already above the tax thresholds varies their share to their children. Because of read-back, the money never lands in the middle generation's estate at all.

To provide for someone who was left out. Wills go stale. A grandchild born after the will was written, a partner who was not a spouse, a carer. If the named beneficiaries agree, a variation fixes it without litigation, which is cheaper and quieter than a claim under the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975.

To move assets into a trust. Used where a beneficiary is vulnerable, receives means-tested benefits, or is not able to manage a lump sum. A direct inheritance can remove a benefits entitlement. A properly structured trust may not.

To clear up uncertainty. A badly drafted clause that could mean two things can be settled by agreement rather than by a court deciding at the estate's expense.

4. Worked example: Priya in Hounslow

Priya's mother died in March. The will leaves the estate equally between Priya and her brother. Priya is 58, owns her own home, has a full pension and does not need the money. Her two children are 27 and 24, both renting.

What she is considering. Varying her half share so that it passes directly to her two children instead of to her.

Why a variation rather than simply passing it on. If Priya inherits and then gives the money to her children, that is a gift from her. It sits outside her estate only if she survives seven years, and taper relief does not begin to apply at all until year three. If instead the share is varied to her children within two years of her mother's death, with the right wording, the transfer is read back to the death and is not a gift from Priya at all. Her own seven year clock is untouched.

The clock. Her mother died in March. Her deadline is therefore the same date two years later, and it does not move if probate drags. That is the diary entry that matters.

Who has to agree. Only Priya. Her brother's share is unaffected, so he is not left worse off and his consent is not required for her half. If she were trying to redirect part of his share, it would be a different conversation entirely.

Does HMRC need a copy? Only if the variation increases the Inheritance Tax due on the estate. Redirecting a share between individuals usually does not change the estate's tax, in which case no copy goes in. If in any doubt, send it, because there is no penalty for telling HMRC about something they did not need to see.

The honest caveat on this example. The read-back is not automatic. The document has to contain the right statements of intention, which is precisely what the IOV2 checklist exists to set out. This is the one part of the process where getting it professionally checked is worth the fee, because the entire tax benefit turns on wording that costs nothing to include and everything to omit.

5. What it does not do

It does not let you take someone else's inheritance. Agreement from anyone worse off is not a formality. Without it there is no variation.

It does not reopen after two years. There is no extension mechanism on the GOV.UK page. After the two year point, a beneficiary passing money on is simply making a gift, with all the ordinary consequences.

It does not undo a gift you have already made. If you have spent or given away the inheritance, there may be nothing left to vary.

It does not cover Scotland identically. Scottish succession law includes legal rights that operate differently from the English and Welsh position, so take advice specific to that jurisdiction rather than assuming the GOV.UK page applies unchanged.

And it does not make an inheritance invisible to means-testing. Deliberately depriving yourself of capital to keep a benefit or reduce a care contribution can be treated as notional capital you still hold. A variation into a trust for a vulnerable beneficiary is a recognised structure. A variation to dodge an assessment for yourself is a different thing and is likely to be seen as one.

6. The charity rate, and what it actually costs the family

The one piece of arithmetic worth doing before the two years run out is the reduced rate. GOV.UK is plain about it: the estate pays Inheritance Tax at 36% instead of 40% on some assets if 10% or more of the net value goes to charity. A variation can create that gift after the death, which is why it belongs in a guide about varying a will rather than only in a guide about writing one.

Net value here means the estate after the nil-rate band, currently £325,000, and HMRC calls it the baseline amount. Take an estate of £800,000 with no residence nil-rate band in play. The baseline is £800,000 minus £325,000, so £475,000, and 10% of that is £47,500.

Check the £800,000 row by hand. With no gift the taxable slice is £475,000 at 40%, so £190,000 of tax and £610,000 to the family. With a £47,500 gift the estate falls to £752,500, the taxable slice becomes £427,500 at 36%, so £153,900 of tax, and the family is left with £800,000 minus £47,500 minus £153,900, which is £598,600.

The last column is the number to carry away, because the proportion is identical at every estate size: giving 10% of the baseline amount costs the family 24p in every pound given. £4,200 on £17,500, £11,400 on £47,500, £21,000 on £87,500, all of them 24%.

Now the honest part, which the law firm pages tend to skate over. The family ends up worse off, not better off. The charity rate is a cheap way to give, not a way to beat the tax: the Treasury funds 76p of every pound donated and the beneficiaries fund 24p. If nobody in the family actually wants to give anything away, the arithmetic says do nothing. It only becomes the obvious move where the person who died supported a charity anyway, or where the beneficiaries were going to donate out of the inheritance in any case, in which case routing it through the variation turns a £47,500 donation that would have cost them £47,500 into one costing £11,400.

Two practical warnings. The 10% test is applied component by component across the estate, so once there is jointly held property or settled property in the mix the single calculation above gets more complicated and is worth an hour of paid advice. And where a variation increases the tax due, which this one does not, GOV.UK requires a copy to be sent to HM Revenue and Customs.

The honest verdict

A variation is one of the few genuinely useful bits of estate admin that is both free to consider and expensive to miss. Two years from the date of death, agreement from anyone left worse off, and a letter satisfying the IOV2 conditions is enough.

If you have inherited money you do not need, and you have children or grandchildren who do, this is the mechanism that moves it without starting your own seven year clock. That is worth understanding before the second anniversary of the death, not after it.

Where it does not help you: if you are the only beneficiary and you simply want to give money away, you do not need a variation, you need the ordinary gifting rules. If a beneficiary who would lose out will not agree, there is no variation to be had. And if the estate is complex or the amounts are large, the wording that triggers read-back is the part to pay somebody to check.

Frequently asked

Does it have to be called a deed of variation? No. GOV.UK states that a formal document or deed is not required and that a letter is enough provided it meets the conditions in HMRC's instrument of variation checklist, IOV2. The name is convention, not requirement. What matters is the content and the signatures.

Can a variation be made if there was no will? Yes. GOV.UK says the inheritance under the intestacy rules can be changed in the same way as if there were a will. The two year window and the agreement requirement apply identically.

What if one of the beneficiaries is a child? A person under 18 cannot give the agreement needed to be made worse off. Where a minor's entitlement would be reduced, a variation generally needs the court's approval, which turns a paperwork exercise into a legal one. Take advice before assuming it can be done.

Do I have to tell HMRC? Only if the variation increases the Inheritance Tax due, in which case a copy must reach HMRC within 6 months of the variation being made. If the tax position is unchanged, GOV.UK says no copy is needed.
