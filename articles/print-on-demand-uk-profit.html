Print on Demand UK: The Real Profit Per T-Shirt in 2026

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Updated 18 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026

Print on demand in the UK pays far less per t-shirt than most guides suggest. On a £22 shirt sold on Etsy with £3.69 postage, a Printful seller keeps about £9.60 before tax, and as little as £2.46 once VAT on the print bill and an Offsite Ads fee come out. The money is in personalised and niche products, not generic tees.

That matters because the business is sold as risk free. It has no stock risk, which is real. It also has thin margins, and the sums below show exactly where your money goes.

The short version
  • A £22 printed t-shirt on Etsy leaves a UK seller about £9.60 after Printful's £8.92 base and £3.69 postage plus Etsy's fees. If Printful adds 20% VAT you cannot reclaim, it is £7.08. An Offsite Ads sale takes it to about £2.46.
  • Etsy charges a 6.5% transaction fee on the item and postage, plus 4% + £0.20 payment processing, plus VAT on those fees for UK sellers who have not added a VAT number.
  • The profit is not in t-shirts. It is in higher margin, lower competition products and in a design people search for by name.
  • You keep no inventory and no stock risk, which is the genuine advantage. You also keep a small margin, which is the trade.
  • Use a UK or EU print partner. Printing overseas means slow delivery and possible import charges landing on your customer.
  • Copyright and trademark infringement is the fastest way to lose a shop, and "it was on a free design site" is not a defence.
  • The trading allowance is £1,000 a year now and rises to £3,000 from 6 April 2027.

How much profit does print on demand make per t-shirt in the UK?

Print on demand is sold as a business with no costs. It has no upfront costs, which is a different thing entirely.

Here is one real UK sale, using Printful's published UK prices and Etsy's own fee pages, checked in November 2026.

That is about 37% of the order kept. It is a better day than most.

Three things make it worse. Printful says it charges tax based on your customer's location. If 20% VAT is added to the £12.61 print and post bill and you are not VAT registered, that is another £2.52, leaving £7.08.

Second, Etsy's Offsite Ads. If the sale came through one, Etsy takes 15% of the order, or 12% once your shop has made $10,000 in any 365 days. On this order that is £3.85, plus £0.77 VAT, leaving about £2.46.

Third, returns and reprints that are your fault, not the printer's. One of those wipes out the profit on several good orders.

Two things make it better. Multi item orders share one shipping charge. A second shirt costs £1.20 to post instead of £3.69, so it carries £2.49 more profit.

And your own shop replaces Etsy's fees with a card fee. On Shopify Basic that is 2% + 25p, about £0.76 on this order, against £3.48 on Etsy.

A worked example: Priya's Etsy shop in Leeds

Priya runs a small Etsy shop from Leeds. She sells cycling slogan shirts at £22 plus £3.69 postage, printed by Printful in its UK and EU network.

In November she sells 40 single shirts and 10 two shirt orders. None came through Offsite Ads, and she has not added a VAT number to Etsy.

  • 40 single orders at £9.60 each leave £384.00.
  • 10 two shirt orders each total £47.69. Printful costs £22.73 (two bases at £8.92 plus £3.69 and £1.20 postage). Etsy takes £3.10 transaction fee, £2.11 processing and £1.04 VAT. Each leaves £18.71, so £187.10 in all.
  • Total before tax: £571.10 from 60 shirts, about £9.52 a shirt.

Now the less happy version. If Printful adds 20% VAT to every print bill, Priya's print costs rise by about £146, so her month drops to about £425.

That is still real money for a spare room business. But it took 60 sales to get there, and November is the best month of her year.

Which print on demand products have the best profit margins?

T-shirts are the most competitive and lowest margin product in this business. They are also the first thing everyone tries.

The pattern is clear. The further you get from a generic garment, the better the economics.

A personalised print for a specific job, hobby, town or family event competes with almost nobody. That buyer is not shopping on price.

How do you choose a UK print on demand supplier?

This is the single most important decision, and the one people give least thought to.

  • Print location. Insist on UK or EU fulfilment for UK customers. Overseas printing means longer delivery, and since Brexit it can mean your customer is asked for import VAT and a handling fee on the doorstep. That produces a refund request and a one star review at the same time.
  • Order a sample of everything you sell. Not optional. You are putting your name on a product you have never touched, and print quality, garment weight and colour accuracy vary enormously.
  • Check who handles returns and reprints when the print is wrong. Good partners reprint free for their own errors. Confirm this in writing before you rely on it.
  • Compare all in cost, not base cost. A partner with a low garment price and high shipping is usually the more expensive one. Ask whether VAT is added on top.
  • Check production time separately from shipping time. Printful, for example, quotes 2 to 5 business days to make and fulfil an order before shipping starts. Quote customers the sum of both.
  • Have a second partner set up before you need one. Printers go down, run out of popular sizes, and get overwhelmed in November.

Copyright, trademarks and losing your shop

This is where shops die, and it happens fast.

  • Song lyrics, film quotes, book lines and character names are protected. Putting them on a shirt is infringement, whoever else is doing it.
  • Trademarks cover phrases too. Ordinary sounding slogans are registered by companies for clothing specifically. Search the UK trade mark register on GOV.UK before committing to a phrase.
  • Football clubs, universities, bands and brands enforce aggressively, and their legal teams watch marketplaces closely.
  • "It was free on a design site" is not a defence. Check the licence, and check whether it permits commercial use on physical goods. Many free fonts are personal use only.
  • AI generated designs are a live legal question. Ownership and copyright status are unsettled, and outputs can resemble protected work. Use them with care and never for anything you intend to build a brand on.

The consequence is not usually a lawsuit. It is a takedown, and after enough takedowns the marketplace can close your shop.

Original work is not only more ethical here, it is more durable. The wider rules are in how copyright and licensing work for your own designs and writing.

Returns, quality and the reviews problem

You never see the product. You are trusting a third party with your reputation on every single order.

  • Sizing is the number one complaint. Publish the actual garment measurements, in centimetres, laid flat, not "fits like a regular tee".
  • Colour on screen is not colour on cotton. Photograph or mock up realistically, and say so.
  • Personalised goods are exempt from the 14 day cancellation right. Regulation 28 of the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013 excludes goods made to the consumer's specifications or clearly personalised. That is another argument for personalisation. Faulty goods still have to be put right.
  • Non personalised items are not exempt, and you must handle change of mind returns properly.
  • Reply to every negative review calmly and factually. Future buyers read the reply more than the complaint.

Is print on demand worth it in 2026?

Only if you treat it as a slow build. There is no official figure for what a typical UK print on demand shop earns, so be wary of anyone who quotes one.

  • Months 1 to 3. You upload your first designs and sell very few, often to friends and family. This is normal, and it is where most people decide the business does not work.
  • Months 3 to 6. One or two designs start selling consistently. That tells you more than all your market research did. Make ten variations of what sold, not ten new ideas.
  • Months 6 to 12. A small, uneven income for a shop that is being worked on properly, heavily weighted to the run up to Christmas.
  • Seasonality is severe. Gift buying piles up between October and December. Upload Christmas designs in September, not November.

Priya's November above is what a working shop can look like in its best month. Her July will look very different.

Do you pay tax on print on demand income?

Yes, once your trading income is over the trading allowance. That is £1,000 a year in the 2026 to 2027 tax year.

The government is raising it to £3,000 from 6 April 2027. Note that the allowance is measured on income, meaning your sales, not your profit. Above it, register for Self Assessment and pay tax on your profit.

Etsy and other platforms now report seller details to HMRC. A seller of goods is left out only with fewer than 30 sales and 2,000 euros (about £1,700) or less in a year. Most active print on demand shops will be reported.

VAT registration is only compulsory once your taxable turnover goes over £90,000 in 12 months. Below that, the VAT that Etsy and your printer charge you is simply a cost.

Frequently asked

Is print on demand profitable in the UK? It can be, but margins are thin. On a £22 Etsy t-shirt printed by Printful, our sums leave about £9.60 before tax, falling to around £2.46 with VAT on the print bill and an Offsite Ads fee. Personalised and niche products, higher prices and multi item orders are where profit actually comes from.

Can I start print on demand with no money? Almost. There is no stock to buy, but you should pay for samples of everything you sell, and Etsy charges $0.20 for every listing. An own shop on Shopify Basic costs £25 a month. Call it a modest start up cost rather than none, and budget for it before your first sale.

Do I need to be a designer? No, but you need something people specifically want. Text and typographic designs sell well and need no drawing ability. What does not work is generic art with no audience in mind. A phrase that only one hobby, job or town understands beats a pretty picture that could be for anyone at all.

Is print on demand saturated? The generic end is completely saturated, especially plain slogan t-shirts. Specific niches with a real community and a phrase only they understand are not. That is where profitable print on demand shops live, along with personalised gifts, where the buyer is not comparing your price against a thousand other identical shirts.

Should I run ads for print on demand? Not until a design has already sold without them. Advertising a product nobody has bought is paying to discover what you could have learned free. On Etsy, Offsite Ads take 15% of an attributed order, or 12% for larger shops, which can turn a good t-shirt sale into a near loss.

The verdict

Print on demand in the UK is a real business with genuinely thin margins. A £22 t-shirt leaves somewhere between about £2.46 and £9.60, depending on VAT and ads.

Skip generic t-shirts, go straight to personalised and niche products, print in the UK or EU, order samples, and keep every design your own. Expect the first six months to teach you rather than pay you, and judge the whole thing on what happens in your second Christmas.

If the idea sells better as words than as a mug, the royalty split on a Kindle ebook is the honest comparison to make, because there is no unit cost at all.
