1. What does the November 2026 jobs data say?

The UK jobs market is flat at a low level, and pay growth is cooling just as inflation picks up. That is the short read of the ONS labour market release of 17 November 2026.

That explains why "the market has stopped getting worse" and "I have sent 80 applications" are both true at once.

2. Why do the jobs numbers seem to contradict each other?

Because they come from different places and measure different things.

The 4.6% unemployment rate comes from the Labour Force Survey, which asks households about their work. The payroll figure comes from HMRC's PAYE records, a count of people actually being paid by employers.

This month the survey says fewer people are out of work. The tax records say fewer people are on payrolls, and the Claimant Count says more people are claiming. They cannot all describe the same trend.

The honest reading is caution. Both are estimates, and the ONS marks the latest payroll figure as provisional, so it is often revised. Neither is the whole truth on its own.

The same goes for vacancies and hiring.

A vacancy count is a stock. A placement count is a flow. The stock is the water in the bath. The flow is the tap.

Vacancies are the open roles sitting there on a given day. Placements are the people actually hired in a month.

You can empty a bath for three years, then turn the tap on for a while. The bath stays nearly empty. That is roughly what happened over the summer, when permanent hiring briefly stopped falling.

If you have a job and wonder whether your employer will cut staff, watch the stock and the payroll count. If you are looking for work, the flow decides whether anyone calls you back.

3. Is the UK jobs market recovering?

Not yet. The summer's green shoots have wilted.

In August, KPMG and the Recruitment and Employment Confederation reported that permanent placements stabilised in July, ending a 45 month decline. Temporary hiring rose for the first time in two years.

Their Report on Jobs of 9 November 2026, based on recruiters surveyed from 12 to 26 October, says that turn has reversed for now:

  • Permanent placements fell for the first time in four months, and the fall was notable. Recruiters blamed "economic uncertainty" and rising costs.
  • Temp billings fell for the first time since March, ending six monthly rises in a row.
  • Vacancies are falling more slowly. The report says "the rate of decline was the softest in over two years". Demand for temporary staff rose slightly.
  • Candidate availability rose sharply, driven by redundancies and fewer openings. More people are chasing each role.
  • Starting salaries rose a little faster, but stayed below their historical average.

So the flow that looked like it was turning in July has turned down again in October. Demand is falling more slowly, which is the one hopeful sign.

If you are weighing up a move, the November data says to keep the job you have until the new one is signed. If you are facing redundancy, how to calculate your redundancy pay sets out the legal minimum.

4. Is UK pay growth beating inflation?

On average, barely. In the private sector, on the latest inflation figure, no.

The ONS says regular pay, excluding bonuses, grew 3.5% in the year to July to September 2026. Total pay including bonuses grew 3.6%. Both are down from the previous three months.

After inflation, real regular pay grew 0.4% on CPI and 0.2% on CPIH. That is thin.

The split by sector is the real story:

Public sector regular pay growth: 5.4%.

Private sector regular pay growth: 3.1%.

CPI inflation in the year to October 2026: 3.6%, up from 3.5% in September. CPIH was 3.7%.

Most UK employees work in the private sector. Their average pay rise is now running half a point below the most recent inflation reading.

The gap between the sectors has narrowed. In the August release it was 3.3 points, public 6.1% against private 2.8%. Now it is 2.3 points. Private pay picked up a little and public pay cooled. In August the ONS warned that public sector growth was being lifted by the timing of pay awards.

Two honest caveats. The pay figures cover July to September, and the 3.6% CPI figure is for October, so the periods do not line up exactly. And a sector average is not a forecast of your own pay rise. It includes people who got nothing and people who got 15%.

The legal floor moves next April. The government accepted the Low Pay Commission's advice, so the National Living Wage rises to £13.21 an hour for workers aged 21 and over from April 2027. That is a 3.9% rise from £12.71.

5. Worked example: Amara in Luton, on £34,000

Amara works in logistics administration for a private employer in Bedfordshire. She earns £34,000 and pays English income tax rates. Suppose her employer awards exactly the private sector average.

The rise. £34,000 × 3.1% = £1,054, taking her to £35,054.

What she needed to stand still. £34,000 × 3.6% = £1,224, so £35,224. She is £170 short before tax.

What she actually keeps. The rise sits above the £12,570 personal allowance and below the £50,270 upper earnings limit. It is taxed at 20% and carries 8% employee National Insurance. She keeps 72p in the pound: £758.88 a year, or £63.24 a month.

If she has a Plan 2 student loan (threshold £29,385, deducted at 9%), her marginal rate becomes 37%. She keeps £664.02 a year, or about £55.34 a month.

Now the same salary on the public sector average of 5.4%. That is £1,836, taking a counterpart to £35,836. The cash gap after one year, on identical starting salaries, is £782.

That gap was £1,122 on the August figures. It has shrunk, but it is still real money.

All the 2026 to 2027 tax figures above come from HMRC's rates and thresholds guidance. The personal allowance and the £50,270 threshold stay frozen. That is why a rise that only matches inflation still lifts the share of your pay that is taxed, as our guide to what the frozen tax thresholds cost you shows.

6. What does the Bank Rate rise mean for jobs and pay?

On 5 November 2026 the Bank of England raised Bank Rate by a quarter point to 4%. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 8 to 1. Catherine Mann wanted a bigger rise, to 4.25%.

The minutes show why pay matters to that decision. Governor Andrew Bailey said "the labour market appears to be stabilising".

Megan Greene said "underlying wage growth is stuck around 3.5%, above target-consistent levels". In plain words, she thinks pay is still rising faster than fits 2% inflation.

For you, that cuts two ways. Higher rates make borrowing dearer and can slow hiring further. But Bailey's words suggest the Bank does not see a jobs collapse coming.

The next decision is on 17 December 2026, two days after the next ONS labour market release on 15 December. What changed for savers and borrowers is in our guide to the 5 November rate decision.

7. Who is still hiring, and who is not?

The detail matters more than the headline, because the picture is uneven.

  • Small firms are pulling back hardest. The ONS says the largest fall in vacancies was at businesses with 1 to 9 employees, down 22,000 on the quarter.
  • Vacancies fell in 11 of 18 sectors. Administrative and support services, construction, and transport and storage each lost 5,000.
  • IT and engineering lead permanent demand. KPMG and the REC say six of ten job categories saw demand rise, led by IT and computing and engineering.
  • Retail and hospitality are weakest. Retail and hotel and catering saw the steepest falls in permanent demand. Retail had by far the steepest drop in temp demand.
  • Blue collar temp work is still growing. It recorded the steepest rise in temporary demand.
  • The South is weakest. The South of England had the sharpest fall in permanent placements and the Midlands a solid fall. The North of England rose fractionally and London showed marginal growth.

So here is the part worth saying plainly. If you work in retail or hospitality in the South of England, none of the faint good news is about you. Your sector and your region are both still shrinking.

That is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to treat "the jobs market is stabilising" headlines as somebody else's news, and plan around it. Seasonal work is one buffer, and Christmas temp jobs in 2026 covers what is on offer and what it pays.

The honest verdict

The UK jobs market in November 2026 is not collapsing, and it is not recovering. It is flat at a low level. The brief summer turn in hiring has reversed for now, and payrolls are still shrinking.

The pay picture is the bigger worry. Pay growth is slowing while inflation rises. If you work in the private sector, the average award is now behind the latest inflation figure.

Asking for more than the average is not greedy this year. It is the only way to stand still. Recruiters say starting salaries are still rising, which is your lever: replacing you costs more than keeping you. Our guide to asking for a pay rise shows how to use it.

Nothing here needs a decision today. The next ONS labour market release is 15 December 2026. If you were going to move jobs anyway, line up the offer before you resign. If you were not, there is no urgency in any of this.

Frequently asked

Is the UK jobs market getting better or worse? Mostly flat, leaning weaker. The ONS says vacancies fell to 690,000 in August to October 2026 and payrolled employees fell 95,000 on the year. Unemployment eased to 4.6%, but the Claimant Count rose. Recruiters say permanent placements fell again in October, though demand is falling at its slowest rate in two years.

What is the UK unemployment rate now? 4.6% for July to September 2026, according to the ONS release of 17 November 2026. That is down 0.3 percentage points on both the previous quarter and the year. The employment rate for people aged 16 to 64 rose to 75.4%. The next figures come out on 15 December 2026.

Are wages rising faster than inflation in the UK? Only just, on average. Regular pay grew 3.5% in July to September 2026, which is 0.4% after CPI inflation. Private sector pay grew 3.1%, below the 3.6% CPI rate for October. Public sector pay grew 5.4%, and that is what keeps the average above inflation.

Why do vacancy figures and recruiter surveys disagree? They are different tools. The ONS Vacancy Survey counts open positions at businesses, so it shows the level. The KPMG and REC report is a diffusion index from recruitment firms, where 50 means no change on the month, so it shows direction. One measures the stock of jobs, the other the flow of hiring.

Should I ask for a pay rise now or wait? If your last review was more than a year ago, waiting has a cost. Inflation is running ahead of the average private sector award. Recruiters report starting salaries still rising, so what your employer would pay to replace you has gone up. That is the strongest argument you have this winter.
