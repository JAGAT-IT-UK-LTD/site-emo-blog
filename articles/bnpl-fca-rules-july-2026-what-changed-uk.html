Buy Now Pay Later FCA Rules: What Changed in July 2026, and the Two Kinds of Plan Still Left Out

Ruhul Amin · Published 11 September 2026 · Updated 11 November 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked November 2026

The Buy Now Pay Later FCA rules started on 15 July 2026, and they are now in force. Regulated plans bring affordability checks, Section 75 and a free Ombudsman route. But plans signed before that date, and plans where the shop is also the lender, are still outside the rules, so it pays to check each one you have.

The short version

  • The FCA started regulating Deferred Payment Credit on 15 July 2026. That is the interest free kind of Buy Now Pay Later, repaid in 12 or fewer instalments over 12 months or less.
  • Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act does apply. The FCA says you may be able to get a refund from the lender if something goes wrong with what you bought. The usual Section 75 limits still apply: a single item priced over £100 and up to £30,000.
  • Carve out one: agreements taken out before 15 July 2026 are still unregulated. The new protections do not apply to them, however long they run.
  • Carve out two: if the shop and the lender are the same business, it is not regulated. Cover only applies where the credit comes from a different business to the seller.
  • Lenders must check you can afford to repay before the agreement starts, and tell you up front what any late fee will be.
  • You can take a complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service if the lender will not put things right.
  • Some lenders are only temporarily permitted, not yet authorised. The FCA consumer page still listed 15 of them when we checked in November 2026, including Clearpay.

1. What do the Buy Now Pay Later FCA rules actually cover?

Two different products share the same checkout label. One charges interest, or starts charging it if you miss the deadline. That kind was already regulated credit.

The other is interest free and repaid in 12 or fewer instalments over 12 months or less. The FCA calls it Deferred Payment Credit. That is the one most people mean, and until this summer it sat outside the rules completely.

Before 15 July 2026 a Deferred Payment Credit lender needed no FCA authorisation and followed no FCA rules. From that date it does.

Firms now either hold full authorisation or sit in a temporary permissions regime while their application is processed. Registration for that regime opened on 15 May 2026 and closed on 1 July 2026. Either way, firms must follow the rules for agreements entered into from 15 July.

This is not a niche product. The FCA says around 11 million people use Buy Now Pay Later. Its Financial Lives Survey found 10.9 million adults used it in the year to May 2024.

2. What protections did you gain on 15 July 2026?

The date has now passed, and the rules are in force. Here is what actually changed for borrowers.

An affordability check before you borrow. Lenders must check you can afford to repay before the agreement starts. Approval at the tap of a button, with no real check, is no longer compliant.

The late fee in writing, before you commit. You must be told the amount borrowed, when repayments fall due and how much each is. You must also be told how much any late fee will be, and what protections you have.

The late fee line is the useful one. Late fees are where the cost of these plans actually lived.

Help if you fall behind. Miss a repayment and the firm has to contact you and explain what it means. If you are struggling, the lender must offer support rather than leave you to it.

A free route to the Ombudsman. If the lender will not put a complaint right, the Financial Ombudsman Service is now open to you. It costs you nothing.

3. Does Section 75 apply to Buy Now Pay Later?

Yes, for regulated plans. Several outlets reported the opposite, so it is worth being precise.

The FCA states that Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act is available for Deferred Payment Credit. If something goes wrong with what you bought, you may be able to get a refund from the lender.

That is the same protection a credit card gives. If the retailer folds and the sofa never arrives, the lender is in the frame.

The normal Section 75 limits still apply. The law covers a single item with a cash price over £100 and not more than £30,000. A basket of small items under £100 each will not qualify, even if the total is higher.

For a fuller comparison, our guide to Section 75 versus chargeback explains when each route works best.

A protection that only attaches to agreements signed after one particular Wednesday in July is not a protection you can assume you have. It is one you have to date check.

4. Which plans are still unregulated?

One: the rules are not retrospective. The FCA says any Deferred Payment Credit agreement taken out before 15 July 2026 remains unregulated. The new protections do not apply to it.

A twelve month plan signed in June 2026 runs well into 2027 with none of this attached. The rules bite on when the agreement was entered into, not on when payments are still being taken.

Two: if the shop is also the lender, it is outside the rules. Deferred Payment Credit is regulated only where the lender and the supplier are different businesses.

A pay later plan from a third party is covered. The same plan provided by the retailer selling you the goods is not regulated at all, whatever the date. That is the catalogue and in house store account model, and it is not a fringe case.

So the checkout button tells you almost nothing. Two people buying identical goods on the same afternoon can have completely different rights. It depends on who lent the money and, for older plans, which month they signed.

5. How do you check if a BNPL lender is FCA regulated?

Use the free FCA Firm Checker. Search the firm by name and choose the borrowing money service.

Then check two things. It should show as Authorised, and it should have permission to lend you money on an unsecured basis.

Not authorised is not automatically a red flag. The firm may sit on the temporary permissions register instead. That means it is not yet authorised, but may still offer these agreements while its application is dealt with, and must follow the rules.

The FCA consumer page listed 15 lenders on that register when we checked in November 2026. They include Clearpay, several credit unions and specialist medical and fertility finance providers.

What you are looking for is the absence of both. A firm that is neither authorised nor temporarily permitted should not be offering you one of these plans.

Worked example: Priya Raval in Leicester

Priya has three pay later plans running at once, which is ordinary rather than reckless. They are not worth the same in rights.

Plan A, taken 2 June 2026. Third party lender, £240 for a washing machine over three instalments of £80. Signed six weeks before the rules started, so it is unregulated. No Section 75 claim, no Ombudsman route, no duty to support her if she falls behind.

Plan B, taken 1 September 2026. Third party lender, a £180 pushchair over four instalments of £45. After 15 July, with lender and shop as different businesses, so it is regulated in full. A single item over £100 means Section 75 is in play too.

Plan C, taken 20 August 2026. A £180 order on a catalogue store account, where shop and lender are the same business. Well after 15 July, but still unregulated because of carve out two. The date did not save it.

What that adds up to: £420 of her £600 total borrowing carries none of the new protections. One of the two plans she signed after the deadline is outside the rules on a technicality about who owns the lender.

She has done nothing wrong, and she could not have known from the checkout pages.

The one thing worth doing: for each plan, note the start date and the lender name. Two facts per plan. That is the only way to know which agreements she can actually complain about.

6. The honest verdict

This is a genuine improvement, and it was overdue. An affordability check, the late fee up front, a duty to help people who fall behind, Section 75 and a free Ombudsman route. That is most of what consumer credit regulation is for.

But the two carve outs are not small print. They are structural. Every agreement signed before 15 July 2026 is untouched, and every plan where the retailer is also the lender is untouched whatever the date.

The protections are real. They are not universal, and nothing at the point of sale tells you which side of the line you are on.

So the useful action is not to shop differently. Spend ten minutes on the plans you already have. Write down the start date and lender for each, and run the lender through the Firm Checker.

If a plan predates July, or the shop lent you the money itself, treat it as unprotected and clear it first. If you want to see how your plans look to lenders, our guide to free credit score services shows where to check your file at no cost.

Frequently asked

Is Buy Now Pay Later regulated by the FCA in the UK? Yes, since 15 July 2026, for interest free plans repaid in 12 or fewer instalments over 12 months or less. Lenders must be authorised or temporarily permitted. Plans signed before that date, and plans where the shop is also the lender, remain outside the rules, so check both facts for each agreement.

Does this mean Buy Now Pay Later is safe now? It means it is regulated where it qualifies. It is still credit, and missing payments still has consequences, including late fees and possible collection activity. Regulation changes how you are treated, with affordability checks and support if you struggle. It does not change whether the debt is real or must be repaid.

Is my plan from before July 2026 covered? No. The FCA says any Deferred Payment Credit agreement taken out before 15 July 2026 remains unregulated, and the new protections do not apply. That holds even if you are still paying it off today. Keep a note of the start date, and treat older plans as having no Section 75 or Ombudsman route.

Does Buy Now Pay Later show on my credit file? The FCA consumer guidance does not set a reporting rule, so practice varies by lender. Some report plans to credit reference agencies and some do not. Ask your lender what it reports, and check your own file with a free credit reference service rather than assuming either way.

What if an interest free plan starts charging interest? Then it is not Deferred Payment Credit as the FCA defines it. Credit that charges interest was already regulated under the Consumer Credit Act before July 2026. That means the usual consumer credit protections, including Section 75 for qualifying items, already applied to it under a different heading.
