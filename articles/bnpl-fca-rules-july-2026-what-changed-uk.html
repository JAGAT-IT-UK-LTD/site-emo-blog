Buy Now Pay Later Came Under Real Rules in July. Two Kinds of Agreement Were Quietly Left Out

Ruhul Amin · Published 11 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The Financial Conduct Authority started regulating Deferred Payment Credit on 15 July 2026, and one of the protections people assumed they had lost turns out to apply after all. But two large carve-outs mean a lot of plans are still outside the rules entirely, and neither got much coverage.

The short version

  • The FCA started regulating Deferred Payment Credit on 15 July 2026. That is the interest-free kind of Buy Now Pay Later, repaid in 12 or fewer instalments over 12 months or less.
  • Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act does apply. The FCA says plainly that if something goes wrong with what you bought, you may be able to get a refund from the lender, and that this is the same protection you would have had on a credit card. Several news reports said the opposite.
  • Carve-out one: agreements you took out before 15 July 2026 are still unregulated. The new protections do not apply to them at all, however long they run on for.
  • Carve-out two: if the shop and the lender are the same business, it is not regulated. Cover only applies where the credit comes from a different business to the one selling you the goods.
  • Lenders must now check you can afford to repay before the agreement starts, and must tell you up front what any late fee will be.
  • You can now take a complaint to the Financial Ombudsman Service if the lender will not put things right.
  • Some lenders are not authorised yet, only temporarily permitted. Fifteen were on the FCA temporary permissions register when this was written, including Clearpay.

1. What actually became regulated, and what did not

Two different products share the same shop-window label. One charges interest, or starts charging it if you do not clear the balance in time, and that kind was already regulated. The other is interest-free, repaid in 12 or fewer instalments over 12 months or less, and the FCA calls it Deferred Payment Credit. That is the one most people mean, and until this summer it sat outside the rules completely.

Before 15 July 2026 a Deferred Payment Credit lender needed no FCA authorisation and followed no FCA rules. From that date it does. Firms either hold full authorisation or sit in a temporary permissions regime while their application is processed, and either way they must follow the rules for agreements entered into from 15 July.

2. The four things you genuinely gained

An affordability check before you borrow. Lenders must now check you can afford to repay before the agreement starts. Approval in the time it takes to tap a button, with no meaningful check, is no longer compliant.

The late fee in writing, before you commit. You must be told the amount borrowed, when repayments fall due, how much each is, how much any late fee will be, and what protections you have. The late fee line is the useful one, because late fees are where the cost of these plans actually lived.

Help if you fall behind. Miss a repayment and the firm has to contact you and explain what it means. If you are struggling, the lender must provide support rather than leaving you to it.

Section 75, which several outlets reported you did not get. Worth being precise, because the coverage contradicted itself. The FCA states that if something goes wrong with what you bought using Deferred Payment Credit you may be able to get a refund from the lender, because Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act is available, and that this is the same protection you would have if you had used a credit card. If the retailer folds and the sofa never arrives, the lender is in the frame. And if the lender will not put it right, the Financial Ombudsman Service is now open to you, free.

A protection that only attaches to agreements signed after one particular Wednesday in July is not a protection you can assume you have. It is one you have to date-check.

3. The two carve-outs nobody mentioned

One: it is not retrospective. In the FCA own words, any Deferred Payment Credit agreement taken out before 15 July 2026 remains unregulated and the new protections do not apply to it. A twelve-month plan signed in June 2026 runs most of the way through 2027 with none of this attached. The rules bite on when the agreement was entered into, not on when payments are still being taken.

Two: if the shop is also the lender, it is outside the rules. Deferred Payment Credit is regulated only where the lender and the supplier are different businesses. A pay-later plan from a third party is covered. The same plan provided by the retailer selling you the goods is not regulated at all, whatever the date. That is the catalogue and in-house store account model, and it is not a fringe case.

So the checkout button tells you almost nothing. Two people buying identical goods on the same afternoon can have completely different rights, depending on who lent the money and, for older plans, which month they signed.

4. How to check your lender in about a minute

Use the free FCA Firm Checker. Search the firm by name, choose the service described as borrowing money including credit card lending and credit information, then check two things: that it shows as Authorised, and that it has permission to lend you money on an unsecured basis.

Not authorised is not automatically a red flag. It may sit on the temporary permissions register instead, which means it is not yet authorised but may still offer these agreements while its application is dealt with, and still has to follow the rules from 15 July. Fifteen lenders were on that register when this was written, including Clearpay, alongside several credit unions and specialist medical and fertility finance providers. What you are looking for is the absence of both. A firm that is neither authorised nor temporarily permitted should not be offering you one of these plans.

Worked example: Priya Raval in Leicester

Priya has three pay-later plans running at once, which is ordinary rather than reckless. They are not worth the same in rights.

Plan A, taken 2 June 2026. Third-party lender, £240 for a washing machine over three instalments of £80. Signed six weeks before the rules started, so it is unregulated: no Section 75 claim, no Ombudsman route, no duty to support her if she falls behind.

Plan B, taken 1 September 2026. Third-party lender, £180 of clothing over four instalments of £45. After 15 July, and lender and shop are different businesses, so it is regulated in full.

Plan C, taken 20 August 2026. A catalogue store account where shop and lender are the same business. Well after 15 July, but still unregulated because of carve-out two. The date did not save it.

What that adds up to: £420 of her £600 total borrowing carries none of the new protections, and one of the two agreements she signed after the deadline is outside the rules on a technicality about who owns the lender. She has done nothing wrong and could not have known from the checkout pages.

The one thing worth doing: for each plan, note the start date and the lender name. Two facts per plan. That is the whole exercise, and it is the only way to know which agreements she can actually complain about.

5. The honest verdict

This is a genuine improvement and it was overdue. An affordability check before lending, the late fee stated up front, a duty to help people who fall behind, Section 75 and a free route to the Ombudsman: that is most of what consumer credit regulation is for, finally applied to a product that reached enormous scale without any of it.

But the two carve-outs are not small print, they are structural. Every agreement signed before 15 July 2026 is untouched, and every plan where the retailer is also the lender is untouched whatever the date. The protections are real. They are not universal, and nothing at the point of sale tells you which side of the line you are on.

So the useful action is not to shop differently. It is to spend ten minutes on the plans you already have, write down the start date and lender for each, and run the lender through the Firm Checker. If a plan predates July, or the shop lent you the money itself, treat it as unprotected and clear it first. Frequently asked

Does this mean Buy Now Pay Later is safe now? It means it is regulated where it qualifies. It is still credit, and missing payments still has consequences. Regulation changes how you are treated, not whether the debt is real.

Is my plan from last year covered? No. Agreements entered into before 15 July 2026 remain unregulated.

Does it show on my credit file now? Not settled by these rules. The FCA has signalled it intends to consult on requiring lenders to report to credit reference agencies, so that is a proposal rather than a current requirement. Ask your lender what it reports rather than assuming.

What if an interest-free plan starts charging interest? Then it is not Deferred Payment Credit as defined, and it was already regulated credit under a different heading.
