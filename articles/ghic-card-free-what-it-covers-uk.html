The Card Is Free, the Form Takes Ten Minutes, and the Fee You Were Charged Was for Nothing

A UK Global Health Insurance Card costs nothing and lasts up to five years. The NHS says so on the page and warns about unofficial sites in the same paragraph. It is also not travel insurance, which is the more expensive misunderstanding.

The short version

  • It is free and lasts up to 5 years. Apply through the NHS website, and apply up to 9 months before your current card expires.
  • One card per person. You can add a spouse, civil partner and children to your application, but each family member gets their own card.

1. What the card is, and what it costs

The UK Global Health Insurance Card lets you get necessary state healthcare in the European Economic Area and some other countries on the same basis as a resident of that country. The NHS is careful with that phrasing, because it cuts both ways: it may be free, or it may require a payment equivalent to what a local resident would pay.

It is free. It lasts up to 5 years. You apply through the NHS website, and the NHS adds a sentence of its own: avoid unofficial websites, they may charge you a fee to apply. That is the entire business model of a certain kind of site, and it is worth saying plainly that there is no premium version, no faster tier and no service they can provide that the free form does not.

The GHIC has replaced the old European Health Insurance Card. An existing EHIC works until the expiry date on the card, and then you apply for a GHIC. You can apply for a new card up to 9 months before your current one expires, which is the sensible time to do it rather than the week before a trip.

To apply you need your full name, address, date of birth, National Insurance number, and a Health and Care number if you are from Northern Ireland. After you submit, the NHS emails within 24 hours to say whether it is approved, and the card arrives within 15 working days by post.

2. What it covers, and what it does not

The card covers state healthcare that cannot reasonably wait until you come back to the UK, which the NHS calls medically necessary healthcare. Its examples are emergency treatment and visits to A&E, treatment or routine medical care for long-term or pre-existing medical conditions, and routine maternity care as long as you are not going abroad to give birth.

Some treatments have to be pre-arranged with the provider in the country you are visiting, and the NHS names kidney dialysis and chemotherapy, because capacity is not guaranteed. Whether treatment is medically necessary is decided by the healthcare provider where you are, not by you and not by the NHS.

What it does not cover is a short list and an important one. Being flown back to the UK, which is medical repatriation. Treatment in a private medical facility. Ski or mountain rescue. And the NHS states twice on the same page that the card is not a replacement for travel and medical insurance.

There is a further catch that surprises people at the till. Not all state healthcare is free outside the UK. If a local resident would be expected to pay, so will you. Where a country asks patients to make a contribution, called a co-payment or patient share, you can claim back the difference between the total bill and the co-payment, but the co-payment itself is not refundable.

3. Where it works, and the family arithmetic

A UK GHIC works in the European Economic Area, Montenegro, Australia, Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man, and St Helena, Tristan and Ascension. Switzerland is conditional: you can use a GHIC there if you are a British national, a Swiss national, an EU citizen, a refugee, a stateless person, or a family member of someone in one of those groups. The NHS notes the UK government is negotiating to expand coverage, so check before you travel rather than assuming from an old list.

Montenegro has its own quirk. Emergency treatment is free but you pay for prescribed medicines and other treatment, and a UK national's passport is enough for emergency care, while a UK resident who is not a UK national needs the passport and the card.

Sandra in Inverness is travelling with her husband and two children. She assumes one card covers the household.

It does not. Every member of the family needs their own card. What she can do is add her spouse and children to her own application, entering her details first, which produces four cards from one sitting.

What those four cards cost: £0. If she had gone through a site charging a processing fee per applicant, she would have paid it four times over for a form the NHS provides free. There is nothing to compare on price, because one side of the comparison is zero.

The timing she should plan for: approval email within 24 hours, cards within 15 working days by post. That is three working weeks before four cards are physically in the house, which is not a thing to start the week before flying.

If the card has not arrived and you need treatment, you can apply for a Provisional Replacement Certificate, which gives the same level of cover. Without a card, a GHIC or a PRC, you pay in full, so ask for the invoice and keep the receipt.

4. The card against travel insurance, line by line

The NHS says it plainly: a UK GHIC is not a replacement for travel insurance. The two cover different things, and the overlap is smaller than most people assume. This is the split.

A worked example, because the timing catches people out more often than the cover does. Nadia in Luton books a half-term trip to Spain on 6 October and flies on 24 October, and her old EHIC expired in August. The NHS answers an application by email within 24 hours and posts the card within 15 working days of approval. Fifteen working days from 6 October runs to 27 October, three days after she flies. So the card she applied for is not what will cover her on that trip. What covers her is a Provisional Replacement Certificate, which carries the same cover as the card and exists for exactly this gap.

The money side has a second trap. The card buys you a local resident's position, not a free one, so whatever a local pays, you pay too. If the patient share where you are treated is one fifth of the bill, a 400 euro hospital bill leaves 80 euros with you, and that 80 euros is not refundable however much paperwork you send. Keep every receipt anyway, because the part you can claim back is the difference between the total and the co-payment.

Who this does not help. Anyone travelling outside the EEA, Montenegro, Australia, Jersey, Guernsey, the Isle of Man, St Helena, Tristan and Ascension, or Switzerland on the narrower conditions: outside those places the card does nothing, and you pay unless the UK has a healthcare agreement with that country. Anyone who is not ordinarily and legally resident in the UK, or who already has healthcare cover from an EEA country or Switzerland, is not entitled to a card at all, and using one you are not entitled to can leave you liable for the full cost of the treatment. And sometimes the answer is to do nothing: an EHIC that has not expired still works to the date printed on it, and you cannot apply for a replacement more than 9 months before that date anyway.

The verdict

Get one, because it is free, it lasts five years, and the application is short. Apply through the NHS route and nowhere else.

Then treat it as what it is. It reduces the cost of state treatment in the countries where it works. It does not fly you home, it does not pay for a private clinic, it does not get you off a mountain, and it does not cover the part of the bill a local resident would pay. Those are exactly the expensive scenarios, which is why the NHS keeps repeating that it is not a replacement for travel insurance.

Where it does not help you at all: a holiday outside the covered list. Outside those countries you pay for treatment unless the UK has a healthcare agreement with that country, and NHSBSA keeps that list separately.

Frequently asked

Do I need a new card if I still have an EHIC? Not until it expires. The NHS says you can continue using an existing EHIC until the expiry date on the card, then apply for a GHIC. You can apply up to 9 months early.

Does the GHIC cover me in Spain and France? Those are EEA countries, so yes, for medically necessary state healthcare on the same basis as a local resident. It does not make that treatment automatically free.

Can one card cover my whole family? No. Every member of the family needs their own card, although you can add your spouse, civil partner and children to a single application.

What if I am charged when I should not have been? Keep every receipt, invoice and discharge document and contact NHS Overseas Healthcare Services. If they decide the treatment should have been covered, they reimburse you or your insurer.
