1. What actually changed in April

Household water bills in England and Wales rose by an average of 5.4% from April 2026, which is around £33 a year or £2.70 a month. The national average bill for 2026-27 is £639.

Water UK's own release makes the uncomfortable point for them: that increase was two percentage points higher than December's official inflation figure. In real terms, water got more expensive.

The money is going into a £104 billion investment programme running to 2030, with around £20 billion of it in 2026-27 alone. There is a money-back guarantee attached, so if the improvements are not delivered the regulator automatically refunds customers. Worth knowing, and worth remembering in a few years.

One caveat on the whole picture: five water companies have appealed Ofwat's final determinations to the Competition and Markets Authority. Depending on that outcome, total projected investment between 2025 and 2030 could move up or down.

2. Why your bill looks nothing like the average

The £639 average hides a spread that makes it almost useless as a personal benchmark. Here are the extremes from Water UK's published regional table for 2026-27.

So a Severn Trent customer saw a rise twenty five times larger in percentage terms than a Thames Water customer, and still pays less in total. Geography, population and what needs building where decide this, not anything you did.

One trap to avoid. Some of the low figures you will see quoted, such as Portsmouth Water at £162 or Bournemouth Water at £205, are water-only companies. Those bills cover your water supply and nothing else. Your sewerage is billed separately by a different company. Comparing a water-only figure with a combined water and wastewater bill is comparing half a bill with a whole one.

Four times as many households get help with a water bill as with a broadband bill. That is not because water companies are generous. It is because the water sector was made to fund it.

3. Social tariffs and WaterSure, which are not the same thing

People use these terms interchangeably and they are different schemes with different tests.

Social tariffs are discounted bills for customers on low incomes. Every water company runs its own, with its own name, its own income threshold and its own discount. There is no single national scheme and no national application. This is where most of the £4.1 billion committed for 2025 to 2030 goes.

WaterSure is a separate, statutory scheme. It is aimed at customers who are on a water meter, receive a qualifying benefit, and have high essential water use, typically because of a medical condition or because there are several children in the household. Rather than a percentage discount, it caps what you pay.

The distinction matters because a household can be turned down for one and still qualify for the other. Being refused a social tariff on income grounds says nothing about whether a WaterSure cap applies to your metered use.

Beyond both, companies offer payment breaks, restructured plans and, in some cases, debt forgiveness. Water UK's wording is that support can often be tailored to individual circumstances, which is a polite way of saying the published scheme is not the whole of what is available if you ring up.

Worked example: the Gallagher household in Portsmouth

Two adults and two young children, one adult working part time. They have a water meter. They looked at the news, saw £639, glanced at their own bill and decided they were doing fine.

What they actually have: two bills. Portsmouth Water supplies the water. Their sewerage comes from a different company and is billed separately. Adding them together gives a far bigger number than the water-only figure they were comparing against.

What a 40% discount would be worth: on a combined bill near the national average of £639, an average social tariff discount of around 40% is roughly £256 a year. That is more than seven times the £33 the bill went up by, and it recurs every year rather than once.

Why they have never applied: nobody wrote to them. There is no automatic enrolment, no DWP data match, and no letter. Their company publishes a scheme on a page they have never visited.

The other route: with two small children on a meter, they should also ask specifically about WaterSure, which turns on essential use rather than income alone. Two questions, one call.

4. How to ask, and what to ask for

Contact your own water company, and if you are on a meter, do it twice. Ask about the social tariff and ask about WaterSure by name. Front line staff will often answer the question you asked and no more.

Work out who bills you for what. If you are with a water-only company, your sewerage supplier is a separate business with its own separate help scheme. Two companies, two applications.

Have the household income figure ready, including benefits, and the number of people living there. Most social tariffs are assessed on total household income rather than one person's earnings.

If you are already behind, say so at the start. Payment breaks and restructured plans exist, and in some cases debt is written off. None of that is offered to people who do not make contact.

If you are refused, ask which scheme you were refused for. A no on income does not answer the WaterSure question, and vice versa.

5. The honest verdict

The £33 rise is the part that made the news and it is the part that matters least. For a household that qualifies, a social tariff at an average discount of around 40% is worth several times that, every single year, and roughly 2.5 million households will have one this year.

The reason more do not is structural. There is no national scheme, no national name, no application form on GOV.UK and no letter through the door. Each of the water companies runs its own version and waits to be asked.

So the action is a phone call, and it is worth making even if you think you earn too much, because the thresholds vary by company and the WaterSure route does not turn on income alone. If the answer is no, it cost you ten minutes.

Frequently asked

Does this apply in Scotland or Northern Ireland? No. These figures cover England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate arrangements, and household water charges work differently there.

Do I have to be on benefits? Not necessarily for a social tariff, which is assessed on household income and varies by company. WaterSure does require a qualifying benefit as well as high essential water use.

Will a meter save me money? It depends entirely on your household size against your property's rateable value. That is a separate calculation and we cover it in our water meter guide below.

What is the money-back guarantee? Bill money can only fund infrastructure independently judged to be new, necessary and value for money, and if improvements are not delivered the regulator automatically refunds customers.
