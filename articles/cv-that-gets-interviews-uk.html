Your CV Gets Seven Seconds, and Three in Four Never Reach a Human

1. What you are actually up against

Before changing a word, it helps to see the scale, because most people conclude their CV is terrible when the real answer is arithmetic.

Employers report receiving an average of around 280 applications per role, against roughly 125 in 2022. Competitive professional roles regularly see 250 to 300, and graduate schemes go well beyond that. Meanwhile the ONS puts the UK at 2.5 unemployed people per vacancy for April to June 2026, up from 2.3 in the same period a year earlier, with total vacancies 10.4% below their January to March 2020 level.

So if you have sent forty applications and heard nothing, that is roughly what the numbers predict. It does not mean your CV is fine. It does mean the fix is about getting through a filter, not about being a better person.

2. The filter you never see

98% of large organisations use some form of automated CV screening, and around 75% of CVs sent to larger businesses are rejected before a recruiter opens them. By 2026, over 85% of medium to large UK firms run applicant tracking systems that rank candidates by machine.

The important thing to understand: these systems are not judging your worth. They are parsing text and matching it against a job description. That means the failures are boring and mechanical.

  • Text inside images or graphics is invisible. A designed CV with your skills in an infographic parses as a blank skills section.
  • Tables and multi column layouts scramble the reading order. Your job titles end up interleaved with your dates.
  • Headers and footers are often skipped entirely. If your phone number and email live in the header, they may simply not exist.
  • Unusual section names fail to map. Call it "Work Experience", not "My Journey". Call it "Education", not "Where I Learned".
  • Missing the words in the advert. If the advert says "stock control" and your CV says "inventory management", a keyword match will not find you.

Fixing all five takes about twenty minutes and is worth more than any redesign.

The software is not deciding whether you are good. It is deciding whether it can read you. Those are very different problems, and only one of them is in your control.

3. What happens in the seven seconds

Once a human does open it, the widely cited figure for the first pass is 7.4 seconds. Studies that track considered reading rather than triage put it at 17 to 46 seconds. Neither number gets anywhere near your second page.

In that time a recruiter is answering three questions, in order:

  1. Has this person done roughly this job before? They look at your most recent job title and the company.
  2. For how long, and recently? They scan the dates for gaps and for job hopping.
  3. Is there a reason to keep reading? One concrete, quantified thing near the top.

Everything on your CV that does not help answer one of those three questions is, for the first pass, decoration.

4. The top third, rewritten

The single highest value change you can make is to rewrite the top third of page one for every application. Not the whole CV. The top third, which is your name block, your profile paragraph and your most recent role.

The profile paragraph. Three lines maximum. It should contain the job title from the advert, your years of relevant experience, and one number. Delete every instance of "hard working", "passionate", "team player" and "results driven". They appear on almost every CV, which means they distinguish nobody, and they burn two of your seven seconds.

Weak: "A hard working and passionate professional seeking a challenging role where I can utilise my skills."

Better: "Warehouse team leader, six years, currently running a shift of eleven. Cut stock discrepancies by 40% in twelve months by moving to weekly counts."

The second one is not better written. It is better because it contains facts, and facts are what a stranger can act on.

5. Duties versus outcomes

Most CV bullets describe what the person was told to do. Hiring managers already know what the job involves. What they cannot know is whether you were any good at it.

Duty, which says nothingOutcome, which does the work
Responsible for stock controlCut stock discrepancies by 40% over twelve months by introducing a weekly count
Handled customer complaintsResolved 30 to 40 complaints a week, keeping escalation to under 5%
Assisted with social mediaGrew the shop's Instagram from 400 to 3,100 followers in nine months, which brought roughly 15 enquiries a month
Trained new staffWrote the onboarding checklist now used for all new starters, cutting time to competent from three weeks to ten days

If you do not have numbers, you usually do, you have just never counted them. How many customers a day? How many orders? How much did the thing cost before you changed it? An estimate you can defend in an interview is far stronger than no number at all. That applies hardest at the start of a career, where an opening shift in retail produces more countable detail than it looks like: covers opened, deliveries checked, tills reconciled.

6. Format rules that matter, and the ones that do not

Rules that genuinely matter in the UK:

  • Two pages maximum. One page if you have under five years of experience.
  • Reverse chronological order. Most recent role at the top. Functional CVs that hide dates make recruiters assume you are hiding something.
  • No photo, no date of birth, no marital status, no nationality. UK employers generally do not want these, and including them can complicate their equality obligations. This differs from much of Europe, which is why templates from abroad get it wrong.
  • Simple single column, standard headings, real text. Save as PDF unless the advert asks for Word.
  • An email address that is your name. Not the one you made when you were fourteen.

Things people agonise over that do not move the needle: the font, as long as it is legible. Whether you write "References available on request", which is assumed either way. Whether you include hobbies, which are neutral unless genuinely relevant. Colour, as long as it prints legibly and does not break parsing.

7. Before and after, a real rewrite

Sam, 29, applying for a customer operations role in Manchester. 46 applications, 2 replies, both rejections.

Before, the top of page one: a two column layout with a photo, a skills bar chart showing "Communication 90%", and a profile reading "A passionate and dedicated individual with excellent communication skills looking for an opportunity to grow within a dynamic company."

What was wrong. The skills chart was an image, so the parser saw no skills at all. The two column layout interleaved his job titles with dates. His phone number was in the header, which many systems skip. The profile contained no job title, no number, and nothing from the advert.

After. Single column, no photo, contact details in the body. Profile rewritten to: "Customer operations coordinator, four years in ecommerce. Handled 120 to 150 tickets a week at a 94% first contact resolution rate. Rebuilt the returns process, cutting average handling time from 9 minutes to 5." Skills moved to a plain text list using the exact words from each advert.

Result: 22 applications, 6 first stage interviews. The work history did not change. Not one job, not one date. Only what a machine could read and what a human saw in the first seven seconds.

This is a composite example built to show the mechanism, not a client case study. The point stands: the change that works is almost never "write better", it is "be readable and be specific".

8. Mistakes that cost UK applicants interviews

  • Sending the identical CV to everything. Volume with no tailoring is the most common pattern among people getting no replies, and it is the one that feels most productive.
  • Hiding gaps. A gap is fine. An unexplained gap invites the worst assumption. One line, plainly stated, closes it.
  • Keyword stuffing in white text. Modern systems flag it, and a recruiter who spots it will reject you outright.
  • Listing every job since 2009. Anything older than ten years can be a single line, or nothing.
  • Writing "I" throughout. UK CV convention drops it. "Managed a team of six", not "I managed a team of six".
  • Letting a generative tool write the whole thing. Recruiters read hundreds of these now and the register is obvious. Use it to tighten your own sentences, not to invent your experience.

The honest verdict

Your CV has two audiences and they want opposite things. Software wants plain, parsable, keyword matched text. A human wants one specific, quantified reason to keep reading. A well designed CV usually fails the first. A generic CV fails the second.

The version that works is deliberately boring to look at and unusually specific to read. Single column, standard headings, real text, and a top third rewritten for each advert with at least one number in it.

And keep the market in view. At 280 applications a role and 2.5 unemployed people per vacancy, twenty tailored applications will beat a hundred identical ones, and the silence you have been getting is mostly arithmetic rather than a verdict on you. Frequently asked

Should I use a CV template from Canva or Word? Only the plainest single column ones. Anything with columns, sidebars, icons or charts risks failing the parse. If you love a design, keep a designed version for people you send it to directly, and a plain version for online applications.

Is a cover letter still worth writing? When there is a box for it, yes, because most applicants leave it empty or paste something generic. Three short paragraphs, specific to that employer, is enough.

How do I explain a career break? Directly and briefly. "Career break, 2024 to 2025, caring responsibilities" is complete. Do not stretch other dates to cover it, because dates get verified.

Does applying early actually matter? Yes. With hundreds of applications per role, many are reviewed in batches as they arrive, and later applicants are read by someone who already has a shortlist.
