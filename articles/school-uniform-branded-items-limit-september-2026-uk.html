School Uniform Branded Items Limit in England: Three Items, Four With a Tie, Since September 2026

The school uniform branded items limit is now law in England. Since 1 September 2026, a state school can require no more than three branded items for a primary pupil, and three for a secondary pupil, or four if one is a tie. Bags, PE kit and summer-only items all count. That matters because the logo items are the ones you can only buy from the school's supplier, so every item over the cap is money you no longer have to spend.

1. What is the school uniform branded items limit?

The school uniform branded items limit is a legal cap on how many logo items a state school in England can make you buy. It is three for primary pupils. It is three for secondary pupils, or four if one of them is a tie.

The rule sits in section 35 of the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026. That section inserts a new section 551ZA into the Education Act 1996.

In the Act's own words, a school "may not require a primary pupil at the school to have more than three different branded items of school uniform for use during a school year". For a secondary pupil the cap is three, "or more than four different branded items of school uniform if one of those items is a tie". Middle schools apply the secondary limit to every pupil.

The Department for Education's statutory guidance, "Cost of school uniforms", last updated 6 July 2026, puts it plainly. Schools must limit compulsory branded items of uniform and PE kit to three or fewer. Secondary and middle schools can go to four if one is a tie.

The guidance is for academies and academy trusts, maintained schools, pupil referral units and non-maintained special schools. That is nearly every state-funded school in England. Independent schools are not on the list, and hospital schools are excluded.

2. Did the limit really start in September 2026?

Yes. This is the question most parents had in the summer, and it now has a firm answer.

The Act received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026. Section 35 was not switched on that day. It came into force in England on 1 September 2026, through the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 (Commencement No. 2) (England) Regulations 2026, SI 2026/803.

legislation.gov.uk now shows section 35 in force from that date, with no outstanding effects. So from the first day of the 2026 to 2027 school year, the cap is the law.

The government confirmed it in a press release on 4 September 2026. It said new rules "in force from this week" cap branded items at 3 for primary pupils and 4 for secondary. The Education Secretary can act to change a school's uniform policy if it fails to comply.

The same release promised more. It said uniform buying guidance will be updated later this year, asking schools to seek suppliers offering payment plans. When we checked on 11 November 2026, the guidance page still showed its 6 July 2026 update.

3. What counts as a branded item?

The Act defines a branded item in two ways. First, anything with the school's name or logo on it, or for an academy, the trust's name or logo.

Second, and this closes the obvious loophole, any item that "as a result of its colour, design, fabric or other distinctive characteristic, is only available from particular suppliers". A plain navy jumper you can buy anywhere is not branded. A navy jumper with a gold trim only the school's supplier stocks is branded, logo or not.

An item is "required" if the pupil must have it for general use at school, for travelling to and from school, or for any lesson, club, activity or event the school facilitates. That includes after-school clubs.

The Act defines school uniform as "a bag and any clothing" required for those purposes. So branded book bags and rucksacks count towards the limit.

Items needed only at certain times of year count separately. The DfE's own example is a secondary pupil required to have a branded winter skirt, a branded summer dress, a blazer, a tie and a PE top. That is five items, one over the limit.

Where pupils may choose between two branded items, such as a branded skirt or branded trousers, the guidance treats that as one item.

Branded items the school lends or gives free still count if the pupil must wear them. Truly optional branded items sit outside the limit, but only if wearing them really is optional and an unbranded equivalent is allowed.

Safety and protective equipment, such as high visibility vests or visors, is not captured by the limit.

4. What does the law not do?

It does not cap what any single item costs. A school can still require three branded items, and the supplier can charge what it likes for them.

The guidance says cost and value for money for parents should be the most important consideration. Schools should avoid single-supplier contracts unless they run regular tendering, and re-tender at least every 5 years. But "should" is guidance, not a price cap.

It does not apply outside England. The cap in section 551ZA is written for relevant schools in England. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own arrangements, so check your own nation's guidance.

Nothing in section 551ZA orders a school to refund or replace kit bought before the change. It does not stop a school setting colours, shades, fabrics or styles for non-branded items either. That is exactly what the guidance encourages.

Sixth forms are covered when they are part of a relevant school. Separate sixth form colleges are not schools under this section.

5. What can parents do if a school has not complied?

Start by counting. Find the school's uniform policy, which the guidance says should be on its website and make clear which items are compulsory.

List every compulsory item that has a logo or is only sold by the named supplier. Include bags, PE kit and seasonal items. If a secondary list has five compulsory branded items and one is a tie, the school is one over.

Then follow the GOV.UK route for complaints about a state school. It has three steps.

  1. Talk to the school first. Write to the headteacher, cite section 551ZA of the Education Act 1996 and the DfE guidance, and ask which items will become non-branded.
  2. Use the formal complaints procedure. Schools usually publish it on their website. It normally goes to the governing body or, for an academy, the trust.
  3. Go to the Department for Education. Once you have finished the school's procedure, or if you believe it is not following the guidance, you can contact the DfE through its complaints page.

The DfE guidance also says pupils who cannot meet uniform rules for money reasons should be treated considerately. They should not miss teaching or school activities because of it.

6. How else can you cut the cost of school uniform?

The branded item cap is only one part of the cost of school uniform guidance. Three other parts help straight away.

Second-hand uniform. The statutory guidance says schools should make second-hand uniform freely accessible to all parents, with details on the school website. Selling it only at paid events, such as a school fair, is not acceptable.

Plain items from anywhere. Every item outside the branded three or four can usually be bought from a supermarket or high street shop in the right colour. That is where the real saving sits.

Other help for school costs. Free school meals now reach every child in a household on Universal Credit in England from September 2026. Some councils also run uniform grants, so check your council's website.

Selling outgrown uniform, or swapping it, also returns money. Our guide on decluttering and selling your old things covers the sites that work.

7. A worked example in Gloucester

Helen in Gloucester has a daughter who started Year 7 in September 2026. Her school's 2025 list required six branded items.

Those were a badged blazer, a crested tie, a jumper with a trim colour sold only by the school shop, a branded PE polo, a branded PE hoodie and a branded rucksack.

Under the new limit the school may require four, because one is a tie. Its 2026 list keeps the blazer, tie, PE polo and rucksack as branded. The jumper becomes plain navy from any retailer, and the PE hoodie any plain black hoodie. That is four, and it complies.

What Helen saves depends on prices, which the law does not touch. Take these illustrative prices from her own shopping: a branded jumper at £24 against a plain one at £8, and a branded hoodie at £28 against a plain one at £12.

That is £16 saved on the jumper and £16 on the hoodie. So Helen is £32 better off per child, before any second-hand rail. Buy two jumpers for the week and the saving on jumpers alone doubles to £32.

If the school had kept all six, Helen's first letter would cite section 551ZA and ask for the jumper and hoodie to lose their branding. That is what the cap was designed to force.

8. The honest verdict

Since 1 September 2026, a state school in England can make you buy at most three branded items, or four at secondary if one is a tie. Bags, PE kit and summer-only items all count. That is law, not guidance, and it is the first time the number has been fixed.

It is a cap on logos, not on prices, and it does nothing outside England. Count the items on your school's list today. If it is over, a written complaint citing section 551ZA is the route. The guidance on second-hand uniform is the saving you can use straight away.

Frequently asked questions about school uniform rules in 2026

How many branded items can a school require from September 2026?

Three for primary pupils. Three for secondary pupils, or four if one of the items is a tie. Middle schools use the secondary limit for all pupils. The rule is in section 35 of the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 and has applied in England since 1 September 2026, under commencement regulations SI 2026/803.

Does a school bag or PE kit count as a branded item?

Yes, both count. The Act defines school uniform as a bag and any clothing required for school or school activities. DfE guidance says branded book bags and rucksacks count towards the limit, and the limit covers compulsory branded uniform and PE kit together, not separately. Seasonal items such as summer dresses count too.

Does the new law cap the price of school uniform?

No. It limits the number of branded items, not what they cost. The statutory guidance says value for money for parents should come first, schools should avoid single-supplier contracts without regular tendering, and contracts should be re-tendered at least every 5 years. There is still no legal price cap on any item.

Does the branded uniform limit apply in Wales or Scotland?

No. Section 551ZA applies to relevant schools in England only. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own school uniform guidance and arrangements. Independent schools in England are also outside the DfE guidance list. If you live outside England, check your own government's guidance and your school's published uniform policy.

What can I do if my child's school has too many branded items?

Count the compulsory branded items on the published list, including bags and PE kit. Write to the headteacher citing section 551ZA. If nothing changes, use the school's formal complaints procedure. After that, you can contact the Department for Education, and the Education Secretary can act to change a school's uniform policy.
