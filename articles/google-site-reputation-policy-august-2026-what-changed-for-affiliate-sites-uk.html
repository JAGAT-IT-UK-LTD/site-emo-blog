Site Reputation Abuse: What Google's August 2026 Policy Update Means for UK Affiliate Sites

Google's site reputation abuse policy, the rule against parasite SEO, only changed its enforcement for searchers in the European Economic Area from 30 August 2026. The UK is not in the EEA, so UK readers see the same enforcement as before, and Google's own pages show no further change up to 11 November 2026. The part worth knowing is smaller and more useful: the policy now names affiliate links, treated appropriately, as not a breach. Here is what changed, what it does not do, and how to check your own site.

1. What is site reputation abuse under Google's policy?

Site reputation abuse is what SEO people call parasite SEO. A strong website hosts content written by somebody else, so that content borrows the host's ranking power.

Google's own name for the rule is now simply the site reputation policy. It sits in the spam policies documentation, and the definition is narrow. It "applies where third-party content is published on a host site mainly because of that host's already-established ranking signals, which it has earned primarily from its first-party content."

Third-party content means content "created by an entity that's separate from the established host site". Google's examples are users of the site, freelancers, white-label services, and people not employed directly by the host.

Then comes the line that matters most for a nervous reader. "Having third-party content alone isn't inconsistent with the site reputation policy." It only becomes a breach when the host's ranking signals are the main reason the content is there.

What does Google count as a breach?

The breach examples are the ones you would expect. An educational site hosts sponsored payday loan reviews, written by a third party that spreads the same page across other sites.

Or a medical site hosts a low-quality "best casinos" advertising page. It is not integrated with the rest of the site, and it is there to exploit the site's rankings.

What does Google say is not a breach?

This list is the useful part. Wire and press release services. News publications syndicating content from other publications. Forums and comment sections.

Columns, opinion pieces and other editorial work are also on it. So are advertorials whose purpose is to reach the publication's own readers, rather than to game rankings.

And, in full, "Using affiliate links throughout a page, with links treated appropriately, or embedding third-party ad units throughout a page." Google adds that the examples are non-exhaustive.

It also says it generally presumes that individual pages, including new ones, match the overall quality of the other pages on the site. If a section looks out of line, it goes to human review. The page lists presentation, content quality, stated authorship and whether the same content appears on other sites among the things reviewers look at.

2. What changed in the site reputation policy on 28 and 30 August 2026?

Google's Search Central blog post of Friday 28 August 2026, "Update to the Site Reputation Policy", recaps the 2024 launch first.

Then it says: "Following discussion with the European Commission, we are adjusting our enforcement approach within the European Economic Area (EEA) and clarifying the criteria we consider when applying the policy."

Google also records its own reservation. It says "an overbroad application of the DMA could prevent us from addressing real threats to the integrity of our search results". The DMA is the EU's Digital Markets Act.

So inside the EEA the mechanism has moved from suppression to separation. The bolted-on section stops borrowing the host's authority and has to rank on its own.

The definition of the offence has not changed. What has changed is that the policy page now spells out the non-breach examples, and the EEA treatment, on the page itself.

3. Has the site reputation abuse policy changed since August 2026?

Not on Google's own pages. We checked again on 11 November 2026.

The Search Central blog lists posts for September, October and November 2026. They cover Search Console reporting, Search Central Live events, an AI training control for websites, learning paths and CDN advice. None is a further site reputation policy update.

The spam policies page carries the same definition, the same non-breach list and the same EEA wording described above. It shows no "last updated" date, so we cannot tell you exactly when the wording was last edited.

You may see SEO blogs talk about a fresh site reputation abuse update or a 2027 change. We found nothing on Google's own pages to support that, so we are not reporting one. If Google publishes something new, it will appear on the Search Central blog first.

4. Does the EEA change apply to UK sites?

For UK readers, no. The EEA is the 27 EU member states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway. The United Kingdom left the EU in 2020 and is not in the EEA.

Google's post frames the change entirely by where the searcher is. A manual action under the policy "will have a different effect for those searching in the EEA than outside of it".

For a UK site whose readers are in the UK, a manual action would still directly affect the flagged section for UK searchers, exactly as before.

If some of your readers are in Ireland, Germany or France, those readers see the separated-ranking version. Because many pages are viewed by people globally, one page can be under an action for one audience and not for another.

The UK has its own competition track for Google search, run by the CMA rather than the European Commission. Our guide to the CMA fair ranking rule for Google search covers what that does and does not mean for a small affiliate site.

5. What does the update not do?

It does not change what counts as a breach.

It does not touch the separate core ranking updates, the helpful content guidance or the thin affiliation policy. Thin affiliation lives on the same spam policies page. It targets affiliate pages that copy merchant descriptions with no original content, not affiliate links as such.

It does not make affiliate content exempt from everything. The words are "with links treated appropriately". In practice that means rel="sponsored" or nofollow on paid links, and a disclosure the reader can see. Our affiliate disclosure rules guide covers the UK side of that.

It does not protect a site that publishes freelance or white-label content under its own name, if that content is there mainly to ride the domain's authority. That was the policy's target in 2024 and it still is.

And it does not create a mediation route for UK-only sites. The mediation offer is written for "eligible sites", and the post does not define eligibility. We would not plan around it.

6. How do you check your own site against the policy?

Three tests cover almost every one-author UK affiliate site.

Authorship. Did you, or someone you employ, write the page? If an outside company wrote it and pays you to host it, that is the risk zone.

Integration. Does the page fit the rest of the site? A coffee site reviewing grinders is integrated. A coffee site hosting a casino or payday loan section is not.

Link treatment. Are affiliate links marked rel="sponsored" or nofollow, with a visible disclosure? Google's qualify outbound links page explains the attributes.

Then look in Search Console. The Manual actions report is where Google says it notifies site owners. An empty report means no manual action is recorded against the site.

If you are unsure whether a section of your site still earns its place, our guide on refreshing affiliate content versus writing new posts is the next practical step.

7. A worked example in Norwich

Leah runs a one-person site in Norwich reviewing home coffee kit. She uses Amazon Associates links and one Awin programme.

Every review is hers, with her own photographs. There is a disclosure at the top of each page and rel="sponsored" on every affiliate link.

In August a content agency offered her £400 a month to host a "best online casinos UK" section under her domain. They would write it and link it from her footer. Over a year that is £4,800.

Run the three tests over both halves. Her reviews are first-party content. Even the affiliate links throughout them are named as not a breach, because she treats them appropriately.

The casino section fails all three. It is third-party content, unrelated to the site, placed there because her domain has earned ranking signals for coffee.

For her UK readers a manual action on that section would hit it directly. For any Irish readers it would be separated to rank on its own. For a section with no standing of its own, that means it would hardly rank at all.

Either way the £4,800 a year buys a manual action notice in Search Console and a reconsideration request. The update of 28 August changes none of that. She declines, keeps writing her own reviews, and the policy does not apply to her.

8. The honest verdict

Google's 28 August 2026 update changes how site reputation manual actions bite inside the EEA from 30 August. It also clarifies the policy's examples everywhere.

The UK is outside the EEA, so for a UK site's UK readers the enforcement is unchanged. Nothing newer had appeared on Google's pages by 11 November 2026.

The clarification is the useful part. Affiliate links throughout a page, treated appropriately, are named as not a breach.

The policy is about renting a domain's authority to somebody else's content. If you write your own pages, disclose, and mark affiliate links as sponsored, this update asks nothing new of you. If you are offered money to host a section you did not write, it is the reason to say no.

Frequently asked questions about site reputation abuse

What is site reputation abuse?

It is Google's term for parasite SEO. A site with strong rankings hosts third-party content, such as sponsored casino or loan pages, mainly so that content ranks on the host's reputation. Google's spam policies page treats this as a breach. Having third-party content is not a breach on its own; the reason it is published is what counts.

Does the site reputation policy ban affiliate links?

No. Google's spam policies page lists "using affiliate links throughout a page, with links treated appropriately" among things not considered inconsistent with the policy. Treated appropriately means marking paid links rel="sponsored" or nofollow, and showing a clear disclosure. Copying merchant descriptions with nothing original falls under the separate thin affiliation policy instead.

What changed on 30 August 2026?

Inside the European Economic Area, a manual action under the policy no longer directly suppresses the affected section. Google may instead separate it so it ranks independently over time. Outside the EEA, the action still directly affects that section, as before. Google announced this in its Search Central blog post of 28 August 2026.

Does the EEA change apply to UK sites?

Not for UK searchers. The UK is not in the EEA, and Google's post ties the change to where the searcher is, not where the site is based. A UK site's readers in Ireland or other EEA countries see the separated-ranking treatment, while its UK readers see the original enforcement. Plan as if nothing has changed.

How do I know if my site has a site reputation manual action?

Open the Manual actions report in Google Search Console. Google says site owners will continue to be notified there when a manual action is applied. If you get one, fix or remove the offending section, then submit a reconsideration request. Mediation is mentioned for eligible sites, but Google has not defined eligibility in the post.
