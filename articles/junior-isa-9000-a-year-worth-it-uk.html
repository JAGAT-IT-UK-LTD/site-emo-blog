Junior ISA: £9,000 a year, locked until 18, and then entirely theirs

Ruhul Amin · Published 6 September 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The tax shelter is genuinely good and the alternative is worse than most parents realise. The catch is not the tax treatment. It is that on their eighteenth birthday the money belongs to a teenager and nobody can stop them spending it.

The short version

  • The Junior ISA limit is £9,000 for the 2026 to 2027 tax year (GOV.UK, page updated 2 September 2026), across cash and stocks and shares combined.
  • Anyone can pay in. Only a parent or guardian with parental responsibility can open and manage the account, but grandparents, relatives and friends can all contribute to it.
  • The child takes control at 16 and can withdraw at 18. There is no mechanism to delay that and no conditions you can attach.
  • The £100 parental interest rule is the reason the ordinary alternative is worse. If a child gets more than £100 of interest in a year from money a parent gave them, the parent is taxed on all of it. Junior ISAs are exempt from that rule.
  • You cannot hold a Junior ISA and a Child Trust Fund at once. If your child has a Child Trust Fund, ask the provider to transfer it in.

1. What a Junior ISA actually is

GOV.UK describes Junior ISAs as "long-term, tax-free savings accounts for children", and every word in that sentence is load-bearing.

The limit is £9,000 for 2026 to 2027. That is the total across both types combined, not £9,000 each.

Two types. A cash Junior ISA, where you pay no tax on the interest, and a stocks and shares Junior ISA, where you pay no tax on capital growth or dividends. A child can have one of each, sharing the same £9,000.

Who can have one. The child must be under 18 and living in the UK. The one exception GOV.UK gives is for children living abroad who depend on a Crown servant, meaning the armed forces, diplomatic service or overseas civil service.

Who opens it and who owns it. This is the line that matters most: "Parents or guardians with parental responsibility can open a Junior ISA and manage the account, but the money belongs to the child." You are a custodian, not an owner. You cannot take the money back, borrow from it, or use it for a family expense, however genuine.

The timeline. The child can take control of the account at 16 but cannot withdraw the money until 18.

Not alongside a Child Trust Fund. GOV.UK: "You cannot have a Junior ISA as well as a Child Trust Fund. If you want to open a Junior ISA ask the provider to transfer the trust fund into it." Anyone born between 1 September 2002 and 2 January 2011 may have a Child Trust Fund sitting somewhere, often forgotten, and transferring it is usually worth doing because Junior ISA rates have generally been better and the charges lower.

2. The £100 rule that makes the ordinary alternative worse

The obvious alternative is a normal children's savings account. It is flexible, you can access it, and there is no lock until 18. So why bother with the Junior ISA?

Because of a rule most parents have never heard of, set out on GOV.UK's interest on savings for children page, updated 14 August 2026.

If a child gets more than £100 of interest in a tax year from money given by a parent, the parent has to pay tax on all of that interest, if it is above the parent's own Personal Savings Allowance. Not tax on the excess over £100. Tax on the whole lot, at the parent's rate, treated as the parent's income.

Three things follow, and they decide the whole comparison.

It is a cliff, not a taper. £99 of interest is fine. £101 of interest makes the entire £101 potentially taxable on you.

It bites much earlier than people expect. At a 4% savings rate, £100 of interest needs a balance of only £2,500. Saving £50 a month reaches that in a bit over four years. This is not a rule for wealthy families. It catches ordinary monthly saving before the child starts school.

It only applies to money from a parent. GOV.UK is explicit that the £100 limit does not apply to money given by grandparents, other relatives or friends, and does not apply to money inside a Junior ISA or Child Trust Fund at all.

That last point is genuinely useful planning. If grandparents want to put money aside for a child and want it accessible rather than locked to 18, an ordinary account funded by them does not trigger the rule. It is money from parents that needs the Junior ISA wrapper to be clean.

One honest qualification: the rule only produces a bill if the interest sits above the parent's own Personal Savings Allowance, which is £1,000 for a basic rate taxpayer and £500 for a higher rate taxpayer. A parent with little of their own savings may have room to absorb it. A parent who has already used their allowance on their own money has none.

3. Sian in Truro: £50 a month from birth

Sian and her partner live in Truro and start putting £50 a month away when their daughter is born. They keep it up for eighteen years, so £10,800 goes in.

Those are compound arithmetic at a constant rate, not forecasts. Cash rates move and investment returns are not guaranteed, so treat the second column as an illustration of what a higher long-run return does over eighteen years rather than as a promise.

Now the tax comparison. In a Junior ISA, none of that growth is taxable and nothing is reportable.

In an ordinary account in the child's name, funded by the parents, the balance passes £2,500 at around year four. From that point the interest exceeds £100 a year and, if Sian has used up her own Personal Savings Allowance, the whole of the child's interest is taxable on her at her rate. By the later years the interest is running at several hundred pounds annually, and it is all being added to Sian's income.

The Junior ISA is not marginally better here. It removes an entire category of admin and an entire category of tax, and it does so from about year four onwards.

The trade-off in one number: the price of that is that Sian cannot touch about £15,800 for eighteen years, no matter what happens to the family in the meantime.

4. The thing nobody wants to talk about: it is theirs at 18

On the child's eighteenth birthday the money is legally and unconditionally theirs. They can take all of it out the same day and spend it on anything.

There is no mechanism to release it in stages, no conditions you can attach, and no version where you keep a veto. The account matures and the young adult holds the money. Providers will typically offer to roll it into an adult ISA so the tax shelter continues, but that is the young adult's choice, not yours.

This is not a reason to avoid Junior ISAs. It is a reason to be honest about what you are doing. You are giving a specific sum to a specific person on a specific date, and you are choosing not to have a say in what happens next. Some families are entirely comfortable with that. Others discover they are not, at exactly the wrong moment.

Two practical mitigations, neither of which is a legal control:

  • Tell them early and tell them the number. An eighteen-year-old who has known about the account since they were twelve, and has been shown the statement, behaves very differently from one who is handed a surprise.
  • Split the plan. Some parents fund a Junior ISA up to a level they are content to hand over at 18, then hold anything beyond that in their own name, in their own ISA, and give it later on their own judgement. That money stays taxable in your hands, which is a real cost, but it buys back the control.

A bare trust is sometimes suggested as the alternative. It has its own tax treatment and its own paperwork, and the money still passes to the child, typically at 18 in England and Wales. It is not a way of keeping control either, and it is not simpler.

5. Who it suits, and who should do something else

It suits you if you are saving specifically for the child rather than for the family, if you are comfortable with the money being theirs at 18, if you would otherwise be tripping the £100 rule, and if you have your own emergency fund already in place.

Do something else if the money might be needed by the household before then, because there is no early access. If you are carrying credit card or other expensive debt, clearing it beats any children's savings rate by a distance. And if you have not used your own ISA allowance and might need the money yourself, your own ISA keeps the tax shelter and keeps the flexibility.

Cash or stocks and shares? The honest answer depends on how long is left. Over sixteen or eighteen years, the historic case for investing rather than holding cash is strong, and that is a long enough run to ride out bad years. Over three or four years before an eighteenth birthday, a market fall has no time to recover and cash is the more sensible place. Plenty of parents start in stocks and shares and shift towards cash in the last few years, which needs no advice and no product, just a transfer between the two.

The verdict

If you are a parent putting money aside for a child every month, the Junior ISA is the right wrapper, and the reason is the £100 rule rather than the headline £9,000 limit. An ordinary account funded by parents starts creating a tax problem at a balance of about £2,500, which £50 a month reaches in four years. The Junior ISA removes that entirely. The price is a genuine one: the money is locked until 18 and then belongs outright to an eighteen-year-old, with no conditions available to you. Decide whether you are comfortable with that before you start, not on their birthday. And if it is grandparents doing the saving and they want access, the £100 rule does not apply to them at all.

Frequently asked

Can grandparents pay into a Junior ISA? Yes. Anyone can contribute. Only a parent or guardian with parental responsibility can open and manage the account, but once it exists, grandparents, godparents and friends can all pay in, up to the same £9,000 combined annual limit.

What happens if my child already has a Child Trust Fund? You cannot hold both. GOV.UK says to ask the provider to transfer the Child Trust Fund into the Junior ISA. This is usually worth doing, and it is also worth checking whether a forgotten Child Trust Fund exists at all, because many families have one they have lost track of.

Can I take the money out if we have an emergency? No. The money belongs to the child and cannot be withdrawn before 18. GOV.UK has a separate page covering the narrow exceptions where a child is terminally ill or dies. Do not fund a Junior ISA with money you might need.

Does a Junior ISA affect benefits or student finance? It is the child's capital, not yours, so it does not count as your capital for a means-tested benefit while they are under 18. Student finance in England assesses household income rather than the student's savings, so a Junior ISA does not usually reduce a maintenance loan. Rules differ across the UK, so check your own nation.
