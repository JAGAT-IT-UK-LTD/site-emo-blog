Airbnb Takes 3% From You and Up to 16.5% From Your Guest. What Letting a Spare Room 60 Nights a Year Actually Leaves You With

Ruhul Amin · Published 5 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Letting a spare room on Airbnb is the side hustle with the most flattering headline number and the longest list of things nobody mentions until afterwards. The fee is small and clearly published. The tax rule is generous. Then there are the rules on nights, rates, mortgages and insurance, and the costs of actually having strangers in the house. Here is the whole list, and what it adds up to on an ordinary room.

The short version

  • Airbnb's host service fee is 3% of the booking subtotal on the split structure, with the guest paying a separate 14.1% to 16.5%. On the single fee structure, mandatory for hosts using property management software and some others, the host pays 15.5% and the guest nothing. Source: Airbnb Help Centre, service fees, read 4 September 2026.
  • Letting a furnished room in your own home is covered by the Rent a Room Scheme: the first £7,500 a year is tax free automatically, halved to £3,750 if the income is shared. Above it you file a return and choose the scheme or actual expenses. Source: GOV.UK.
  • In London, a whole home cannot be let as short-term accommodation for more than 90 nights in a calendar year without planning permission (Deregulation Act 2015, section 44). Elsewhere the limit is set by your council, lease and mortgage, not by statute.
  • A whole property in England available 140 nights and actually let 70 nights in a year is valued for business rates, not council tax. A room in your own home is not.
  • Your mortgage lender's consent and your home insurer's agreement are not optional, and Airbnb's AirCover for hosts is a guarantee with conditions, not an insurance policy.
  • On our arithmetic, a spare room at £55 a night for 60 nights grosses £3,300, pays £99 in host fee, and after realistic running costs nets about £2,700, or about £1,960 once the changeover hours are paid at the minimum wage, tax free under Rent a Room. Good money for the effort; not the £3,300 in the listing.

1. The fee, and which structure you are on

Airbnb charges a service fee when a booking is confirmed, and it publishes two structures. On the split fee, most hosts pay 3% of the booking subtotal, which is the nightly price plus any cleaning or extra guest fees the host sets, and the guest pays a separate service fee of 14.1% to 16.5% on top of that subtotal. The host fee is deducted before the payout. On the single fee, the whole charge is taken from the host: most pay 15.5%, others 14% to 16%, and the guest pays no service fee. Airbnb says the single fee is mandatory for traditional hospitality listings, for hosts who use property management software, and for hosts in certain countries, and that the split fee will no longer be available to certain hosts, who are being migrated.

For an ordinary UK homeowner listing one room without software, the split fee at 3% is the normal position, and it is worth confirming in the earnings screen of the app, where the service fee is shown per reservation. VAT may be added to the fee; Airbnb says the fee shown is VAT inclusive where it applies.

The two structures matter for pricing rather than for the fee alone. Under the split fee a room listed at £55 shows the guest a total of roughly £63 to £64 with their fee added; under the single fee the same £55 shows as £55 but the host receives £46.48. Hosts moved to the single fee are expected to raise their nightly price to compensate, and Airbnb has built a tool for exactly that.

2. The tax: Rent a Room, and when it does not apply

The Rent a Room Scheme lets you earn up to £7,500 a year tax free from letting furnished accommodation in your own home. The exemption is automatic if you are under the threshold, so you do not even tell HMRC. It is halved to £3,750 if you share the income with someone else, for example a partner who co-owns the house. Short lets to Airbnb guests of a room in the home you live in qualify; there is no minimum length of stay in the rule.

Above £7,500 you must complete a tax return, and you then choose between the scheme, paying tax on the excess over £7,500 with no expenses, or the normal method, paying tax on income minus actual expenses. We covered the arithmetic of that choice for lodgers in our lodger guide, and it is identical for Airbnb income.

Rent a Room does not apply if the property is not your home: a second flat, a house you have moved out of, or a converted outbuilding let as a separate unit. That income is ordinary property income. The £1,000 property allowance can cover very small amounts, but GOV.UK is explicit that the property allowance cannot be used on income from letting a room in your own home under Rent a Room, so the two do not stack. Anyone letting a whole property is in a different tax position from a room host, and the rest of this article separates the two.

3. Nights, planning and business rates

In Greater London, section 44 of the Deregulation Act 2015 allows a home to be used as temporary sleeping accommodation for up to 90 nights in a calendar year without planning permission, provided the person providing it is liable for council tax on the premises. Beyond 90 nights the use is a change of use needing permission from the borough. Airbnb enforces the limit automatically on entire home listings in London by blocking the calendar once 90 nights are booked, unless the host confirms they hold permission. The rule is about whole homes; letting a room while you live there is treated differently, but check with the borough before relying on that.

Outside London there is no statutory night cap in England, and the constraints come from the council's planning policy, which varies, and from the property's lease and mortgage. Scotland requires a short-term let licence from the council for any host, including a room in your home, and Wales has its own rules; both are outside this article's scope but are the first thing to check in either nation.

Business rates catch whole properties, not rooms. GOV.UK's test for England: a property is valued for business rates as self-catering accommodation if it is let commercially for short periods of 28 nights or less, was available for at least 140 nights in the last 12 months, was actually let for at least 70 of them, and is intended to be available for 140 nights in the next 12 months. Meet all four and you leave council tax for business rates, which for a single property with a rateable value under £15,000 may attract small business rate relief. A room in your own home does not meet the test, because it is not a self-contained unit.

4. Mortgage, lease and insurance

Most residential mortgage terms require the lender's consent before you take in paying guests, and some prohibit short lets outright. Letting without consent is a breach of the mortgage conditions, which in the worst case lets the lender call in the loan. Ask in writing before the first listing. The same applies to a leasehold flat, where the lease very often prohibits short-term letting or any business use, and a freeholder or management company can enforce it.

Home insurance is the other quiet problem. A standard policy usually excludes paying guests, so a claim arising from a guest's stay, from a broken window to a fire, can be refused. Airbnb provides AirCover for hosts, which it describes as host damage protection and host liability insurance; it comes with conditions, exclusions and a claims process run by Airbnb, and it is not a substitute for telling your own insurer. Specialist host insurance exists and costs money, and that cost belongs in the arithmetic.

Then the practical items: a lock on the guest's door and on yours, a spare set of bedding, a cleaning routine between stays, laundry, toiletries, and the electricity and heating a guest uses. None of these appears on the listing page, and all of them come out of the £55.

5. A worked example in Bristol

Rahim owns a three bedroom terrace in Bristol with his wife Sana and lets the smallest room on Airbnb at £55 a night for about 60 nights a year, mostly weekends and university events. They are on the split fee. The mortgage lender has confirmed consent in writing, and their insurer has added a paying guests endorsement for £60 a year.

Gross bookings: 60 nights at £55 is £3,300. Airbnb's host fee at 3% is £99, so payouts total £3,201 on our arithmetic. Guests paid Airbnb a further £465 to £545 in guest fees, which is why the room looked more expensive to them than £55.

Costs across the year, on our estimates: the insurance endorsement £60, laundry and cleaning products £120, replacement bedding and towels £90, toiletries and welcome basics £60, and extra electricity, hot water and heating for 60 guest nights, which we put at £2.50 a night, £150. That is £480 of running costs, and it leaves £2,721. Sana does the changeovers, about 60 hours a year, and at £12.71 an hour, the National Living Wage, that labour is worth £763. Counting it, the net is about £1,958; not counting it, about £2,700. Either way the tax is nil, because the income is shared between two people and £3,300 is under both the £7,500 and the £3,750 halved threshold each.

The room paid roughly £45 a night after costs, or £33 after paying themselves for the changeovers. That is a fair return for a room they were not using. It is also about 60% of the number in the listing, and the missing 40% is the part every guide should show and most do not.

6. The honest verdict

The fee is the smallest cost: 3% on the split structure, or 15.5% if you are moved to the single fee. Confirm which you are on before you set a price.

A room in your own home is the easy case: £7,500 tax free under Rent a Room, no business rates, and only the mortgage, lease and insurer to square. Get all three in writing first.

A whole property is a different business: 90 nights in London, the 140 and 70 night business rates test, ordinary property income, and in Scotland a licence. Do not price it like a spare room.

Frequently asked

How much does Airbnb charge hosts in the UK? 3% of the booking subtotal on the split fee structure, where guests pay 14.1% to 16.5% separately. On the single fee structure, mandatory for some hosts, the host pays 15.5% and the guest pays nothing.

Do I pay tax on Airbnb income from a spare room? Not on the first £7,500 a year, or £3,750 each if the income is shared, under the Rent a Room Scheme, provided it is furnished accommodation in the home you live in. Above that you file a return.

What is the 90 night rule? In Greater London a home cannot be used as short-term sleeping accommodation for more than 90 nights in a calendar year without planning permission, under section 44 of the Deregulation Act 2015. Airbnb blocks the calendar at 90 nights for whole-home London listings.

Will my council tax change? Not for a room in your own home. A whole property in England available for 140 nights and let for 70 in a year is valued for business rates instead of council tax.
