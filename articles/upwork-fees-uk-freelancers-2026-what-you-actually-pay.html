Upwork fees in 2026: the 20/10/5 sliding scale is gone, and most guides have not noticed

Ruhul Amin · Published 6 September 2026 · 9 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Upwork's own help page now describes a service fee of 0% to 15%, set per contract and locked before you accept it. That is a different animal from the lifetime-billings tiers every other article still quotes. Here is what it means for a UK freelancer's pricing and tax return.

The short version

  • Upwork's freelancer service fee is 0% to 15% per contract, in Upwork's own words on its help page read on 6 September 2026. It is not a flat 10% and it is not a sliding scale based on how much you have billed a client.
  • You see the exact percentage before you commit, when you submit a proposal or when a client sends an offer. Once the contract begins it is fixed and cannot change.
  • The old 20% / 10% / 5% tiers on lifetime billings per client no longer appear in Upwork's documentation. If a guide is still quoting them, it has not been checked.
  • Gross up correctly. To take home a given amount at a 15% fee you must charge 17.65% more, not 15% more. Upwork's own example works the same way: to net 20 dollars an hour at a 10% fee you charge 22.22.
  • HMRC counts the gross billed, not what lands in your bank. The fee is an allowable expense, but your turnover is the full amount the client paid.

1. What Upwork's own page says today

The primary source for this is Upwork's help article "Learn about the Freelancer Service Fee". Read on 6 September 2026, it says four things that matter.

The range. "The fee ranges from 0% to 15% per contract." Not a flat rate, and not a schedule you can look up in advance for your category.

How it is set. Upwork says it is "set based on factors that help support a balanced and competitive environment across different types of work". That is not a formula, and Upwork does not publish one. In practice it means the platform decides, and different contracts in the same account can carry different percentages.

When you find out. "You'll see the fee percentage when a client sends you an offer, or you are submitting a proposal." You can also see it in the contract details and in your transaction summaries at any time. So you are never guessing on a live job, even if you cannot predict it before you go looking.

That it then locks. "Once your contract begins, the fee is fixed and won't change." Upwork repeats this: once a proposal, offer or contract is sent, the fee is locked in for that contract's life. It can differ on the next one.

There are documented exceptions. Freelancers on Upwork Payroll pay no service fee because they are paid through a third-party staffing firm. Any Hire contracts carry no freelancer service fee. Direct Contracts, where you bring your own client to the platform, carry a reduced fee. Enterprise clients are customised, and Upwork says freelancers working with them "typically" pay 10%.

One more useful line: if money is refunded to a client, Upwork returns the service fee you paid on that amount, so you are not paying a fee on money you did not keep.

2. The numbers most guides are still quoting

Search for Upwork fees and you will find a great deal of confident writing about a tiered structure: 20% on the first 500 dollars billed to each client, 10% from 500 to 10,000 dollars, and 5% above that, all measured on lifetime billings with that one client. You will also find articles insisting the fee is a flat 10%.

Neither of those descriptions appears on Upwork's own help page as at 6 September 2026. The page describes a 0% to 15% per-contract fee and nothing else.

We are not going to tell you exactly when that changed, because Upwork does not date the change on that page and we do not publish figures we cannot source. What we will say is this: the only fee that is true for you is the one shown on your own contract details page. Go and look at it. If you have older contracts running, check each one, because they can differ.

This matters more than it sounds. Under the old tiered system, the sensible strategy was to keep clients for a long time so your fee fell from 20% to 5%. Under a per-contract system that logic disappears. A ten-year client relationship does not earn you a lower rate on a new contract. If you built your business model around grinding down through the tiers, the model no longer describes reality.

3. How to price so the fee does not come out of your pocket

Here is the mistake. You want 100 pounds. The fee is 10%. You add 10% and charge 110. The platform takes 10% of 110, which is 11, and you receive 99. You are a pound short, every time, on every job.

The correct sum is division, not addition. To take home an amount T at a fee of f, charge T divided by (1 minus f). Upwork's own worked example on the help page does exactly this: to take home 20 dollars an hour at a 10% fee you charge 22.22 dollars an hour, because 20 divided by 0.9 is 22.22.

The gap widens as the fee rises. At 5% the difference between adding and dividing is trivial. At 15% it is 2.65 percentage points of your rate, which on a 5,000 dollar project is around 130 dollars you thought you had.

Upwork's help page also notes that rounding happens, so the final figure can be a couple of cents either side of your target. Their example: 12 hours at 22.22 dollars bills the client 266.64, the 10% fee is 26.66 after rounding, and you net 239.98, two cents under the 240 you were aiming at. Not worth worrying about on one job, worth knowing about before you argue with a transaction summary.

4. Callum in Swansea: the same take-home at three different fees

Callum writes technical documentation from Swansea. He has decided he needs 45 dollars an hour in his pocket, and he has a 100-hour project on the table. Upwork prices contracts in US dollars.

Callum's take-home is identical in all three rows, to within a few pence. What changes is the number the client sees. The same freelancer, doing the same work, for the same money, is quoted at 47.37 or at 52.94 depending on a percentage he did not choose and cannot negotiate.

That is the real cost of a higher fee, and it is not the fee itself. It is that a 557 dollar difference in the client-facing price on a single project decides whether you win the job against someone whose contract happened to be priced at 5%.

There is no trick here and we are not going to pretend there is one. The only levers Callum actually has are: check the fee before accepting rather than after, gross up by dividing rather than adding, and take seriously the routes Upwork itself documents as carrying no fee or a reduced one, particularly Direct Contracts if he brings his own client to the platform.

Then there is the currency. Callum is paid in dollars and lives in pounds. The rate on the day and the cost of converting it are a second, separate deduction that has nothing to do with Upwork's service fee, and withdrawal charges vary by method. Check the current figures on your own account rather than trusting any article, including this one, on that point.

5. What HMRC counts, and it is not what lands in your bank

This is the section that costs UK freelancers real money, and it has nothing to do with the platform.

Your self-employed turnover is the gross amount the client was billed, converted to sterling. The Upwork service fee is a business expense you deduct from it. Your taxable profit is the same either way, but your declared turnover is not, and turnover is what several tests are measured against.

Take Callum's 10% row. His turnover from that project is 5,000 dollars converted to sterling, not the 4,500 that reached him. If he declares the 4,500 as his income and never mentions the fee, his profit figure happens to come out right but his turnover is understated by 500 dollars on that one job alone.

Why that matters:

  • The £1,000 trading allowance is tested on gross income, not on profit. Someone who thinks they are under it because only £950 landed in their account may actually be over it on the billed figure.
  • Turnover-based registration thresholds are measured on the gross too.
  • Platforms report to HMRC. Digital platforms send seller data to tax authorities, and the figure they report is the billed amount. If your return says something smaller, that is a mismatch HMRC can see.

The practical version: download your Upwork transaction summary rather than reading your bank statement. It shows the billed amount, the fee and the net, which is exactly the three-line record a self-employed set of accounts wants. And if crossing £1,000 of gross side income in 2025 to 2026 was new for you, the deadline to tell HMRC is 5 October 2026.

The verdict

Stop looking for Upwork's fee schedule and go and read your own contract details page, because that is the only place the number that applies to you exists. It will be somewhere between 0% and 15%, it was shown to you before you accepted, and it will not change on that contract. Price by dividing your target by one minus the fee, not by adding the fee on top, because at 15% those two sums differ by nearly three points of your rate. And whatever the platform takes, tell HMRC the gross figure and claim the fee as an expense, because the trading allowance and every turnover test are measured on what the client paid, not on what reached your bank.

Frequently asked

Can I find out my fee before I apply for a job? Not in advance as a general rule, but you will see the exact percentage when you submit a proposal and again when a client sends an offer, so you are never committing blind. Upwork does not publish a lookup table by category or by seniority.

Does keeping a client for years reduce my fee now? Not according to Upwork's current help page. The fee is set per contract, so a long relationship does not earn a lower rate on a new contract the way the old lifetime-billings tiers did. That is the single biggest practical change for established freelancers.

If I refund a client, do I get the fee back? Yes. Upwork's page says that if money is refunded to your client, either by you or through payment protection, the service fee charged on that amount is returned to you.

Is the fee charged on top of what the client pays, or taken out of it? Taken out of it. The client is charged the rate you entered, Upwork deducts the service fee percentage from that, and the remainder is yours. That is why you have to gross your rate up rather than expecting the fee to be somebody else's problem.
