Freelance Day Rate UK: How to Set Yours Without Undercharging

21 July 2026 · 8 min read · Updated 18 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026

Your freelance day rate in the UK should come from the profit you need, divided by the days you can actually bill, not from your old salary divided by 260. Most solo freelancers bill around 130 to 140 days a year, so the right rate is often close to double what the salary sum suggests. For a sense check, the median UK developer contract rate in job adverts is £500 a day in the six months to 18 November 2026, according to IT Jobs Watch.

Get the divisor wrong and you can be busy all year and still short. Get it right and the same income needs far fewer days.

The short version
  • Your day rate is not your salary divided by 260. Plan for about 200 working days and 130 to 140 billable ones.
  • Work backwards: take home you need, plus 20% income tax and 6% Class 4 National Insurance on profit between £12,570 and £50,270 in 2026 to 2027, plus your business costs, divided by billable days.
  • Our worked example, Priya in Leeds, needs at least £310 a day to take home £32,000. At £450 she reaches the same income in 94 billable days instead of 136.
  • Developer contract medians from IT Jobs Watch: £500 UK wide, £527 in London, £510 in Manchester. The London premium for this role is now only about 5%.
  • The UK median for developers was £520 two years ago. If you have not moved your rate since 2024, the market has not moved it for you either, so do not wait for it.

1. Why does dividing your salary by 260 fail?

Almost every new freelancer starts the same way. They take their old salary, divide it by 1,950 hours, get something like £18 an hour, and charge that.

Then they wonder why they are working constantly and still short at the end of the month. Three things break that sum.

You do not bill every hour you work. Quoting, invoicing, chasing payment, marketing, bookkeeping, learning and sales calls that go nowhere all take time. A common planning assumption is that 60 to 70% of your working time is billable. That is a rule of thumb, not a measured statistic, so track your own hours for three months and use your real figure.

The rest is running the business, and nobody pays you for it directly. Part of that overhead is cash rather than time, including the public liability cover clients and venues insist on.

You have no paid holiday, no sick pay and no employer pension. An employee on £35,000 costs their employer about £40,360 a year before any other benefit. That is 15% employer National Insurance on pay above £5,000, which is £4,500, plus the 3% minimum pension on qualifying earnings above £6,240, which is £862.80.

The employee also gets at least 28 days of paid leave, the legal minimum for a five day week. All of that now has to come out of your rate.

So does travel. An employee gets it reimbursed. You claim it against tax at HMRC's 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles and 25p after that.

Hourly billing punishes you for being good. If you get faster, you earn less for the same result. That is the main reason experienced freelancers move to day rates and project fees.

2. How do you calculate a freelance day rate in the UK?

Work backwards from the money you actually need, not forwards from an hourly figure. Any freelance day rate calculator worth using does these four steps.

  1. Target take home. What do you need in the bank, after tax, per year?
  2. Gross it up for tax. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland for 2026 to 2027, profit between £12,570 and £50,270 pays 20% income tax plus 6% Class 4 National Insurance. That is 26p in every pound over the allowance. Above £50,270 it becomes 40% plus 2%. Scotland has different income tax bands.
  3. Add business costs. Software, insurance, accountant, equipment, phone, travel, professional bodies and training. List your own. Priya's, below, come to £3,080.
  4. Divide by billable days, not working days. Start from 260 weekdays. Take off 28 days of holiday including bank holidays, the same as an employee's legal minimum. Take off 10 days for illness and about 22 days for training, gaps between clients and dead time. That leaves about 200 working days. At a 68% billable ratio you get roughly 136 billable days.

That last step is where nearly everyone goes wrong. Dividing by 260 instead of 136 makes your rate almost half what it needs to be.

£310 is the floor, not the target. It is the number below which Priya is working for less than she thinks.

With four years of experience and a portfolio, she decides to quote £450. At that rate she covers the same £41,910 in 94 billable days rather than 136.

That is the whole argument for pricing properly. The same income with about 30% less billable work, or the same work and a great deal more money.

Rates vary widely by trade, and what photography work actually pays in the UK is a useful benchmark for anyone pricing creative work.

5. Does London still pay more?

Less than most people assume. For developer contracts, the London median of £527 is only about 5% above the UK figure of £500.

Manchester, at £510, sits above the UK median and close to London, after a sharp rise from £425 a year earlier.

Remote work is a big part of the reason. London clients hire remote contractors at close to national rates. A freelancer in Hull or Swansea can bill a London client without London costs, which is a lasting improvement in regional earning power.

Practical takeaway: if you are outside London and pricing against local competitors, you are pricing against the wrong market. Price against the client's market, not your postcode.

6. Day rate vs hourly, project or value pricing?

Hourly. Fine for open ended support work and small one off jobs. Bad for anything with a defined outcome, because it caps your income at your available hours and rewards slowness.

Day rate. The default for design, development and consultancy. Easy for clients to budget and easy for you to forecast. Agree in writing how many hours a day means, because a half day somehow always becomes six hours.

Project fee. One price for a defined deliverable. Better margin if you are efficient, and clients generally prefer the certainty. It only works with a written scope. Without one, you have signed up for unlimited work at a fixed price.

One more deduction before you quote. If the work comes through a marketplace, the platform takes its share of the fee first, and what Upwork leaves a UK freelancer shows how much.

Value pricing. The fee reflects what the outcome is worth to the client, not the time it takes. A landing page that lifts conversion on a large revenue line is worth far more than two days of work. This is where the real money is, and it needs you to understand the client's numbers well enough to have that conversation credibly.

Whichever you use, put a revision limit in writing. Unlimited revisions is one of the quickest ways to turn a good rate into a bad one.

When a client asks for your rate before you know the scope, give a range and a condition. "Projects like this usually land between £2,500 and £4,500, depending on the number of pages and the research needed." That answers honestly without pricing blind.

7. How do you raise your rate with an existing client?

The fear is that they will leave. Some will. In practice, the ones who leave over a 10% increase are often the ones costing you most in chasing and scope creep.

  1. Give notice. Six to eight weeks. The new rate applies from a specific date, and to work commissioned after that date.
  2. State it, do not ask it. "From 1 February my day rate moves to £475." Not "would it be possible to maybe increase".
  3. Give one reason, briefly. Costs, demand, or a change in what you deliver. Do not write three paragraphs justifying yourself.
  4. Have a fallback ready. If they push back, offer to hold the old rate for work already committed, or a smaller scope at the old price. Never just cave to the old number with nothing changed.
  5. Do it every year. A rate that never moves is a rate falling in real terms every year.

8. How much tax comes off your day rate?

Your rate is revenue. Your income is what survives HMRC. These are the 2026 to 2027 figures from GOV.UK.

  • The personal allowance is £12,570. Budget 2025 froze it, and the £50,270 higher rate threshold, until 5 April 2031. Frozen thresholds mean more of each rate rise is taxed. Our guide to frozen tax thresholds shows the effect.
  • Class 4 National Insurance is 6% on profits between £12,570 and £50,270, and 2% above that.
  • Class 2 National Insurance is £3.65 a week. If your profits are £7,105 or more you do not pay it, but it is treated as paid, so your State Pension record is protected. Below that you can choose to pay it voluntarily.
  • The VAT registration threshold is £90,000 of VAT taxable turnover. Cross it and you must register. If your clients are VAT registered businesses, registering is often neutral. If they are consumers, it is effectively a 20% price rise you may not be able to pass on.
  • Payments on account catch people out in year two. If last year's bill was £1,000 or more and less than 80% was taken at source, HMRC asks for two advance payments, each half of last year's bill, due 31 January and 31 July. Our guide to the 31 January payment on account walks through it.

Put 30% of every invoice into a separate account the day it lands. Not at the end of the month. The day it lands.

Whether to stay a sole trader or set up a company is a separate question, covered in sole trader vs limited company. IR35 matters too if you contract through your own company, and what off payroll status actually turns on explains it. If agency work is on the table, read the umbrella company against agency PAYE comparison before you sign anything.

Professional indemnity insurance is often required in client contracts. Prices depend on your trade and cover level, so get quotes and add the cost to your business costs before you set your rate, not after.

Myths that cost people money

"I will start low and raise it once I have reviews." Rates are far harder to raise than to set. The client who hired you at £150 a day anchored on £150 and will resist £400 for a long time.

Start where you intend to be. If you must discount, do it deliberately, so the discount is visible and temporary.

"Charging more means I will get no work." The number of enquiries usually falls. But a very low price can signal inexperience to the buyers with real budgets. What often happens is fewer, better clients.

"My rate should match what other freelancers charge." Other freelancers have different costs, different tax positions and different billable ratios. Their number tells you about the market ceiling. It tells you nothing about your floor.

The honest bottom line

If you take one thing from this, take the divisor. Two hundred working days, not 260. Around 136 billable days, not 200. Everything else is refinement.

And a plain warning: freelancing is not automatically better paid than employment.

At £310 a day for 136 days, Priya takes home about £32,000. An employee in England on a salary of about £39,550 takes home the same, after 20% income tax and 8% employee National Insurance, before pension. And the employee also gets paid holiday, sick pay and at least a 3% employer pension.

Freelancing starts to pay properly when your rate sits well above your floor, as Priya's £450 does. If the market will only pay you close to your floor, a salary is often the better deal. That is not a popular thing to say, and it is still true.

Frequently asked questions about freelance day rates

How do I calculate my freelance day rate in the UK? Add your target take home, the income tax and National Insurance on it, and your yearly business costs. Divide the total by your billable days, which for most solo freelancers is around 130 to 140 a year. Then compare the result with market rates for your role and adjust upwards, never below the floor.

How many hours is a freelance day rate? There is no legal definition, so it is whatever your contract says. Many freelancers and clients assume seven and a half or eight hours. Write the number of hours into every contract, and say what happens when a day runs longer, so a day rate does not quietly become unpaid overtime.

How do I convert a day rate to an annual salary? Multiply the day rate by your billable days, not by 260, then take off business costs, income tax and National Insurance. Priya's £310 a day over 136 days is £41,910 of revenue and about £32,000 take home, the same as an employee salary of about £39,550 before pension.

Should I put my rates on my website? A starting from figure filters out the wrong enquiries and saves you hours of calls. Exact prices for complex work can cost you room to negotiate. A range, or a from price with what it includes, is the usual middle ground and tells serious clients they can afford you.

Is it worth going limited instead of sole trader? It can be at higher profits, but it depends on your income, how you pay yourself and whether IR35 applies to your contracts. Since dividend tax and corporation tax rules change often, run your own numbers with an accountant. This article is general information, not financial or tax advice.
