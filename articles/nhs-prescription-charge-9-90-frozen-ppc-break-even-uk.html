Twelve. That Is the Whole Decision on Whether a Prescription Certificate Is Worth Buying

1. What is frozen, and where it applies

The prescription charge in England stands at £9.90 per item. Note the wording: per item, not per prescription. A single piece of paper with three medicines on it costs £29.70.

Prepayment certificate prices are £32.05 for three months and £114.50 for twelve months. Those are the figures published by the NHS Business Services Authority, checked in September 2026.

This matters only in England. Prescriptions are free at the point of dispensing in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, so there is no decision to make and no certificate to buy. The freeze is relief rather than a saving. It does not put money in your pocket. It means one of the few household costs that could have gone up this year did not.

Check the arithmetic rather than taking it from us. The break-even on the 12 month certificate is £114.50 divided by £9.90, which is 11.57 items, so the twelfth item of the year is the one that pays for the certificate. On the three month certificate it is £32.05 divided by £9.90, which is 3.24 items, so the fourth item inside three months does the same job. NHSBSA publishes the same thing from the other end: two items a month saves £123 a year, three saves more than £241 and four saves more than £360, which lines up with the table above to the penny.

The honest part, which the pharmacy poster never prints. A PPC is worth nothing at all to most people who look at one. If you are over 60, under 16, 16 to 18 in full time education, pregnant or within a year of giving birth, on a qualifying benefit with the right exemption, or holding an HC2 certificate, you already pay nothing and a PPC would be £114.50 spent on a thing you do not need. If you are in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland, prescriptions are free and none of this applies. And if you take a single repeat item with nothing else in the year, the certificate saves £4.30, which is real but is not a plan. The people it genuinely changes are those on two or more regular items in England, and for them the saving is larger than almost anything else on a household budget for the same five minutes of effort.

The three month certificate at £32.05. Three items cost 3 × £9.90 = £29.70, which is less than £32.05, so paying per item wins. Four items cost £39.60, so the certificate saves £7.55. The turning point is the fourth item.

The twelve month certificate at £114.50. Eleven items cost 11 × £9.90 = £108.90, which is less than £114.50, so paying per item wins. Twelve items cost £118.80, so the certificate saves £4.30. The turning point is the twelfth item.

Once you are past those points the gap widens quickly, because the certificate price stops moving and the per item cost does not.

The certificate can be paid in 10 monthly Direct Debit instalments, which works out at £11.45 a month. That option is only available for the 12 month certificate and only online, not over a pharmacy counter. For anyone on two items a month it is the obvious route, because £11.45 a month replaces £19.80 a month.

A certificate is not a discount scheme. It is a cap. Past twelve items the NHS stops charging you and that is the entire benefit.

3. The HRT certificate, which beats both

There is a separate certificate for hormone replacement therapy. It costs £19.80 for twelve months, which is deliberately set at the price of two prescription items, and it covers certain HRT medicines for the whole year.

The useful part is that you can hold it alongside paying normally for anything else. The NHSBSA gives this example: four items in a three month period, three of them HRT medicines covered by the certificate. Buy the HRT certificate for £19.80 and pay £9.90 for the other item and you spend £29.70, which is less than the £32.05 three month general certificate, and it lasts a year rather than three months.

If a meaningful share of your items are HRT, run that comparison before buying a general certificate. It is easy to buy the wrong one.

Worked example: Priya Kanani in Leicester

Priya is 41, works full time, and picks up two items every month, one inhaler and one long term medicine. She has been paying at the counter for three years.

What she pays now: 24 items a year at £9.90 is £237.60.

With a 12 month certificate: £114.50. She saves £123.10 a year, and by paying the 10 instalment Direct Debit she never faces the £114.50 in one go. Her monthly outlay falls from £19.80 to £11.45.

The mistake she nearly made: buying a three month certificate first to try it. At two items a month that costs £32.05 to save £27.35 over the quarter, when the annual one saves £123.10 over the same items. The short certificate suits short courses, not ongoing conditions.

What she did first, correctly: checked the NHS eligibility tool. She is not exempt, so the certificate is the right answer. Had she been exempt, buying anything would have been wasted money.

4. Check you should be paying at all

Before buying anything, run the NHSBSA eligibility checker. Free prescriptions in England cover a long list of situations, including certain medical exemption certificates, maternity exemption certificates, and people receiving qualifying benefits. The Pension Credit route in particular is missed constantly, because people assume they are just above the line and never check.

Two practical points if you do pay by mistake.

Ask for form FP57 at the counter, at the moment you pay. You cannot get one later, and without it there is no refund. You then have three months from paying to claim.

If you become entitled to free prescriptions while holding a certificate, you may be able to get a refund of the certificate fee. The NHSBSA sets the arrangements out in its HC11 booklet.

5. The honest verdict

For anyone in England on one repeat item a month, a certificate saves £4.30 a year, which is barely worth the form. For anyone on two or more, it is one of the cleanest savings available: £123.10 a year at two items, with no catch, no comparison site and no risk.

The three sentences worth remembering: twelve items a year is the break-even. Pay by the 10 instalment Direct Debit if you can. And check whether you are exempt before you buy anything at all, because the best price is still nothing.

Frequently asked

Does a certificate cover any medicine? A general prepayment certificate covers all your NHS prescriptions in England for the period, whatever they are and however many.

Can I buy one at a pharmacy? Yes, some pharmacies sell them, but the 10 instalment Direct Debit option is online only. Paying in full is available either way.

What if I need it today? You can receive a digital certificate by email. Contact the NHSBSA to switch to digital, otherwise a paper certificate takes up to 10 working days.

Is the £9.90 charge going up soon? The government has said it is freezing prescription charges. Charges are set annually, so check the NHSBSA page before assuming the figure still holds in a new financial year.
