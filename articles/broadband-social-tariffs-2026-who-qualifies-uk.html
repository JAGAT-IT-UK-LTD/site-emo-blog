Seven in Ten Households That Could Have a Cheaper Broadband Deal Do Not Know It Exists

1. The size of the gap

Ofcom's pricing and consumer engagement research puts 532,000 customers on a fixed or mobile broadband social tariff as at June 2025, up 26,000, or 5%, over the year.

Growth of 5% sounds healthy until you see it against the denominator. Take-up is under 9% of eligible households. In October 2025, 70% of eligible households were unaware social tariffs existed.

Put those two figures next to each other and the diagnosis is obvious. This is not a scheme people are rejecting. It is a scheme people have never been told about. For comparison, the equivalent support in the energy sector reaches a far higher share of those entitled to it, largely because energy support is signposted relentlessly and telecoms support is not.

The supply side has moved. Broadband social tariff options grew from three in 2020 to more than 30 by December 2025. There are now plenty of them. Almost nobody knows.

Thirty tariffs, half a million takers, and seven in ten eligible households who have never heard the phrase. The problem was never the product.

2. What a social tariff is, and the second saving

A social tariff is a cheaper broadband or mobile package offered by a provider to customers on qualifying benefits. It is a normal service on a discounted price, not a stripped down or throttled one, though speeds and allowances vary between offers as you would expect at a lower price.

The headline discount is the obvious saving. The second one is quieter and worth as much over a couple of years: most social tariffs are exempt from mid-contract price rises. Everyone else on a standard package absorbs an annual increase inside their contract. On a social tariff, in most cases, the price you agreed is the price you keep.

The wider rules have changed here too. Under commitments providers gave, April 2026 was the last time inflation-linked in-contract price rises applied. Increases now have to be set out in pounds and pence before you sign, which at least makes them comparable. Providers also committed to making social tariffs better signposted and easier to access, which is the commitment this article exists to test.

3. Who qualifies, and how it is checked

Eligibility is set by each provider, but the common route is receipt of a qualifying benefit. Universal Credit is the one most schemes are built around, and many providers also accept Pension Credit, Employment and Support Allowance, Jobseeker's Allowance and Income Support. Some go wider.

The check is usually automatic. Providers verify eligibility against benefit records rather than asking you to post documents, so the application is typically short. That is worth knowing because the assumption that it will be a paperwork ordeal is one reason people do not start.

Two practical points that decide whether this works for you.

Availability depends on the network at your address, not just on the provider. A social tariff you find online may not be available on your line. Check by postcode.

You do not have to wait for your contract to end. Many providers allow a move to a social tariff mid-contract without an exit fee. It is not universal and it is rarely advertised, so ask the question directly rather than assuming you are locked in until renewal.

Worked example: the Mensah household in Luton

Two adults and a teenager. One adult works part time, the household receives Universal Credit. They have had the same broadband package for four years and it has been repriced at renewal twice.

What they believe: that support with bills means energy, and that broadband is just a bill you pay. Nobody at the provider has ever mentioned an alternative, and they are in the 70% Ofcom measured.

What is actually available to them: a choice from more than 30 social tariffs, subject to what is available at their postcode, on the strength of the Universal Credit award they already have. The eligibility check is against benefit records, so there is very little for them to do.

The saving they are not counting: the exemption from mid-contract rises. Their current package has been increased inside the contract term twice. On most social tariffs that does not happen, so the gap between the two prices widens every year rather than staying flat.

What is stopping them: one phone call they have never made. There is no form, no means test beyond the benefit they already receive, and in many cases no exit fee for moving now rather than at renewal.

4. How to move, in four steps

One. Check by postcode, not by brand. Ofcom publishes a list of social tariffs and which providers offer them. Availability at your address is the constraint that matters.

Two. Ring your current provider first and use the words social tariff. If they have one and you qualify, moving within the same provider is usually the least disruptive route. If they do not, that answer tells you to look elsewhere.

Three. Ask two specific questions. Is there an exit fee for moving to the social tariff mid-contract, and is this tariff exempt from in-contract price rises. Both answers change the value of the deal and neither is usually volunteered.

Four. Compare the speed you actually need, not the highest number. A cheaper tariff that streams and video calls fine is a saving. A cheaper tariff that cannot handle the household is a false economy, and switching back costs time.

5. The honest verdict

This is one of the few pieces of household support where the scheme works, the products exist, more than 30 of them, and the only broken part is that people are not told.

If your household receives a qualifying benefit, this is worth one phone call and ten minutes. The discount is real, the exemption from mid-contract rises compounds it, and the eligibility check is done against records you have already given the state.

If you do not receive a qualifying benefit, this is not for you and no amount of asking will change that. Say so plainly rather than pretending otherwise. In that case the useful move is a normal comparison at renewal, where the new pounds and pence disclosure rules at least make deals comparable for the first time.

Frequently asked

Do I lose my speed if I move to a social tariff? Not necessarily. Packages vary, and some social tariffs offer speeds comparable to entry level standard packages. Check the specific offer rather than assuming.

Can I get one if I am mid-contract? In many cases yes, and often without an exit fee. It is not guaranteed across every provider, so ask before you assume you are stuck.

Is there a mobile version? Yes. Ofcom's figure of 532,000 covers fixed and mobile broadband social tariffs together, and several mobile providers offer them.

Why did nobody tell me? Ofcom's own research found 70% of eligible households were unaware in October 2025. Providers have committed to better signposting, which is a fair measure of how bad it has been.
