Marriage Allowance: £252 a year, backdated four, and the income line that decides it

Ruhul Amin · Published 6 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026

One of you transfers £1,260 of Personal Allowance to the other. Their tax bill falls by up to £252. The word doing the work in that sentence is up to, and GOV.UK's own worked example quietly shows why.

Share Facebook X WhatsApp Pinterest LinkedIn

The short version

  • The lower earner transfers £1,260 of their Personal Allowance to their husband, wife or civil partner, which cuts the higher earner's tax by up to £252 a year (GOV.UK, page updated 2 September 2026).
  • You only get the whole £252 if the lower earner's income is £11,310 or less. Above that they start paying tax themselves on the allowance they gave away, and the couple's net gain falls.
  • The higher earner must be a basic rate taxpayer, so income between £12,571 and £50,270. In Scotland it is the starter, basic or intermediate rate, £12,571 to £43,662.
  • You can backdate to 6 April 2022. A first claim made now can cover four earlier tax years at £252 each plus the current year, so up to £1,260 in total.
  • It is free to claim on GOV.UK. Companies that offer to do it for a share of the refund keep a large slice of money you could have had in full for ten minutes of typing.

1. What actually moves, and what you get back

Everybody gets a Personal Allowance, the slice of income you can earn before Income Tax starts. It is £12,570 and it has been frozen at that figure for years. If one partner in a married couple or civil partnership does not use all of theirs, that unused slice is simply wasted.

Marriage Allowance lets the lower earner hand over a fixed £1,260 of it. Note that it is a fixed amount, not "whatever you have spare". The higher earner then has £1,260 less income taxed at 20%, which is a saving of £252.

The mechanics matter for when you see the money:

  • For the current tax year, HMRC changes the higher earner's tax code, so the £252 arrives as slightly larger take-home pay spread over the rest of the year rather than as a cheque.
  • For earlier years, it comes as a repayment, usually by bank transfer or cheque.
  • It then repeats automatically every year until one of you cancels it. That is convenient and it is also the trap covered in section three.

One thing it is not: it is not a payment for being married. It is your own unused allowance being moved to where it can do some good.

2. Who can claim, and who cannot

GOV.UK sets three conditions, all of which must hold.

You are married or in a civil partnership. Living together does not qualify, however long you have done it. This is the single most common reason a claim fails.

The lower earner does not pay Income Tax, or has income below the Personal Allowance, usually £12,570.

The higher earner pays basic rate tax, which usually means income between £12,571 and £50,270. In Scotland the test is different: your partner must pay the starter, basic or intermediate rate, so roughly £12,571 to £43,662.

Things that do not stop a claim, which is worth knowing because people assume otherwise:

  • Either of you receiving a pension.
  • Either of you living abroad, as long as you still get a UK Personal Allowance.
  • The lower earner having no income at all.
  • Your partner having died since 5 April 2022. You can still claim, by phoning the Income Tax helpline. If it was your partner who was the lower earner, the person handling their tax affairs makes the call.

Two genuine blocks. If either of you was born before 6 April 1935, Married Couple's Allowance is usually worth more, and you cannot have both. And if the higher earner has income above £50,270 they are a higher rate taxpayer and the claim is not available at all, however close to the line they are.

3. Why most couples get less than £252

This is the part the headlines skip, and GOV.UK's own example gives it away if you read the numbers rather than the summary.

Their example: your income is £11,500 and your partner's is £20,000. You transfer £1,260. Your own Personal Allowance drops from £12,570 to £11,310. Your income of £11,500 is now £190 above your reduced allowance, so you start paying tax on £190, which is £38 at 20%. Your partner saves the full £252. The couple is £214 better off, not £252.

So the rule underneath the rule is this: the lower earner needs income of £11,310 or less for the couple to get the whole £252. Between £11,310 and £12,570 the gain shrinks by 20p for every extra pound of the lower earner's income.

Lower earner's incomeTax they now payCouple's net gain
£0 to £11,310£0£252
£11,500£38£214
£12,000£138£114
£12,570£252£0

Read the last row honestly: if the lower earner is right at £12,570, the claim is worth precisely nothing. It is not a loss, but it is not a gain either, and it is not worth the admin.

This is also why the automatic renewal deserves a note in your diary. If the lower earner picks up more hours and their income climbs through £12,570, the transfer keeps running and the couple can end up marginally worse off, because the lower earner is now taxed on income the allowance would have covered. Cancelling is straightforward, but nobody sends you a letter telling you to.

4. Backdating: four years plus this one

GOV.UK says you can backdate a claim to 6 April 2022, which is the start of the 2022 to 2023 tax year. Claiming in September 2026 therefore covers:

Tax yearWorthHow it is paid
2022 to 2023up to £252repayment
2023 to 2024up to £252repayment
2024 to 2025up to £252repayment
2025 to 2026up to £252repayment
2026 to 2027up to £252tax code change

Up to £1,260 in total, and it is the same £252 in every one of those years because the Personal Allowance has been frozen at £12,570 throughout. You have to have been eligible in each year you are claiming for, and HMRC will check.

Now the warning that justifies this section existing. Search for "marriage allowance" and you will find companies offering to handle the claim. They are legal, they are not HMRC, and they typically keep a substantial percentage of the refund, sometimes with an authorisation that routes future refunds to them as well. The claim takes about ten minutes on GOV.UK, needs your National Insurance numbers and a form of ID, and costs nothing. There is no version of this where paying someone a cut is the sensible move.

5. Aisha and Daniel in Shrewsbury

Aisha works three days a week in a school office and earns £10,800. Daniel earns £34,000 as a warehouse supervisor. They married in 2019 and have never claimed.

Aisha transfers £1,260. Her own Personal Allowance falls to £11,310, which is still above her £10,800 of income, so she pays no tax at all. Nothing is lost on her side.

Daniel's taxable income falls by £1,260 and his tax bill falls by £252. Because Aisha's income has never been above £11,310 in any of the relevant years, they get the full amount every time.

Their claim in September 2026 is worth £1,008 as a repayment for 2022 to 2023 through 2025 to 2026, plus £252 for the current year delivered through Daniel's tax code. £1,260 in total, from one online form.

What would change the answer: if Aisha took on a fourth day and her income went to £13,500, she would be a taxpayer herself, the couple would no longer qualify, and the transfer would need cancelling. If Daniel were promoted past £50,270, the claim would end too. Neither of those things happens automatically in HMRC's system, so the responsibility to tell them is yours.

The verdict

If one of you earns under £11,310 and the other is a basic rate taxpayer, claim it. It is ten minutes for up to £1,260 and it renews itself. If the lower earner is between £11,310 and £12,570 it is still worth doing but it is worth less than the headline, and you should work out your own figure from the table above rather than expecting £252. If the lower earner is at or above £12,570, or the higher earner is above £50,270, this does not help you at all. And whatever you do, claim it yourself on GOV.UK rather than paying anyone a share of your own money.

Frequently asked

What if we are not married but have lived together for years? You do not qualify. GOV.UK is explicit that you cannot claim Marriage Allowance if you are living together but are not married or in a civil partnership. There is no long-term cohabitation equivalent in the Income Tax system.

Which of us has to make the claim? The lower earner, because it is their allowance being transferred. If it turns out the wrong person applied, HMRC will usually sort it out, but it is quicker to get it right first time.

Will this affect Universal Credit, tax credits or benefits? Marriage Allowance changes how much Income Tax is paid, not how much income you have, so it does not reduce a means-tested award. A backdated repayment is a one-off sum of your own money returned, though a large lump sum sitting in a bank account can affect capital limits on some benefits, so keep a note of what it was.

Can I claim if my partner has died? Yes, if they died on or after 6 April 2022. GOV.UK asks you to phone the Income Tax helpline rather than apply online. If your late partner was the lower earner, the person responsible for their tax affairs needs to make the call.
