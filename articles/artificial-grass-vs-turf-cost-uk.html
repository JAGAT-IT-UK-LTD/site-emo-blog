Artificial Grass Does Not Compete With Your Lawn. It Competes With Your Gardener

Ruhul Amin · Published 15 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Over ten years on a 40 square metre garden, real turf you mow yourself costs about 1,090 pounds and artificial grass costs about 2,600. The arithmetic only flips when somebody else is being paid to do the mowing, and then it flips hard.

The short version

  • Turf supplied and laid: 8 to 14 pounds a square metre, so 320 to 560 pounds for a 40 square metre garden.
  • Artificial grass supplied and installed: 45 to 75 pounds a square metre including the groundworks, so 1,800 to 3,000 pounds for the same garden.
  • Ten year total, mowing it yourself: turf about 1,090 pounds, artificial about 2,600. Real grass wins by a wide margin.
  • Ten year total, paying a gardener: turf about 5,940 pounds, artificial about 2,600. Artificial pays back the difference in about 3.7 years.
  • Nobody quotes the end. Lifting and disposing of artificial grass at 12 to 15 years costs 400 to 800 pounds and most of it cannot be recycled kerbside.

1. What each one actually costs to put down

Take a 40 square metre lawn, which is a fairly typical small back garden at roughly 8 metres by 5.

Real turf supplied and laid costs 8 to 14 pounds a square metre depending on the grade and on how much preparation the ground needs. Call it 11 pounds for a middle figure, so 440 pounds. That assumes the ground is reasonably level and the old surface is coming up but does not need excavating deeply.

Artificial grass costs 45 to 75 pounds a square metre supplied and installed, and the installation is most of it. The grass itself is 15 to 30 pounds a square metre. On top of that come the excavation of 75 to 100 millimetres of soil, the disposal of that soil, a compacted MOT type one sub base, a sharp sand or granite dust blinding layer, a geotextile membrane, timber or composite edging, and the jointing tape and adhesive. Call it 60 pounds a square metre, so 2,400 pounds.

That is a five to one difference on day one, and nobody disputes it. The case for artificial grass has never been the installation price. It is everything that happens afterwards.

2. The running costs, which is where it is decided

A real lawn in a British garden needs cutting roughly 22 times a year, from March to October, with the interval stretching in high summer and in a dry spell.

If you do it yourself, the honest annual cost is: a mower at about 180 pounds lasting eight years, so 22 pounds a year, electricity or petrol at about 8 pounds, feed and moss treatment at about 25 pounds, and an allowance of 10 pounds for watering a newly laid area or a drought year. That is roughly 65 pounds a year, and about twelve hours of your time.

If somebody else does it, the going rate for a small garden cut and tidy is 20 to 30 pounds a visit. At 22 visits and 25 pounds that is 550 pounds a year.

Artificial grass is not maintenance free, whatever the brochure says. It needs brushing up every few weeks so the pile stays vertical, washing down where pets use it, moss treatment on the shaded side, and the sand infill topping up every few years. Realistically 20 pounds a year and about two hours.

So the annual gap is 45 pounds if you mow, and 530 pounds if you pay. Those two numbers produce completely different answers and that is the whole article.

Read the top three rows together. Against a lawn you mow yourself, artificial grass costs about 1,510 pounds more over ten years and never gets it back. Against a gardener, it costs 3,340 pounds less, and the extra 1,960 pounds of installation is repaid by the 530 pounds a year of avoided mowing in about 3.7 years.

The fourth row is the one nobody considers and it is the cheapest per year on the table. Gravel over a membrane on a small garden is genuinely low maintenance, costs a fraction of artificial grass and does not have to be sent to landfill in fifteen years. It is not a lawn, and for some households that is the objection. For others it has never occurred to them.

4. The end of life cost nobody puts in the quote

Artificial grass has a working life of about twelve to fifteen years before the pile lies flat, the colour goes and the joints start lifting. At that point somebody has to take it up.

That is real work. The grass is bonded at the joints, pinned at the edges and full of sand infill, and it comes up in heavy strips that have to be cut and carried. Expect 400 to 800 pounds to lift and dispose of a 40 square metre installation, and more if the sub base is coming out too.

Disposal is the harder part. Most artificial grass is a polypropylene or polyethylene pile on a latex or polyurethane backing, and that combination is not accepted by ordinary kerbside recycling or by most household waste sites as recyclable. In practice it goes to general waste. Some manufacturers now offer take back schemes and it is a fair question to ask before you buy.

Anybody removing it for you must be a registered waste carrier. Check the register on GOV.UK. If waste traced back to you is fly tipped, the duty of care sits with you, not only with the person who dumped it.

There is also the drainage point. A properly installed artificial lawn drains through the membrane into the sub base, which works fine until it does not. A badly installed one on clay, or one laid over an existing patio, creates a surface that sheds water somewhere else. That is worth raising with the installer in writing. If the plan is to lose the lawn altogether rather than fake it, the hard surface sum is a separate one: what a patio costs against decking over ten years.

5. Worked example: the Pemberton garden in Solihull

Ruth Pemberton is 71 and has a 38 square metre back lawn in Solihull. She stopped mowing it herself three years ago and has been paying a gardener 24 pounds a visit, roughly 21 visits a year, which is 504 pounds a year.

She gets two quotes for artificial grass. 2,280 pounds and 2,650 pounds, both including excavation, sub base and disposal of the turf.

On her numbers the arithmetic is straightforward. The cheaper quote is 2,280 pounds against 504 pounds a year of gardening she would stop paying, less about 20 pounds a year of brushing and washing. That is a net saving of 484 pounds a year and a payback of 4.7 years.

She is not going to live there forever and she says so, which is the right thing to put into the sum. On a ten year horizon she saves about 2,560 pounds. On a four year horizon she saves nothing and has spent 2,280 pounds.

What decides it is that she still wants the gardener for the borders, at eight visits a year rather than twenty one. That drops the gardening bill to 192 pounds rather than removing it, so the real saving is 292 pounds a year and the payback stretches to 7.8 years.

She goes ahead anyway, for a reason that is not in any table: she had stopped using the garden in wet weather because the lawn turned to mud. That is a legitimate reason to spend 2,280 pounds and it is not a payback. Being honest about which of the two is driving the decision is the useful part.

Frequently asked

Does it get hot in summer? Yes, noticeably. Dark infill and a south facing garden can put the surface well above air temperature on a hot day, which matters if you have young children or a dog that lies on it. Hosing it down cools it quickly but that is a thing you have to remember to do.

Is it better for a dog? Mixed. It does not turn to mud and it does not get dug up, which is the case for it. It also holds urine odour in the infill unless it is washed regularly and installed with a free draining base, which is the case against. Ask specifically about a permeable backing and a drainage layer if you have a dog.

Do I need permission? Not usually in a private back garden. Some leases and new build estate covenants restrict it, and front gardens can raise drainage questions with the local authority if you are covering a large area. Check your deeds before ordering.

What about the wildlife argument? It is a real one. A plastic lawn over a membrane supports nothing, where even a badly kept real lawn feeds worms, which feed birds. That is not a cost you can put in a table, and for some households it settles the question on its own.

The honest verdict

If you mow your own lawn, do not buy artificial grass. Over ten years it costs about 1,510 pounds more than turf and returns nothing but time you were spending anyway.

If you pay a gardener 500 pounds a year to mow, it pays back in under four years and it is a sound purchase. And if what you actually want is a low maintenance garden rather than a green rectangle, gravel over a membrane does the same job for a quarter of the money and does not need landfilling in 2041.
