How to Ask for a Pay Rise in the UK: The Number to Ask For in 2026

Ruhul Amin · Published 21 August 2026 · Updated 23 November 2026 · 10 min read · Figures checked November 2026

To ask for a pay rise in the UK, ask before pay is set, bring one page of evidence, and name a specific figure above your employer's budget of around 3%. That matters because CPI inflation is 3.6%, so the standard offer this year is a small real terms cut, not a rise.

The short version

  • UK regular pay rose 3.5% in the year to July to September 2026, and total pay 3.6%, according to the ONS. After CPIH inflation, regular pay grew just 0.2% in real terms.
  • The median pay award was 3.2% in the three months to October 2026, says Brightmine. Employers expect awards of around 3% in 2027.
  • Inflation is now higher than the typical award. CPI was 3.6% and CPIH 3.7% in the year to October 2026. A 3% rise is a real terms cut.
  • The sector gap is large. Public sector regular pay grew 5.4% against 3.1% in the private sector. Finance and business services grew 3.5%.
  • Ask above the budget, not just above inflation. If your employer has budgeted 3%, asking for 3.5% is a rounding argument. A specific market figure with evidence is a different conversation.
  • The strongest case is a change in your job, not a change in prices. Nobody is persuaded by your bills. They are persuaded by what you now do that you did not do before.

What is the average pay rise in the UK in 2026?

The latest official figure is 3.5% for regular pay, in the year to July to September 2026. That comes from the ONS release of 17 November 2026.

Pay awards, the formal rises employers agree, are running a little lower. Here is the full picture.

Take two things from that table before you walk into any meeting.

Your employer almost certainly has a number in mind, and it is close to 3%. That is what pay budgets are being built around.

Where you work changes the odds. Public sector pay is growing far faster than private sector pay, partly because of when awards land. That is worth knowing when you decide whether to negotiate or to move.

The hiring market has cooled too. The ONS puts vacancies at an early estimate of 690,000 for August to October 2026, down 22,000 on the quarter. Our 2026 jobs market and pay rise guide works through what that means for your leverage.

Why is a 3% pay rise not really a rise?

CPI inflation was 3.6% in the year to October 2026, and CPIH was 3.7%. A 3% award does not keep up with either.

Even the average pay growth of 3.5% left regular pay just 0.2% ahead in real terms, on the ONS's own CPIH measure. Within measurement error, that is standing still.

This changes the whole conversation. If you are offered 3%, you are being offered slightly less buying power than last year.

A rise also moves two numbers that never appear on the payslip. Your pension provider claims the first 20% and almost nobody claims the second, so crossing into higher rate makes a claim worth making.

The income tax thresholds are frozen until April 2031, after the Budget of 28 October 2026. So more of any rise can be pulled into a higher band. Our guide to frozen tax thresholds shows how that works.

If the conversation goes the other way entirely, the £30,000 tax free rule covers less of a redundancy payment than the headline implies. And if a court order already takes a slice of your wages, a rise moves that too, because an attachment of earnings is worked out on what you earn each pay period, not on a fixed amount.

So separate two things, in your head and in the meeting. The cost of living adjustment keeps you level, and it is not a reward. The rise is the part above inflation, and that is what you are negotiating for.

Employers who blur the two are not being dishonest. It is how the budget is presented. It is your job to unbundle it.

If your pay sits near the legal floor, the National Living Wage rises from £12.71 to £13.21 an hour on 1 April 2027. Our guide to the April 2027 National Living Wage decision covers who gets it, and what a £12.71 payslip should show covers the current rate.

With inflation at 3.6% and the typical award at 3.2%, accepting the standard offer means taking a small pay cut and calling it a rise.

How much should I ask for?

The most common mistake is asking for a figure just above the budget. It invites a split down the middle and lands you back at the budget.

  • Find the market rate for your role, in your region, at your level. Use real job adverts for your job today, not a national salary survey. Five adverts is enough. If you are early in your career, the comparison that sets your floor is graduate scheme pay against apprenticeship pay.
  • If you are below market, ask to be brought to market and say so plainly. This is the strongest position, because it is a fact rather than an opinion, and losing you costs more than fixing it.
  • If you are already at market, you need a different argument. That argument is scope: you are doing more than the job you were hired for.
  • Ask for a specific number, not a range. A range is heard as its lowest value.
  • Aim clearly above the budget. With budgets near 3%, an ask of 6 to 12% backed by evidence starts a real conversation. A much bigger jump without a role change is usually a new job title, and it may be worth asking for that instead.

How do I build the case on one page?

Your manager may have to take this to someone else. So write it down and make it easy to forward.

  1. What you were hired to do, in one line.
  2. What you do now, and what has been added since. Scope is the whole argument.
  3. Three outcomes with numbers. Money saved, revenue brought in, time reduced, errors cut, people trained. Anything measurable beats anything adjectival.
  4. The market evidence. Three or four current adverts for equivalent roles, with their advertised salaries.
  5. The ask. One number, and the date you would like it to start.

Leave out entirely: your rent, your energy bill, your mortgage, what a colleague earns, and how long you have been there.

Length of service is the weakest argument in the room. Personal costs, however real, are not something your manager can take to a budget holder.

Pay is also decided for you in the weeks you are legally away from work. Two guides are worth reading before you plan a year: the 18 weeks of unpaid parental leave and who can take them, and what jury service pays when your employer does not top it up.

When is the best time to ask for a pay rise?

The same request succeeds or fails depending on when it lands in the budget cycle.

  • Ask before the budget is set, not after. Many organisations decide pay months before it takes effect. By the time you hear your increase, the money is allocated and your manager may not be able to change it.
  • Find out when your organisation sets pay, and open the conversation a month before that. Your manager will usually tell you if you ask directly.
  • Ask after a visible win, while the evidence is fresh in everyone's mind.
  • Do not ask in the week of bad results, during a redundancy process, or in the same conversation as a complaint.
  • Book a specific meeting for it. Raising it at the end of a one to one gives it five rushed minutes. A meeting called "career and pay" gives your manager time to prepare, and unprepared managers tend to say no.

How do I ask for a pay rise in the meeting?

It is shorter than people expect. Fifteen minutes is normal.

  1. Say why you are there in the first sentence. "I would like to talk about my salary" removes the awkward preamble that costs you confidence.
  2. Give the case, not the feelings. Scope, three outcomes with numbers, market evidence. Two or three minutes.
  3. State the number, then stop talking. The silence after your number is the most important moment. People fill it by negotiating against themselves. Do not.
  4. Expect not to get an answer in the room. Few managers can approve on the spot. "When do you think you can come back to me on this?" is the right closing question.
  5. Follow up in writing the same day. A short email with the one page attached, so it can be forwarded.

Kavita, 33, marketing executive in Birmingham on £31,000. Private sector.

Her position. The standard offer this year is 3%, which is £930. That is below CPI inflation of 3.6%, so it is a small real terms cut.

Five current adverts for equivalent roles in her city advertise £34,000 to £38,000. She is below market. Knowing that is easier now that more employers publish a pay band with the advert.

What she asks for. £36,000, a 16% increase. Large as a percentage, but it is the market midpoint, not a wish. She frames it that way: "I am asking to be brought in line with the market for this role."

Her evidence. Scope: she took on the email programme in January, which was an agency contract costing £900 a month. Outcomes: email revenue up 22% since she took it over, agency cost of £10,800 a year removed, and two juniors trained on the platform.

The outcome. Her manager cannot approve £36,000. Two weeks later she is offered £34,500, an 11.3% increase, with a review in six months.

What that is worth. The standard 3% would have taken her to £31,930. The negotiated figure is £2,570 a year more than that, before tax.

Why it worked. Not confidence, and not the phrasing. She removed £10,800 of cost, more than the £3,500 she gained, and showed five adverts proving the role was underpaid. Saying yes became cheaper than the alternative.

If the answer is still no and she decides to leave, she should check what notice pay and garden leave actually owe you before handing anything in.

How do I ask for a pay rise in writing?

Many people search for a pay rise email template. Use writing for two jobs: booking the meeting, and following it up. Do not use email instead of the conversation.

The email to book the meeting can be two lines. "I would like to book thirty minutes to talk about my role and my salary. Could we find a time before pay is set this year?"

The follow up email goes out the same day. Keep it to five short parts.

  1. Thank them for the time.
  2. Restate the number and the start date you asked for.
  3. List the three outcomes in one line each.
  4. Attach the one page and the job adverts.
  5. Confirm the date they said they would come back to you.

Written follow up protects you. If your manager changes or forgets, the request and its evidence are on record.

What if the answer is no?

A refusal is information. Collect it rather than absorb it.

Price any non cash offer honestly. Extra remote days are a lifestyle benefit, not a tax one, because employees can no longer claim homeworking tax relief from HMRC from 6 April 2026.

If what you really want is flexibility rather than money, a statutory flexible working request is a separate process with eight set grounds for refusal. It is a right to ask, not a right to receive.

  • Ask what would have to be true. "What would I need to be doing for this to be a yes in six months?" A manager who cannot answer is telling you the answer is no for good.
  • Get it written down with a date. Specific objectives and a review date. Vague encouragement without a date is a polite refusal.
  • Ask about non salary options. A four day week, extra leave, a training budget, a title change, or a bonus can be possible when base pay is not. A title change can be worth more at your next move than the money. A salary sacrifice scheme can also stretch the pay you already have.
  • Look at the sector numbers. Private sector regular pay grew 3.1% against 5.4% in the public sector. If you are already at market in a slow sector, the biggest rise on offer is often a move.
  • Do not threaten to leave unless you will. It is the last card and it can be played once. Some employers treat a resignation as the start of a search for your replacement.

The honest verdict

With regular pay up 3.5%, awards at 3.2% and CPI at 3.6%, the default outcome this year is going slightly backwards. If you accept the standard offer, that is what you have chosen.

The version that works is unglamorous. Find out when pay is decided and ask a month before. Bring one page with scope, three numbers and five current job adverts. Ask for a specific figure, then stop talking.

If you are below market, say exactly that. It is the one argument that is a checkable fact rather than an opinion.

If the answer is no and you are already at market in a slow sector, the money is probably somewhere else. That is not a failed conversation. It is the conversation telling you something useful.

Frequently asked questions

What is a reasonable pay rise to ask for in the UK?

With employers budgeting around 3% and CPI inflation at 3.6%, anything near 3% only keeps you level. A request of 6 to 12% backed by evidence is a real conversation. If you are below the market rate for your role, ask for the market rate, whatever percentage that turns out to be.

How do I ask for a pay rise by email?

Use email to book a meeting, not to make the whole case. Ask for thirty minutes to discuss your role and salary before pay is set. After the meeting, send a short follow up that restates your number, lists three results, attaches your one page and confirms when they will reply.

Can my employer stop me discussing my pay?

They can decline to raise it, but under section 77 of the Equality Act 2010 a term that stops you discussing pay to find out about discrimination cannot be enforced. If a pay dispute turns formal, Acas early conciliation has to come first, before any tribunal claim can start.

Should I mention another job offer?

Only if it is real and you would take it. Bluffing works until it is called, and it is called more often than people expect. If you do mention it, stay factual: name the salary on offer and say you would rather stay if the pay can move closer to it.

Can I ask for a pay rise after six months?

Yes, but the argument has to be scope rather than time. If the job has truly changed since you were hired, that is a fair case at any point. If it has not, wait for the review cycle. If you are still on probation, read what a probation period does and does not change first.
