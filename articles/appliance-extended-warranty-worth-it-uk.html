You Already Have Six Years Of Free Repair Rights

Ruhul Amin · Published 11 September 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked September 2026

The extended warranty offered at the till is insurance against a risk you are substantially already covered for, sold at a moment when you have just spent a lot of money and are feeling anxious about it. That is not an accident, and the law says so more clearly than you would expect.

The short version

  • GOV.UK states it plainly: "A customer has the same right to free repairs or a replacement regardless of whether they have a warranty or guarantee or not." The retailer's obligation does not end when the manufacturer's guarantee does.
  • You have up to six years to make a claim in England and Wales, five in Scotland. That is the window in which a fault that was present at purchase can be pursued.
  • The catch is the burden of proof. Within six months the retailer must prove the item was not faulty. After six months you must prove it was. That gap is what the warranty is genuinely selling.
  • The arithmetic: £60 a year on a £500 machine is £300 across five years, 60 per cent of the purchase price. A typical repair is £150, so the premium only pays if the machine breaks more than twice in five years.
  • Self-insure instead. Put the premium in a savings account, keep the receipt, and know the two sentences from GOV.UK above. You will be ahead in almost every outcome.

1. What the law actually gives you, in GOV.UK's own words

Most articles on this subject paraphrase. We are going to quote, because the wording is unusually direct and because it is the single most useful thing on this page.

GOV.UK's guidance to retailers on accepting returns and giving refunds, on a page updated on 9 September 2026, says under the heading "Warranties and guarantees":

"A customer has the same right to free repairs or a replacement regardless of whether they have a warranty or guarantee or not. So you may still have to repair or replace an item if a customer's warranty or guarantee has run out."

And under "Repairs and replacements":

"Customers have up to 6 years to make a claim for an item they've bought from you (5 years in Scotland)."

Read those together and the shape of the product becomes obvious. An extended warranty is being sold as though it creates a right to a repair in years two to five. In substantial part, that right already exists, it exists against the retailer rather than the manufacturer, and it costs nothing.

Two clarifications so this is accurate rather than merely encouraging. The six years is the time limit for bringing a claim, not a guarantee that everything is covered for six years. What you are claiming is that the goods were not of satisfactory quality when sold, which for an expensive appliance reasonably means it should last a reasonable time. A washing machine that fails at four years has a decent argument. A pair of headphones that fails at four years has a weaker one. And it is the retailer you claim against, not the manufacturer, which is why keeping the receipt matters more than registering the guarantee.

2. The six month line, which is the only real gap

Here is the part that warranty sellers are entitled to point at, and it is worth being straight about.

GOV.UK again: "You must repair or replace an item if a customer returns it within 6 months, unless you can prove it was not faulty when they bought it." And: "You can ask a customer to prove an item was faulty when they bought it if they ask for a repair or replacement after 6 months."

So the burden of proof reverses at six months. Before it, the retailer has to disprove your claim. After it, you have to prove that the fault was inherent rather than caused by wear, misuse or bad luck. In practice that can mean paying an independent engineer for a report, which on a £500 appliance is a real obstacle.

That gap is what an extended warranty genuinely sells: not a right, but the avoidance of an argument. With a warranty you ring a number and someone comes. Without one you may have to write to the retailer, cite the Consumer Rights Act 2015, and occasionally commission a report.

That is worth something. The question this article answers is whether it is worth £300.

3. The arithmetic, and how many breakdowns it takes

Take a common case: a £500 washing machine with a two year manufacturer's guarantee, offered an extended warranty at £60 a year covering years three to seven, or as a lump sum.

An out of guarantee repair on a domestic washing machine, call out plus parts, typically lands between £100 and £180. Take £150.

So the payback question in the standard form: premiums paid, divided by the cost of a typical repair, gives the number of breakdowns required to break even. £300 divided by £150 is 2.

The machine has to break twice in five years before the warranty is worth what you paid. Ask yourself honestly how many of your appliances have done that.

There is a second, sharper way to see it. £300 of premiums on a £500 machine is 60 per cent of the purchase price. You would not insure a £500 bicycle for £60 a year against mechanical failure, and nobody would offer it, because the numbers are absurd. They are equally absurd here; the difference is the moment at which they are offered.

4. Self-insuring, and how to actually do it

"Put the money aside instead" is advice everybody gives and almost nobody follows, so here is the version that works.

Open one savings account and call it the appliance fund. Not a mental note. An actual account with a name on it. Set a standing order for what the warranties would have cost across all your appliances, which for most households with a washing machine, dishwasher, fridge freezer and oven is somewhere around £15 to £20 a month.

Within two years that fund covers any single repair you are likely to face. Within four it covers a whole replacement machine. And unlike a warranty, the money is still yours if nothing breaks, and it covers the appliance that actually fails rather than only the one you insured.

That last point matters more than it sounds. Warranties are per item. A fund is pooled, and pooled risk across four appliances is far more efficient than four separate policies, which is the entire principle insurance companies run on and the reason they make money selling it to you one item at a time.

Three things to do alongside the fund, all free:

  • Keep the receipt, photographed and in an email to yourself. Your claim is against the retailer and proof of purchase is what makes it work. GOV.UK notes a bank statement or packaging can serve.
  • Register the manufacturer's guarantee, because it is free and it sometimes extends the cover from one year to two or five.
  • If you paid any part of it on a credit card and it cost over £100, you have a second route entirely under section 75, which makes the card provider jointly liable with the retailer. That is free too and it survives the retailer going bust, which a warranty from that retailer does not.

That last point is the strongest argument of all against a retailer's own extended warranty: if the retailer fails, the warranty it sold you may fail with it. Your statutory rights and your section 75 claim do not.

5. Worked example: Claire in Barry

Claire buys a £549 washer dryer. At the till she is offered a five year protection plan at £7.99 a month, which is £479.40 across the term, or 87 per cent of the machine's price.

She declines, and sets up a standing order of £8 a month into a savings account instead.

Year three: the door interlock fails. A local engineer charges £135 including the part. She pays from the fund, which by then holds £288. Balance after: £153.

Year five: nothing has gone wrong since. The fund holds £345 plus a little interest, and she has spent £135 on one repair. Her total outlay across five years is £135, against £479.40 if she had taken the plan. She is £344.40 better off, and she still has £345 in an account.

What she also did, at year three, was ask the retailer first rather than going straight to an engineer. The machine was two years and eight months old, and she wrote a short email saying the goods were not of satisfactory quality under the Consumer Rights Act 2015 and referring to the six year claim window. The retailer declined, saying the fault was wear rather than inherent, which is a defensible position at that age. So she paid the £135 and moved on.

That is the realistic outcome and it is worth stating plainly: the statutory route does not always work, particularly on older appliances. But it costs one email to try, and on a machine that fails at eighteen months it works very often indeed.

6. The honest verdict

Do not buy the extended warranty. On the arithmetic it needs the machine to break twice in five years, and most machines do not. At 60 to 87 per cent of the purchase price it is one of the worst value products sold in Britain.

Set up an appliance fund instead and pool the risk across everything you own. Fifteen to twenty pounds a month covers any repair within two years, and the money stays yours.

Learn the two GOV.UK sentences in section one. Free repair rights exist whether or not you have a warranty, and you have six years to claim in England and Wales, five in Scotland. Most people paying for warranties do not know this.

And always keep the receipt and pay at least £100 on a credit card. Those two habits are worth more than any protection plan and they cost nothing at all.

If you are buying

The useful purchase here is not a warranty, it is the ability to fix small things yourself. A great many appliance failures are a blocked pump filter, a worn door seal or a failed carbon brush, all of which are cheap parts and a twenty minute job with the right tools. Look up your model number before you call anybody out.

Frequently asked

Is there ever a case for buying one? Two, and they are narrow. If the appliance is genuinely expensive relative to your ability to absorb a sudden bill, a warranty converts an unpredictable cost into a predictable one, and there is real value in that even at a poor price. And if the item is an American style fridge freezer or a range cooker where a single repair can run to several hundred pounds, the maths moves closer. Even then, compare the premium to the actual repair cost rather than to the purchase price.

What if the retailer just refuses? Escalate in writing, cite the Consumer Rights Act 2015 and the six year window, and then use the retailer's alternative dispute resolution scheme if it has one. If you paid by credit card and the item cost over £100, section 75 gives you a claim against the card provider. Free advice is available from Citizens Advice, who will also tell you frankly when a claim is weak.

Does a longer manufacturer's guarantee change which machine I should buy? It is a reasonable signal, because a manufacturer offering ten years on a motor is making a statement about its own failure data. But read what is actually covered: a ten year guarantee on the motor with two years on everything else is common, and motors are rarely what fails. We used the same test on repairing or replacing a washing machine.
