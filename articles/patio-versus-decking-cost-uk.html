Decking Wins on the Day You Buy It and Loses by Year Six

Ruhul Amin · Published 18 September 2026 · 8 min read · Figures checked September 2026

Every comparison of patios and decking is written about the installation price, which is the one number that reverses. Timber is the cheapest to lay and the only one with an annual bill attached. Here is the ten-year arithmetic, and the planning rule that catches raised decks.

The short version

  • Timber decking is typically £70 to £120 per square metre laid, against £90 to £160 for a paved patio and £150 to £250 for composite.
  • Timber needs cleaning and treating annually, roughly £3 to £6 per square metre a year in materials plus a weekend. Paving and composite do not.
  • Over ten years on a 25 m² area, the ranking changes: paving comes out lowest per year, then composite, then timber.
  • Raised platforms including decking are permitted development only up to 300mm high, and only if all structures together cover no more than 50% of the garden.
  • Decking is the better answer on a sloping or awkward garden, where levelling for paving costs more than the deck does.

1. The upfront prices, and what sits inside them

Both jobs are mostly groundwork, which is why quotes vary so much on identical-looking gardens.

Paved patio, £90 to £160 per square metre laid. Inside that: excavation and removal of spoil, a compacted sub-base of typically 100 to 150mm, a mortar bed, the slabs themselves, cutting, jointing and a perimeter edge. The slab is often less than a third of the total. Porcelain costs more than concrete and needs a slightly different laying method, and both need falls set correctly so water runs away from the house.

Timber decking, £70 to £120 per square metre laid. Inside that: a bearer and joist frame, posts and footings, weed membrane, the boards, and fixings. It is cheaper partly because it needs far less excavation and no spoil removal, and it sits above the ground rather than being bedded into it.

Composite decking, £150 to £250 per square metre laid. The same substructure, with a board that costs several times what softwood does. Substructure requirements are usually stricter, with closer joist centres, so the frame costs more too.

Two things move all three prices hard. Access, because a garden reachable only through the house doubles the labour on materials handling. And levels, because a sloping garden means either retaining structures for paving or simply longer posts for a deck.

2. The annual bill nobody quotes

This is where the comparison is won and lost, and it is left out of nearly every quote because a quote covers installation.

Softwood decking needs cleaning and re-treating annually, or at the outside every two years, to stay sound and not become lethal in the wet. Cleaner, brush, and decking oil or stain for a 25 m² deck come to roughly £80 to £150 a year in materials, which is £3 to £6 per square metre, plus a full weekend of work you do not get paid for. Skip it for three or four years and the boards cup, split and grow algae, and the replacement cycle begins.

Hardwood decking can be left to silver naturally without structural harm, which removes the annual bill at a higher purchase price.

Composite needs washing and nothing else. It does not need sealing, and the good ones carry long warranties. It scratches, it can fade, and cheap early-generation boards absorbed water at the cut ends and swelled, which is why brand and warranty matter more here than on almost anything else in the garden.

Paving needs occasional washing and, every few years, attention to the joints. Budget under a pound per square metre a year unless you are pressure washing annually, which drives sand out of the joints and creates the work it appears to solve.

Three readings. Concrete paving is the cheapest per year over ten years and by a wider margin over twenty-five, because its life is double the deck's. If you are staying twenty-five years, extend the table yourself. Paving and composite both survive it. Softwood needs replacing twice.

4. The 300mm rule and the 50% rule

Decking is permitted development, and does not need a planning application, provided three conditions hold.

  • The decking or raised platform is no higher than 300mm above ground level.
  • Together with other extensions and outbuildings, it covers no more than 50% of the garden area.
  • None of it is forward of the principal elevation.

Within the curtilage of a listed building, no decking or raised platform is permitted development at all. In National Parks, the Broads, Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty and World Heritage Sites, additional limits apply to structures more than 20 metres from the house.

The 300mm limit is the one people trip over, because a garden that slopes even slightly means a level deck is above 300mm somewhere along its length. If your garden falls away from the house, measure before you design. Two cheaper solutions exist: step the deck down in two levels, each below the limit, or excavate the high end so the finished surface sits lower.

There is a separate consideration that is not a planning matter but decides the neighbourly outcome: a raised deck looks into next door's garden. A deck at 500mm on a boundary is the single most common reason for a retrospective planning complaint about a garden, and no amount of being right about permitted development makes that conversation pleasant.

Worked example: the Iqbal household, Solihull

A 26 m² seating area at the top of a garden that falls about 400mm from the house to the boundary.

Paving quote: £4,100. High because the fall meant either a retaining edge or importing and compacting a considerable volume of sub-base to build the level up.

Softwood deck quote: £2,290. Cheap precisely because the fall is free to a deck, which simply uses longer posts at the low end.

Composite deck quote: £5,150.

Over ten years, allowing £120 a year for treating the softwood: paving £430 a year, softwood £349 a year, composite £517 a year. The slope reversed the usual ranking, because it loaded the cost onto the paving option.

The planning check. A level deck across the whole area would have sat at 420mm at the low end, over the 300mm limit. They stepped it into two levels, each below 300mm, at an extra cost of £180 for the additional frame and step.

They took the softwood deck and set a calendar reminder each April, which is the entire difference between the £349 row and the £475 one.

5. The part of the quote worth arguing about

Both of these jobs fail in the same place, and it is not the surface you can see.

For paving, it is the sub-base. A patio wants a compacted granular sub-base of typically 100mm for foot traffic and 150mm or more on soft ground, laid in layers and compacted with a plate. A quote that gets a patio down in a day on 50mm of whatever came out of the hole will look identical for two years and then sink, rock and open its joints. Ask what depth of sub-base is allowed for, and what it is being compacted with.

For decking, it is the frame and the ground under it. Joist centres matter: softwood boards generally want support at around 400mm, and composite boards usually want closer, commonly 300mm or less, which is why a composite deck on a frame designed for timber goes wavy. Posts need proper footings rather than sitting on a slab on soil. And the whole structure needs air moving under it, because a deck built tight to the ground with no ventilation rots from beneath while looking perfect on top.

One detail that applies to both: the finished surface must sit at least 150mm below the damp proof course of the house, or two courses of brick. Bridging the damp proof course with a new patio is one of the most common causes of damp appearing in a ground floor wall the year after the garden was improved.

6. The honest verdict

On a flat garden where you intend to stay, paving is the cheapest per year and it is not close over twenty-five years. Buy concrete rather than porcelain unless you specifically want the look, because the price difference buys nothing functional.

On a sloping or awkward garden, decking wins on installation by enough to change the answer, and a softwood deck that is actually treated every spring is a perfectly sensible purchase.

Composite makes sense only if you know you will not do the maintenance and you are staying long enough to reach year twenty. If you are honest that the treating will never happen, composite is cheaper than replacing a rotten softwood deck twice.

Frequently asked

Does a patio need planning permission? Laying a patio at ground level is normally not development at all and needs no permission. A raised patio is a raised platform and falls under the same 300mm rule as decking.

Do I need to worry about drainage? Yes. A large area of hard surface in a front garden draining to the highway may need permission unless it is permeable or drains to a soakaway. In a back garden the requirement is practical rather than legal: set falls away from the house and away from the damp proof course.

How close to the boundary can I build a deck? Permitted development does not set a distance from the boundary for a platform under 300mm, but overlooking, the 50% garden rule and any restrictive covenants on your title all apply. Check the deeds as well as the planning rules.

Is composite decking slippery? Modern grooved and embossed boards perform reasonably, and better than untreated algae-covered softwood. Any smooth surface in a shaded damp corner will be slippery, whatever it is made of.
