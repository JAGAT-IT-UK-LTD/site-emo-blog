How Many Paying Subscribers You Actually Need, On British Card Fees Rather Than American Ones

Substack's 10% is the easy part and everyone gets it right. The card processing underneath it is where the published advice goes wrong, because almost all of it quotes Stripe's American pricing. Stripe publishes a UK rate, it is materially lower, and it has a fixed component that punishes cheap monthly subscriptions far harder than the percentage suggests.

The short version

  • Substack takes 10% of paid subscription revenue and charges nothing for publishing free. That part is not in dispute.
  • Stripe's published UK pricing is 1.5% plus 20p on standard UK cards, 2.8% plus 20p on premium UK cards, 2.5% plus 20p on European cards and 3.15% plus 20p on international cards, with 2% more if currency conversion is needed.
  • At £5 a month you keep about £4.22, or roughly 84%, on a standard UK card. Most guides tell UK writers to expect closer to 80% because they are using American numbers.
  • The 20p is what hurts cheap tiers. It is 6.7% of a £3 subscription and 2.5% of an £8 one. Pricing low costs you proportionally more.
  • Annual billing saves you eleven fixed fees a subscriber, because one payment a year means one 20p rather than twelve.
  • 17 subscribers at £5 a month crosses the £1,000 trading allowance. Around 1,500 would put you near the £90,000 VAT registration threshold.

1. The two fees, and the one everybody gets wrong

Two organisations take money out of a paid Substack subscription before it reaches you, and they work differently.

Substack takes 10% of what your paid subscribers pay. Publishing, growing an audience, posting Notes and being recommended by other writers all cost nothing; the 10% only applies once somebody pays. That is a genuinely clean model and it is why so many people start there.

Stripe processes the card payment, and this is where UK advice goes off the rails. Search for Substack fees and you will be told to expect 2.9% plus 30 cents. That is Stripe's American pricing. Stripe publishes separate pricing for the UK, and on its UK pricing page the rates are:

  • 1.5% plus 20p for standard UK cards
  • 2.8% plus 20p for premium UK cards
  • 2.5% plus 20p for European Economic Area cards
  • 3.15% plus 20p for international cards
  • plus 2% on top where currency conversion is required

The difference is not academic. On a £5 subscription, the American rate implies roughly 44p of processing. The standard UK rate is 27.5p. Across a year and two hundred subscribers, that is the gap between a figure you planned around and a figure you actually receive.

Two honest caveats. Premium cards cost nearly twice as much. Stripe distinguishes standard from premium UK cards, and premium ones, typically rewards and business cards, are charged at 2.8% plus 20p. You do not choose which card a reader uses, so treat 1.5% as your best case and expect a blend. And a reader abroad costs you more: an EEA card is 2.5% plus 20p and anything further afield 3.15% plus 20p, with another 2% if conversion is involved. A newsletter with a heavily international readership is meaningfully more expensive to run than the same newsletter read in Britain.

Stripe also charges separately for its Billing product, which is what handles recurring subscriptions. Check the current rate on Stripe's pricing page before you treat the numbers below as your floor, because this article does not include it.

2. What you keep at £3, £5 and £8 a month

Taking Substack's 10% and Stripe's standard UK card rate of 1.5% plus 20p, on a single monthly payment:

At £3 a month. Substack takes 30p. Stripe takes 4.5p plus 20p, so 24.5p. Total 54.5p. You keep £2.46, which is 81.8%.

At £5 a month. Substack takes 50p. Stripe takes 7.5p plus 20p, so 27.5p. Total 77.5p. You keep £4.22, which is 84.5%.

At £8 a month. Substack takes 80p. Stripe takes 12p plus 20p, so 32p. Total £1.12. You keep £6.88, which is 86%.

Look at what moves across those three. The 10% is constant by definition. The Stripe percentage is constant too. The only thing changing is the 20p, and it is doing all the work: it is 6.7% of a £3 subscription and 2.5% of an £8 one. Between £3 and £8 you gain more than four percentage points of margin purely from spreading one fixed fee over a larger payment.

The practical reading: £3 a month is close to the cheapest price that makes sense on a monthly plan. Below that the fixed fee starts taking a genuinely unpleasant share. If you want a cheap entry point, do it annually rather than monthly, for the reason in the next section.

Run the same three on a premium UK card at 2.8% plus 20p and you keep £2.42 at £3, £4.16 at £5 and £6.78 at £8. The shape does not change, the level drops by a few pence, and it is worth knowing your real figure will sit between the two.

3. Why annual billing is worth more than the discount you give

A monthly subscriber generates twelve card transactions a year. An annual subscriber generates one. Substack's 10% does not care, because it is purely a percentage. Stripe's 20p cares enormously.

Monthly at £5: twelve payments, twelve lots of 20p, so £2.40 a year in fixed fees alone. Total annual fees are £6 of Substack commission plus £0.90 of Stripe percentage plus £2.40 of fixed fees, so £9.30. You keep £50.70 of £60.

Annual at £50, a common ten months for twelve offer: one payment, one 20p. Substack takes £5, Stripe takes 75p plus 20p. Total fees £5.95. You keep £44.05.

So the annual plan at £50 nets you £6.65 less than a monthly subscriber who stays all twelve months. That looks like an argument against annual billing until you add the two things that actually happen.

Monthly subscribers do not all stay twelve months. If your average monthly subscriber lasts seven months, they are worth about £29.60 to you, against £44.05 for the annual one paid upfront. Annual billing is not really a pricing decision, it is a churn decision.

And you get the money now. With Bank Rate at 3.75%, held on 16 September 2026, cash a year early is worth something real rather than nothing.

The honest version: if your retention is strong, monthly is worth more per subscriber. If you do not know your retention, it is probably not strong, and annual is the safer instrument. Either way, price the annual plan knowing you are buying certainty, not saving fees.

4. The subscriber counts for real targets

Using the standard UK card figures above, monthly billing, and rounding up to whole people:

To clear £100 a month: 41 subscribers at £3, 24 at £5, 15 at £8.

To clear £500 a month: 204 at £3, 119 at £5, 73 at £8.

To clear £1,000 a month: 407 at £3, 237 at £5, 146 at £8.

To clear £2,000 a month, which is roughly a modest full time income before tax: 813 at £3, 474 at £5, 291 at £8.

The £3 column is the one worth staring at. Getting 813 people to pay you anything at all is dramatically harder than getting 291 people to pay a sensible amount, and the low price does not make the first job three times easier. It rarely makes it easier at all.

Two tax thresholds sit across these numbers. The £1,000 trading allowance is on gross income, so at £5 a month, £60 a year each, you cross it at 17 subscribers. Above that you have to tell HMRC. And VAT registration becomes compulsory at £90,000 of taxable turnover on a rolling twelve months, which at £5 a month is around 1,500 subscribers. That is a long way off for almost everyone, but it is the number, and a paid newsletter is a digital service.

Between those two, the useful mental model is that a paid newsletter is a hundreds business, not a thousands business, and the price you set decides which.

5. Worked example: Rhian in Wrexham, 210 subscribers

Rhian writes a weekly newsletter from Wrexham about the north Wales food scene. After two years she has 210 paying subscribers: 150 monthly at £5, and 60 annual at £50.

Gross. 150 at £60 a year is £9,000. 60 at £50 is £3,000. Total £12,000 a year.

Substack's 10%: £1,200.

Stripe. The monthly group generates 1,800 transactions a year. At 1.5% that is £135, and 1,800 lots of 20p is £360. The annual group generates 60 transactions: 1.5% of £3,000 is £45, and 60 lots of 20p is £12. Total Stripe, before any premium or overseas cards, is £552.

She keeps £10,248, or 85.4% of gross.

Now the line worth pulling out. Of her £552 of card fees, £372 is fixed 20p charges and only £180 is percentage. The fixed fees are more than twice the percentage fees. Her 150 monthly subscribers generate 1,800 transactions between them; her 60 annual subscribers generate 60.

What if she moved a third of the monthly group to annual? Fifty subscribers moving from twelve payments to one removes 550 transactions, saving £110 a year in fixed fees. If she gives them two months free to do it, she gives up £500 of revenue to save £110, which is a bad trade on fees alone and possibly a good one on churn. That is the honest framing, and it is not the framing you will get from anyone selling you newsletter growth advice.

Tax. £12,000 gross is well over the £1,000 trading allowance, so this goes on a Self Assessment return, with the £1,752 of platform and card fees deductible as expenses. She is a long way below the £90,000 VAT threshold. If this is alongside employment, the £10,248 profit stacks on top of her salary for rate purposes, which is the thing most newsletter writers forget until January.

The honest verdict

For a UK writer, Substack's economics are better than the internet says, because the internet is quoting American card fees. On a standard UK card at £5 a month you keep about £4.22, not the £4 or less most guides imply.

The number that should change your behaviour is not the 10%, which you cannot do anything about. It is the 20p. It is 6.7% of a £3 subscription and 2.5% of an £8 one, and it is charged per transaction rather than per subscriber, which is why a monthly reader costs you twelve of them a year and an annual reader costs you one.

So: do not price below about £3 a month, take annual plans seriously as a churn instrument rather than a fee saving, and if you are choosing between chasing 800 people at £3 and 300 people at £8, the second is not only more profitable, it is the easier job.

And know your two tax marks. Seventeen subscribers at £5 puts you over the trading allowance and into telling HMRC. That arrives much sooner than anyone expects, and it is on gross.

Frequently asked

Does Substack really take nothing until I charge? Publishing, growing an audience and using the app are free; the 10% applies to paid subscription revenue. You will still pay card processing through Stripe on anything you charge, and that is separate from Substack's cut.

Why do other guides say I keep about 80%? Because they use Stripe's US pricing of 2.9% plus 30 cents. Stripe publishes a UK rate of 1.5% plus 20p for standard UK cards. If your readers are mostly British, your real figure is higher than the American guides suggest. If they are mostly overseas, it is closer to their number.

Should I price in pounds or dollars? Pounds, if your readers and your bank are British. Stripe adds 2% where currency conversion is required, so pricing in a currency your account does not hold costs you on every transaction rather than once.

At what point do I need to tell HMRC? When your gross trading income across all side activities passes £1,000 in a tax year, which at £5 a month is 17 subscribers. Gross means before Substack and Stripe take their cut, not after. Registration for VAT is a separate and much higher bar at £90,000 of taxable turnover on a rolling twelve months.
