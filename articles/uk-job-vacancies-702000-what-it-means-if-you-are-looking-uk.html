1. The numbers published this morning

The ONS Labour market overview for September 2026 was released on 15 September 2026. The headline figures:

  • Vacancies: 702,000 for June to August 2026. That is a fall of 8,000, or 1.1%, on the previous quarter, and a fall of 16,000 since January to March 2026.
  • Claimant Count: 1.692 million for August 2026, up on the month and up on the year. The July figure was 1.665 million. The latest month is provisional and gets revised.
  • Payrolled employees: 30.2 million on the August flash estimate, down 26,000 on the month and down 145,000, or 0.5%, on the year.
  • Unemployment rate: 4.9% for May to July 2026, up 0.2 percentage points on the year but broadly unchanged on the quarter.
  • Employment rate: 75.1%, down 0.1 percentage points on the year.
  • Regular pay growth: 3.5%. Total pay growth 3.9%, down from 4.2%, and the lowest since September to November 2020 when it was 3.7%.

The single most quotable line in the bulletin is the historical comparison. Outside the coronavirus pandemic period, the last time there were 702,000 or fewer vacancies was August to October 2014, when there were 701,000. So the advertised job market is back at a level last seen more than a decade ago.

2. What 702,000 actually feels like from the inside

Aggregate figures are cold comfort when you are the one applying. What they tell you, practically, is about competition per opening rather than about there being no openings.

The ONS adds a detail worth repeating because it explains where the missing vacancies went. Feedback from its Vacancy Survey continues to suggest that smaller firms may not be recruiting because of increases in labour costs. That is a specific claim about a specific part of the market. Small employers are the ones who have quietly stopped posting.

The practical read: the roles that vanish first in this kind of market are the discretionary ones. The extra pair of hands, the junior hire a small firm was thinking about, the role a business creates because it can rather than because it must. Roles filling a genuine gap are still being advertised. That changes what a good application looks like, which we come back to below.

3. The ratio nobody puts in the headline

Take the Claimant Count of 1.692 million and the vacancy count of 702,000 and divide. That gives roughly 2.4 claimants for every advertised vacancy. We have done that arithmetic ourselves from the two ONS figures, and it deserves two honest caveats.

First, the two measures are not counting matched things. The Claimant Count includes people receiving Jobseeker's Allowance and Universal Credit claimants in the searching for work conditionality group. It is not the same population as the unemployment measure from the Labour Force Survey, and the ONS describes it as an official statistic in development. Second, vacancies are a stock at a point in time, not a flow, and jobs are also filled through routes that never become a counted vacancy.

So do not read 2.4 as "2.4 people apply for each job". Read it as a direction. Both numbers moved the wrong way for a job hunter in the same month. That is the signal.

4. Worked example: Declan in Sunderland

Declan was made redundant from a warehouse coordinator role in July. He has been applying at a rate of about twelve applications a week, mostly through job boards, mostly the same CV.

In a market with 702,000 vacancies and a rising Claimant Count, volume is the strategy with the worst return. Twelve near identical applications into a pool where employers are receiving far more responses per advert than they were a year ago produces, predictably, silence.

The arithmetic that matters to him is not applications per week. It is this: if he spends the same six hours a week on four applications instead of twelve, and each of those four is tailored to the actual advert and sent alongside a direct approach to a named person, his response rate per application does not need to triple for him to be better off. It needs to rise from roughly one in twelve to roughly one in four. In a market where small firms have stopped advertising, the direct approach is also the only way to reach the roles that were never posted.

There is a second thing Declan should do that has nothing to do with applications, and it is the one people skip. Check what he is entitled to while he looks. Being one of the 1.692 million on the Claimant Count is not a badge of failure, it is the system working as designed, and the money buys him the time to make four good applications instead of twelve rushed ones.

5. What to change about how you are looking

  1. Cut your application volume and raise the quality. In a tighter market, a generic application is not a low value application, it is a zero value one.
  2. Go where the vacancies are not advertised. The ONS is explicitly telling you small firms have stopped posting. Direct approaches to small employers in your sector reach a market the job boards cannot show you.
  3. Watch the pay figure, not just the vacancy figure. Regular pay growth of 3.5% with real terms growth of 0.6% on CPIH means there is very little room in employers' budgets for a large jump on your last salary. Pitch accordingly.
  4. Do not over read one month. The ONS warns repeatedly that short term movements in these series are volatile and that users should focus on long term movements. The trend here is a slow grind downwards, not a cliff edge.
  5. If you are self employed or contracting, price off the same data. Workforce jobs fell by 48,000 between March and June 2026, with a 43,000 drop in the self employment jobs component. Our guide on setting freelance rates in the UK covers how to hold a rate when demand softens.

The next labour market release is 20 October 2026, which is when you will find out whether August was a wobble or a trend.

The honest verdict

702,000 vacancies is genuinely the lowest outside the pandemic since 2014, and a Claimant Count rising to 1.692 million in the same month is not a coincidence. This is a harder market than it was a year ago and there is no point pretending otherwise.

What it is not is a market with no jobs in it. 702,000 advertised roles is still 702,000 advertised roles, plus everything small employers are filling without advertising. The honest advice is unglamorous: fewer applications, much better ones, and more direct approaches to the small firms the ONS says have stopped posting. If you are sending twelve identical applications a week and hearing nothing, the market is not the only thing that needs to change.

Frequently asked

When is the next set of ONS labour market figures? 20 October 2026. The bulletin is published at 7am on the ONS website and covers vacancies, employment, unemployment, the Claimant Count and average weekly earnings.

Why do vacancies and unemployment sometimes move in the same direction? They measure different things and come from different sources. Vacancies come from a survey of businesses, unemployment from the Labour Force Survey of households, and the Claimant Count from benefits administration. The ONS advises using them together as a suite rather than relying on any one.

Are 702,000 vacancies low by historical standards? Outside the coronavirus pandemic period, the ONS says the last time there were 702,000 or fewer vacancies was August to October 2014, when there were 701,000. So yes, by the standards of the last decade it is low.

Does falling pay growth mean I should not negotiate? No, but it tells you what room employers have. Total pay growth of 3.9% is the lowest since late 2020, and real regular pay growth is 0.6% on CPIH. Asking for a large uplift on your last salary is harder to land in that environment than it was two years ago.
