The Brown Envelope That Is Not a Tax Return, and the Deadline Inside It That Expires First

A Simple Assessment tells you what HMRC thinks you owe without you filing anything. It carries two separate clocks: one for paying and one for disagreeing. The one for disagreeing runs out a long time before the one everybody writes in the diary.

The short version

  • A Simple Assessment (letter PA302) replaces a tax return. If you get one, you do not register for Self Assessment and you do not file a return.
  • Letter sent before 31 October 2026, for the 2025 to 2026 tax year: pay by 31 January 2027.
  • Letter sent on or after 31 October 2026, for 2025 to 2026 or earlier: pay within 3 months of the date on the letter.
  • If you think anything is wrong you must contact HMRC within 60 days. That clock starts at the date of the letter, not at the payment deadline.

  • You can pay in instalments as long as the full amount clears by the deadline on the letter.
  • You need the 14 character payment reference beginning with X. A wrong reference delays the payment rather than losing it.
  • There is a free HMRC service to check the calculation and see exactly which income, savings interest or benefits were included.

1. What a Simple Assessment is, and who gets one

Simple Assessment is HMRC working out your tax for you and sending you the answer. The letter is a PA302. It matters because a valid one replaces the tax return entirely: you do not register for Self Assessment, you do not file anything, you just check the figure and pay it.

It usually turns up where HMRC already knows about all your income but cannot collect the tax through a tax code. The common cases are pensioners whose State Pension is paid without tax taken off, people with savings interest above their allowances, and people who owe an amount that does not fit into PAYE.

That third category is worth pausing on. Because the personal allowance has been frozen while pensions and interest rates have risen, a slowly growing number of people who have never filed anything in their lives now receive one of these. Getting a brown envelope about tax for the first time at 74 is unnerving, and the instinct is to put it somewhere safe and deal with it later. That instinct is the problem this article is about.

2. The two clocks, and which expires first

The letter contains two deadlines. Only one of them reads like a deadline.

The payment clock. If HMRC sent the letter before 31 October 2026, and it covers the 2025 to 2026 tax year, you must pay by 31 January 2027. If it was sent on or after 31 October 2026, for 2025 to 2026 or any earlier year, you must pay within three months of the date of the letter.

A small oddity falls out of that: a letter arriving in November gives you slightly longer than one arriving in October, because three months from November lands past 31 January. Nothing to plan around, but worth not being confused by.

The dispute clock. If you think any of the information is wrong, you must contact HMRC within 60 days. That runs from the letter, and it is nowhere near the payment date.

So on a letter dated 20 September 2026 you have 133 days to pay and 60 days to argue. For 73 of those days the bill is still not due and your right to challenge it has already gone.

Everybody diaries the date the money is due. Almost nobody diaries the date the objection is due, and that is the one that closes first.

3. What to actually check on the calculation

HMRC runs a free service to check your Simple Assessment, which shows why it was issued and exactly what income, savings interest and benefits went into it. Use it inside the 60 days rather than after.

Three things are worth looking at properly.

The savings interest figure. HMRC gets this from banks and building societies, and it can be out of date or can include an account you closed. Interest is also reported for the year it was credited, which is not always the year you think of it as belonging to.

Pension income you no longer receive. If a pension stopped, was transferred, or changed part way through the year, an estimate can be carried forward that no longer reflects reality.

Allowances that should be there and are not. Marriage Allowance and Blind Person Allowance are the two that get missed, because they depend on a claim rather than on data HMRC already holds.

If the figure is right, that is genuinely the end of it. Pay it and there is nothing else to do, which is the actual benefit of this system over a tax return.

4. Paying it, and what to do if you cannot

You need the 14 character payment reference beginning with X from the letter. If you use the wrong reference the payment is delayed rather than lost, but chasing it is tedious, so copy it carefully.

You can pay online, by bank transfer or by cheque. You can also pay in instalments, provided the whole amount has cleared by the deadline on your letter. You do not need permission to split it up that way.

If you cannot pay by the deadline at all, that is a different conversation and HMRC has a formal route for it. Doing that before the deadline is much better than after, and it is free to ask. Ignoring it is the only approach with no upside.

Worked example: two letters, two sets of dates

Letter one, dated 20 September 2026. Sent before 31 October, covering 2025 to 2026.

Payment deadline: 31 January 2027. From the date of the letter that is 133 days.

Dispute deadline: 60 days from 20 September, which is 19 November 2026.

The gap between the two is 73 days. Someone who files the letter away in September meaning to sort it out over Christmas will sit down in January with a bill they can still pay on time and an objection they can no longer make.

Letter two, dated 5 November 2026. Sent on or after 31 October, so the three month rule applies instead.

Payment deadline: three months from the letter, so 5 February 2027, which is actually later than the January date on the first letter.

Dispute deadline: 60 days from 5 November, which is 4 January 2027.

What both have in common: roughly two months to check the figure, and roughly four to find the money. The checking window is always the tighter one, and it is the one with no reminder attached.

The five minute version: when the letter arrives, write two dates on the envelope. The payment date from the letter, and the date 60 days after the letter date. Then open the free HMRC checker before the second one.

5. The honest verdict

Simple Assessment is genuinely simpler than a tax return and for most people who get one the right answer is: check it once, confirm it looks right, pay it, forget it. No registration, no filing, no accountant.

The design flaw is the mismatch between the two deadlines. The payment date is prominent and months away. The 60 day window to say the figure is wrong is short, starts immediately, and is exactly the sort of thing a person who has never dealt with HMRC before will not notice. The people most likely to receive a first Simple Assessment, older people with modest pension and savings income, are also the people least likely to know that the objection clock is separate.

So the whole of the useful advice is one sentence: check the figure in the first two months even though you do not have to pay for another four. If it is right you have lost ten minutes. If it is wrong you have kept a right that was about to expire quietly.

Where this does not help you: if the figure is correct, none of this gets you out of the tax. It is a calculation, not a penalty, and disputing a correct assessment simply wastes the time of somebody who could be helping you arrange to pay it instead.

Frequently asked

Do I need to file a tax return as well? No. A Simple Assessment replaces the return. If you have been sent one, do not also register for Self Assessment.

What if I miss the 60 days? Contact HMRC anyway and explain. The 60 days is the stated window on GOV.UK, and being outside it makes things harder rather than automatically hopeless. TaxAid and the Low Incomes Tax Reform Group both give free help in exactly this situation.

Can I pay it in bits? Yes, as long as the full amount is paid by the deadline on your letter. If it cannot be, use the official route for difficulties paying before the deadline passes.

How do I know the letter is genuine? A real Simple Assessment is a PA302 and carries a 14 character reference starting with X, and you can check the calculation through your own account on GOV.UK rather than through any link or phone number in a message. If anything asks you to pay by unusual means, treat it as a scam.
