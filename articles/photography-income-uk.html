Making Money From Photography in the UK: The Six Real Routes

21 July 2026 · 6 min read · Updated 18 November 2026 · Figures checked November 2026

Making money from photography in the UK is realistic, but the money is rarely where people expect it. Local commercial work, such as property, headshots and product shots, pays fastest. Weddings pay most per day but carry the most risk, and stock libraries pay a small share of each sale.

If you want a part time income inside a year, this guide shows which route to start with, what the official tax rules mean for you, and where the numbers come from.

The short version
  • Bridebook's 2026 data puts the UK average wedding photographer cost at £1,484, with London and the South East usually £200 to £500 higher.
  • Stock pays a share of each sale, not a fee. Shutterstock pays contributors 15% to 40% of the sale price across six levels, and every level resets to level 1 on 1 January.
  • Weddings are where the money is and where the risk is. One Saturday can match months of stock income, and one botched Saturday can become a legal claim.
  • Side income above the £1,000 trading allowance means registering with HMRC. The allowance rises to £3,000 from 6 April 2027.
  • If you want money this quarter, do local commercial work, not weddings and not stock.

1. Which photography routes pay fastest in the UK?

Here are the six main ways of making money from photography in the UK, ranked by how quickly the first payment tends to arrive.

Notice the shape. The routes that pay quickly are the ones nobody dreams about.

The routes people dream about, weddings and stock, are the slowest to start. They are also the most crowded.

2. How much do wedding photographers really make?

The headline is good. Bridebook's 2026 wedding report puts the UK average wedding photographer cost at £1,484. London and the South East usually run £200 to £500 higher.

Bridebook's own packages table lists £1,500 to £3,000 for a standard full day. So a full day booking sits above the average, because the average includes shorter and cheaper bookings.

Now the part the courses skip.

A wedding is not a day of work. There is the enquiry and consultation, the contract and admin, a venue visit, the day itself, then culling and editing.

In our estimate that adds up to roughly 35 to 45 hours per wedding. At £2,000 that is £44 to £57 an hour before costs. Good, not extraordinary.

It is seasonal. Most UK weddings fall in late spring and summer. You will have a few very busy months and a January with little or no income. Price for twelve months, earn in five.

You cannot reshoot it. A corrupted card, a failed camera or an illness on the morning can destroy something irreplaceable.

That is why professionals shoot to dual card slots, carry two bodies and back up to two places before sleeping. They also carry professional indemnity and public liability cover.

All of that is cost. All of it is the difference between a cheap photographer and an expensive one.

Start by second shooting. Assist an established photographer for a day rate. You get real conditions, real timelines and portfolio images.

You also find out whether you actually like it before anyone is depending on you.

3. Can you make money from stock photography?

Making money from stock photography is possible, but it is the most often recommended and most often misrepresented route.

The key fact: you earn a percentage of each sale, and the sale price on a subscription download can be very small.

Shutterstock15% to 40% of the sale priceSix levels based on downloads. All contributors reset to level 1 on 1 January each year.
Adobe StockA percentage royalty, with a small guaranteed minimum on some large subscription plansAdobe's royalty page blocked our checker in November 2026, so we have not confirmed the current percentage.

Shutterstock figures from Shutterstock's contributor help page, checked 18 November 2026. Both platforms have changed their rates before.

The yearly reset matters. Your best selling year still starts at the bottom rate on 1 January.

Building a portfolio of a thousand accepted images takes hundreds of hours of shooting, editing, keywording and uploading. It is a long stretch of unpaid work for a return that is usually modest.

It works as a by product, not a plan. If you are already shooting, uploading the outtakes costs you only keywording time.

Deliberately shooting for stock, while competing with an endless supply of generated images, is a poor use of a photographer's time. Our guide to selling photos to stock sites goes through each library in more detail.

4. Where does local commercial work come from?

This is where most working photographers actually earn. Almost nobody writes about it because it is unglamorous.

The prices below are rough asking prices you will see from UK freelancers. They are not official figures. Check what photographers near you charge before you set yours.

  • Estate agents. £60 to £150 per property, about an hour on site. Agents shoot constantly and value reliability over artistry.
  • Restaurants and cafes. £200 to £500 for a menu shoot. Every independent needs images for delivery apps and social media.
  • Small business product photography. £15 to £40 per product on a white background, or £300 to £800 for a half day. Anyone selling on Etsy or Amazon needs this.
  • Headshots. £80 to £200 per person, or £600 to £1,500 for a team day. Solicitors, estate agents, recruiters and accountants all refresh these.
  • Local sport and school events. Reliable, repeatable, and nobody is competing hard for it.

The route in is simple and a little uncomfortable. Walk into twenty independent businesses with a printed sample and a price.

Why 30%? Her profit is £1,363 a month, about £16,356 a year. Her salary already uses her £12,570 personal allowance, so the photography profit is taxed at 20% if she stays a basic rate taxpayer.

She also pays Class 4 National Insurance at 6% on self employed profits over £12,570. On these sums that comes to roughly 21% of her profit, so 30% is a cautious buffer. It also leaves some room if part of her profit falls into the higher rate band above £50,270.

The interesting lines are the first and the fourth. Estate agent work, the least romantic item, produces 70% of the gross. Stock produces under 3%.

That ratio is typical. It is worth sitting with before you spend a year building a stock portfolio.

6. What kit do you actually need?

Less than you think for everything except weddings. For weddings the requirement is not quality but redundancy.

  • Portraits, product, estate agents: any modern body, one sharp prime, one wide zoom, a tripod and a couple of speedlights. A used full frame body from five years ago is entirely sufficient.
  • Weddings: two bodies, both with dual card slots, two lenses that cover the range, two flashes, and enough batteries and cards that a failure is an inconvenience rather than a catastrophe. This is not preference, it is the professional minimum.
  • Everything: a colour calibrated screen, and a backup routine you follow the same night, every time. Two copies in two places before you sleep.

You can browse used bodies and lenses on Amazon UK, though for used gear the specialist dealers usually offer better condition grading and a warranty. As an Amazon Associate we earn from qualifying purchases.

7. Do you pay tax on photography income?

Yes, once it passes the trading allowance. GOV.UK sets the trading allowance at £1,000 a year.

If you earn more than £1,000 in a tax year, you must register as a sole trader. The deadline is 5 October after the end of that tax year, and registering late can bring a penalty.

The rule is changing. The government is raising the trading allowance to £3,000 from 6 April 2027. Our guide to the £3,000 trading allowance explains who gains.

One catch: you can claim the allowance or your real expenses, not both. A photographer with a new camera, insurance and mileage will often be better off claiming expenses.

On profits, income tax for 2026 to 2027 is 20% between £12,571 and £50,270 outside Scotland. Class 4 National Insurance is 6% on profits from £12,570 to £50,270 and 2% above that.

8. Insurance, contracts and copyright

  • Public liability insurance is asked for by many venues before they will let you work. Get quotes, because prices vary with the work you do. Our guide to public liability insurance for side hustles explains what it covers.
  • Professional indemnity covers you when the work itself goes wrong. Essential for weddings. See our guide to professional indemnity insurance for freelancers.
  • Equipment cover is separate. Your home contents policy almost certainly excludes commercial use.
  • You own the copyright in the UK as the photographer, unless you sign it away or you are an employee. Clients get a licence, not ownership, and that should be written into every contract. Owning it is not the same as being free to use it, and this is the part that catches wedding photographers out: the Intellectual Property Office is explicit that you should not put a couple's wedding images on your website or into public display without their permission, even though the copyright is yours.
  • Model releases are needed for stock and for most commercial use of recognisable people. Property releases for private buildings.
  • Photographing children at schools and clubs needs written parental consent and often a DBS check. The check is not instant and it is not free: what a DBS check costs and who actually needs one is worth reading before you take a school booking, and GOV.UK sets out which level applies.
  • Data protection. You are holding personal data. Have a retention policy and a way to delete images on request. The ICO guidance for small organisations is the plain English version, and a one page retention policy is enough for a solo photographer.

Myths that cost people money

"I need better gear before I can charge." Clients cannot tell what body shot the image. They can tell whether you turned up on time and whether the files arrived when promised.

Buy gear when a specific job requires it.

"Do a few free shoots to build a portfolio." Two, maybe. Beyond that you are training a local market to expect free photography.

Free clients are often the most demanding. Charge a small fee instead of nothing, and the relationship changes completely.

"Stock photography is passive income." At a 15% to 40% share of often small subscription prices, a month's income needs a lot of downloads. That needs a portfolio that took you a year to build.

It is passive in the same sense that a pension is passive.

The honest verdict

This helps you if you will do unglamorous commercial work and can sell to local businesses face to face. Treat weddings as something to grow into through second shooting, not something to start with.

On that path, a steady part time income inside a year is realistic, as Aisha's example shows.

This does not help you if your plan is stock libraries, or shooting only what you find beautiful. The market pays for solving a business problem.

A landscape at golden hour is not a business problem.

There is genuinely nothing to buy to get started here. If you own a camera and can get to clients, you have what you need for your first paid jobs.

Frequently asked questions about making money from photography in the UK

Do I need to register as self employed to sell photos? Yes, once your trading income passes £1,000 in a tax year. Register with HMRC by 5 October after that tax year ends, or you may get a penalty. From 6 April 2027 the trading allowance rises to £3,000, so smaller sellers will have more room before they need to report.

How can I make money from photography online? The main online routes are stock libraries, selling prints through your own site or a marketplace, and finding local clients through a simple portfolio site. Stock pays a share of each sale, from 15% to 40% at Shutterstock. Online is best used to win local paid work, not as the income itself.

Should I do a photography course? A short course on the business of photography is often worth more than a technical one. The technical side is widely taught for free online. Pricing, contracts and client management are what new photographers usually get wrong, and they are what decides whether clients come back and recommend you.

What should I charge for my first paid job? Work out your minimum acceptable hourly figure. Multiply it by the realistic total hours, including travel and editing, and do not go below the result. Add a share of your insurance, software and kit costs, and set aside money for tax. Then compare with what photographers near you actually charge.

Is AI killing photography work? It has hollowed out generic stock badly, because generated images compete directly with it. It has barely touched work that needs a real person, a real place or a real event, which is where the paying jobs are. Weddings, headshots, property and food photography all still need someone with a camera on site.
