Crypto for Beginners in the UK: The Rules, the Tax, the Risks

22 August 2026 · 9 min read · Rules checked August 2026
The short version
  • Crypto is not covered by the FSCS. If a platform fails or you are defrauded, there is no compensation scheme behind you.
  • HMRC treats most crypto as a chargeable asset. Gains above the £3,000 annual exempt amount are taxed at 18% or 24%.
  • Swapping one coin for another is a disposal and can create a tax bill even though you never saw a pound.
  • The FCA banned the sale of crypto derivatives to UK retail consumers and requires a risk warning and a cooling-off period before you can buy.
  • Nobody can tell you where the price goes. Anyone who says otherwise is selling something.

What protection you have, which is very little

This is the part that gets skipped, so it goes first.

  • No FSCS protection. The Financial Services Compensation Scheme covers up to £85,000 per person per institution for deposits at authorised banks. It does not cover crypto holdings. If the platform collapses, that money is simply gone in most scenarios.
  • No Financial Ombudsman route for the crypto asset itself in most cases. If you are unhappy with an outcome, there is no free adjudicator to appeal to.
  • Registration is not endorsement. UK crypto firms must register with the FCA for anti-money-laundering supervision. That is a check on financial crime controls. It is not a judgement that the firm is safe, well run, or that the product is suitable.
  • Cross-border risk. Many platforms UK residents use are based overseas. If something goes wrong, you are pursuing a company in another legal system.

None of this makes crypto a scam. It makes it an asset class where the safety net most British consumers assume exists does not.

The UK rules as they stand

  • Crypto derivatives are banned for retail consumers. The FCA prohibited the sale of derivatives and exchange-traded notes referencing crypto assets to UK retail consumers. Anyone offering you leveraged crypto trading as a UK retail customer is operating outside that.
  • Financial promotions are regulated. Crypto adverts must carry a prominent risk warning, cannot offer refer-a-friend bonuses, and first-time investors face a 24-hour cooling-off period plus an appropriateness assessment before they can invest.
  • Anti-money-laundering registration is mandatory for UK crypto asset firms. You can check the FCA register yourself. If a firm is not on it and is marketing to UK consumers, that is a serious flag.
  • Reporting is tightening. International frameworks now require crypto platforms to report user information to tax authorities, and HMRC receives that data. The assumption that crypto gains are invisible to HMRC is out of date.

Rules checked August 2026. Crypto regulation in the UK is moving quickly, so check the FCA's own pages for the current position before acting on anything here.

Tax, including the trap nobody expects

HMRC treats most crypto held by individuals as a chargeable asset for Capital Gains Tax, not as currency and not as gambling.

Item2026/27 position
Annual exempt amount£3,000, shared across all your chargeable assets, not per asset
CGT rate, basic rate band18%
CGT rate, higher or additional rate band24%
Crypto received as incomeMining, staking rewards, airdrops for a service and payment for work are generally Income Tax, not CGT

The trap: a disposal is not just selling for pounds. It includes swapping one crypto asset for another, spending crypto on goods or services, and gifting it to anyone other than your spouse or civil partner. Every one of those is a disposal at market value on the day, and can create a taxable gain even though no money reached your bank account.

People discover this after a year of active trading, when they have a tax bill and no cash, because the gains are still sitting in a coin that has since fallen. That is the single most common crypto tax problem in the UK.

Two more things worth knowing. HMRC applies share pooling rules, so you cannot simply pick which coins you sold; there is a specific ordering including same-day and 30-day rules. And losses can be claimed to offset gains, but you have to report them, and they are worth having on record.

Keep every transaction record from the start. Exporting two years of trades retrospectively from three platforms is miserable, and it is the reason crypto tax software exists.

Who is holding your coins

There is a phrase in this world: not your keys, not your coins. It is glib and it is also accurate.

  • On an exchange: the platform holds the asset and you hold a claim against the platform. Convenient, easy to recover if you lose your password, and completely dependent on that company remaining solvent and honest. Several large ones have not.
  • In your own wallet: you hold the private key. Nobody can freeze it or lose it for you. Equally, if you lose the recovery phrase, it is gone permanently. There is no support line, no reset, no ombudsman.

Both options have a failure mode that loses you everything. Pick the one whose failure mode you can actually manage. For most people holding a small amount, a reputable registered exchange with two-factor authentication is the realistic answer, and a hardware wallet only becomes worth the complexity at larger sums.

If you do self-custody: write the recovery phrase on paper, never photograph it, never type it into anything that asks, and store a second copy somewhere else. Every "wallet support agent" who asks for your seed phrase is a thief, without exception.

The scams that actually work

UK losses to crypto fraud run into the hundreds of millions a year. The patterns are consistent:

  • Fake celebrity endorsements. An advert or article claiming a well-known British figure made money on a platform. They did not, and several have taken legal action over it.
  • Romance and long-con investment fraud. Weeks of genuine-feeling conversation, then an introduction to a trading platform that shows fake profits. Withdrawals work at first for small amounts, which is what builds the confidence to send more.
  • Recovery scams. After you lose money, someone contacts you offering to recover it for a fee. This is a second theft aimed at people already down, and it is common.
  • Fake apps and cloned sites. Always reach a platform through a bookmark you saved yourself, never through a link in a message or an advert.
  • Guaranteed returns. There is no such thing. Any fixed percentage promised on a volatile asset is either a lie or a Ponzi structure.

The one reliable test: if someone contacted you first, it is a scam. Legitimate investments do not need to find you.

If you are going to do it anyway

A short list, and none of it is exciting, which is the point.

  1. Only money you can lose entirely without it changing your life. Not the deposit, not the emergency fund.
  2. Clear the expensive debt first. Paying 24% APR on a credit card while hoping for a 20% gain is a losing trade with extra steps.
  3. Use the boring wrappers first. A stocks and shares ISA gives you tax-free growth up to the annual allowance. Crypto gets you neither the wrapper nor the protection.
  4. Check the FCA register before you send money anywhere, and check the FCA warning list too.
  5. Two-factor authentication on the exchange and on the email address it is registered to. SIM-swap fraud is a real route into crypto accounts.
  6. Keep records from day one. Date, asset, amount, value in pounds, fees. Every disposal.
  7. Decide in advance what would make you sell. Writing it down before you have a position is the only time you will think about it clearly.

Questions people ask

Do I have to tell HMRC if I made a small gain? If your total gains across all chargeable assets are within the £3,000 annual exempt amount and your disposal proceeds are below the reporting threshold, generally no. Above that, you report through Self Assessment. If you are unsure, check your own position or ask an accountant, because the thresholds interact.

Is crypto gambling for tax purposes? No. HMRC has been explicit that it does not regard buying and selling crypto assets as gambling, which means gains are taxable.

What about crypto ETFs or funds? The FCA has moved on retail access to some crypto-backed exchange-traded products, and the position has been changing. Check the current rules directly rather than relying on any article, including this one.

Is Bitcoin safer than the small coins? It is larger, older and more liquid, which reduces some specific risks. It is still highly volatile and still outside the compensation schemes. Safer than a coin invented last month is a low bar.

The verdict

Crypto is legal in the UK, taxed as a chargeable asset, and protected by essentially nothing. If you take part, do it with money you can afford to lose entirely, on a registered platform, with records kept from the first transaction, and after the ISA and the credit card have been dealt with. Treat anyone promising certainty as the risk.
