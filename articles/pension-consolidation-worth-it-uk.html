Merging Your Old Pensions Can Quietly Cost You Two Years of Access

Pension consolidation is sold on tidiness: one login, one statement, one set of fees. Those benefits are real. What the adverts do not lead with is that a transfer usually cannot be reversed, and that some of the things you give up do not appear anywhere on the annual statement you are comparing.

The short version

  • The normal minimum pension age rises from 55 to 57 on 6 April 2028. Some older schemes carry a protected lower age, and MoneyHelper warns you may have to wait longer to access your money if you move out of one.
  • A pension transfer usually cannot be undone. MoneyHelper's guidance is blunt about this. There is no cooling off that gives you the old scheme back on the old terms.
  • Safeguarded benefits worth more than £30,000 require regulated financial advice before you can transfer. The threshold is applied to the total value of safeguarded benefits under the scheme, not to each part separately.
  • Guaranteed annuity rates are the most valuable thing people throw away. They were often set in the 1980s and 1990s when rates were far higher, and they vanish on transfer.

  • Defined benefit is a different question entirely. The FCA and the Pensions Regulator both say most people are better off keeping one.

1. The case for merging, which is genuinely good

Start with the honest positive, because the rest of this article is cautions.

Most people who have worked for several employers since 2012 have four, five or six small defined contribution pots, each with its own charge, default fund and address on file. The costs are real and mostly invisible:

  • Duplicated charges. Older personal pensions frequently charge more than a modern workplace scheme, and nothing forces the old provider to tell you.
  • Lost pots. A pot from an employer you left in 2014, at an address you moved out of in 2016, is not being watched by anybody.
  • Unsuitable default funds. An old scheme's lifestyling may be moving your money towards bonds on a timetable set for a retirement date you have since changed.
  • Decisions at retirement. Six providers means six identity checks and six chances for something to go wrong when you need the money.

MoneyHelper is clear you do not have to consolidate all or nothing. You can choose which pensions to combine and which to keep separate, which is usually the right shape of answer.

2. The protected pension age nobody mentions

This is the cost most likely to catch someone out, because it never appears on a fee comparison.

The normal minimum pension age, the earliest you can normally take a private pension, is currently 55. It rises to 57 on 6 April 2028. Some older schemes carry a protected age below 55, and some will still allow access from 55 after the change. MoneyHelper lists this directly among the things to check before transferring: you might need to wait longer to access your pension if your current scheme has either of those features.

A protection attached to an old scheme is attached to that scheme. Move the money and, in most cases, the protection does not come with it.

Two years of access is not a rounding error. For anyone planning to stop work at 55, or to bridge a gap before the state pension, losing a protected age moves the whole plan. Because it costs nothing today and surfaces only in 2028, nobody notices at the point of transfer. The check is free: ask each provider in writing whether the policy has a protected pension age.

3. Guarantees, bonuses and the £30,000 rule

Some older policies contain promises nobody sells today.

Guaranteed annuity rates. A promised minimum rate at which you can convert your pot into an income at a set age. Many were written in the 1980s and 1990s when interest rates were far higher, and MoneyHelper notes they may beat anything available today. Transfer out and the guarantee is gone permanently.

With profits terminal bonuses. Your money may sit in a with profits fund that pays an extra amount after a certain date. Leaving early can forfeit it, and the amount is often not shown on the transfer value quotation.

The advice requirement. Where safeguarded benefits, including guaranteed annuity rates, are worth more than £30,000, you must take regulated financial advice before transferring. Government guidance is specific that the threshold applies to the total value of safeguarded benefits under the scheme, so advice can be required even where one component alone is below the line.

If the advice gate applies to you, that is the system telling you there is something here worth paying to understand.

Exit charges. Since 31 March 2017 the FCA has capped early exit charges at 1% of the value of existing contract based personal pensions, and charges already below 1% may not be increased. Firms cannot apply one at all to contracts entered into after those rules took effect. The limit on that protection: it applies once you are eligible to access the pension freedoms, which is to say at normal minimum pension age. Before then, the cap is not doing the work you might assume.

4. Worked example: Gordon in Dundee, four pots

Gordon is 49, lives in Dundee and has four defined contribution pots. He wants one login before he turns 50.

Pot A, £41,000, current workplace scheme, low charge, still receiving contributions. Leave alone, it is the destination not the source.

Pot B, £12,400, opened 2011, charge clearly higher than Pot A, no guarantees, no protected age. Obvious candidate to move.

Pot C, £8,900, opened 2009, similar charge to Pot A, no guarantees. Moving it saves paperwork but little money.

Pot D, £19,600, a personal pension his father set up in 1994. The provider confirms in writing that it carries a guaranteed annuity rate and a protected pension age of 50.

Pot D is the whole story. On paper it is the second largest at a middling charge, so a fee led comparison marks it for consolidation. In reality it gives Gordon access seven years earlier than the post 2028 rules allow, plus a conversion rate no provider would write today.

Gordon moves B and C into A and leaves D where it is. Two pensions instead of four, both guarantees kept, two phone calls and four letters. Note what he did not do: consolidate everything because a comparison table showed lower charges, or decide anything about Pot D without a written answer first.

The honest verdict

Consolidation is usually worth doing, and usually worth doing partially. Plain defined contribution pots with no guarantees, sitting at higher charges than your current scheme, are the easy yes.

The rule that saves people money is duller than any advert. Write to every provider before you move anything and ask four questions. Does this policy have a protected pension age. Does it have a guaranteed annuity rate or other safeguarded benefit. Is there a terminal or loyalty bonus I would lose. What exit charge applies to me today. Get it in writing, because a transfer usually cannot be undone.

If you hold a defined benefit pension, treat it as a separate question with a different default answer. The FCA and the Pensions Regulator both say most people are better off keeping one, and giving up a guaranteed income with automatic annual increases is not a tidiness decision.

Frequently asked

How do I find pensions I have lost track of? The government's free Pension Tracing Service finds the current provider's contact details from an employer or scheme name. It gives you an address, not a balance, so you still need to write to the provider. From 31 October 2026 pensions dashboards begin showing pots in one place, which will make this far easier.

What does regulated advice cost if the £30,000 rule applies? It varies widely, usually a fixed fee or a percentage of the transfer value. Get the fee in writing first, check the adviser on the FCA register, and remember that an adviser recommending against a transfer is doing their job, not failing to.
