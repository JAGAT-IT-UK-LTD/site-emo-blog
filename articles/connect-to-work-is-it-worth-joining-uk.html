1. What the 10 September figures actually say

On 10 September 2026 DWP published the second set of official statistics for Connect to Work, its local employment programme for disabled people, people with health conditions and people with complex barriers to work.

Between April 2025 and the end of June 2026, 25,000 participants started on the programme. The comparable figure for April 2025 to March 2026 was 14,000.

4,100 of those who were out of work started in a job thanks to the programme.

Over a quarter of participants were aged 16 to 24.

The structural facts around it matter as much as the counts. Connect to Work runs across England and Wales, through 48 delivery areas, with local areas designing their own delivery rather than running a single national model. Total confirmed funding is £1.2 billion: £1.1 billion to England and £64.1 million to Wales.

Two exclusions are worth knowing. Greater Manchester is not in the statistics, because it is using its Connect to Work funding to pilot something different called a Prevention Demonstrator. And of the 46 areas open to participants by the end of June 2026, only 28 have delivery data covering six months or more.

2. Why 4,100 out of 25,000 is not the failure rate

The temptation is to divide one by the other, get about 16%, and call it a poor return. That calculation is wrong, and it is wrong for a reason that is stated plainly in DWP's own notes rather than hidden.

The 25,000 is a count of people who started, not a count of people who finished. Someone who joined in May 2026 is in the 25,000 and has had six weeks of support. Someone who joined in early 2025 has had a year and a half. You cannot judge an outcome rate on a population where a large share have barely begun.

The rollout was still happening throughout the period. Full national rollout only completed in July 2026, which is after the data period closes. Only 28 of the areas have six months of data. DWP's own framing is that during this period "delivery was rolling out and ramping up".

So the honest reading is narrower and less quotable. Starts nearly doubled, from 14,000 to 25,000. Job outcomes more than doubled. Both are growing, from a small base, in a programme that was still opening its doors. Whether it works is a question the third or fourth release can answer. This one cannot, and anybody telling you it does, in either direction, is reaching.

3. The word that matters: voluntary

Here is the part that changes whether you should personally care, and it gets almost no coverage.

Participants do not need to be receiving benefits. That is DWP's phrasing, in the release. And the programme is described throughout as voluntary, with participants referred rather than required.

Both halves of that matter. If you are out of work with a health condition and not claiming anything, most government employment support is closed to you because it is attached to a benefit. This one is not. And because it is voluntary, joining it is not a condition of anything and leaving it is not a sanctionable event.

We should be careful about how far that is pushed. The release describes Connect to Work as voluntary and says participants do not need to be on benefits. It does not say anything about how a work coach might treat a refusal in a wider conversation about your claim commitments, because that is a different system. What the page supports is this: Connect to Work is not itself a condition of any benefit. That is the claim, and it is worth having in writing.

What the support actually is: intensive one-to-one help from specialist advisers, who meet people in GP surgeries, cafes, parks or community hubs rather than requiring an office. The listed activities are job matching against skills and circumstances, practical skills support including CV writing, work directly with employers on recruiting and retaining disabled workers, and continued support after someone has started a job.

4. Worked example: Marta in Paisley thinks it through

Marta is 34 and has been out of work for two years with a long-term health condition. She is not claiming any out-of-work benefit, because her partner works and household income rules her out.

The first thing she should check is geography, not eligibility. Paisley is in Scotland. Connect to Work runs in England and Wales only. So on the facts, this programme is not open to her at all, and no amount of reading about it changes that. Scotland runs its own employability provision through Fair Start Scotland and local partnerships.

Why this example is here rather than a tidier one: because "England and Wales" is one line in a press release that is otherwise written as though it covers the country, and it is the single most common way a reader wastes an afternoon.

If Marta lived in Wrexham instead, the answer flips. Wales is in. She is not claiming, which does not matter, because participants do not need to be receiving benefits. She would go through her local delivery area rather than a national application. Her realistic expectation should be set by the data: this is intensive one-to-one support measured over months, not a job placement service, and the current outcome numbers are drawn from a programme that has mostly been running for less than a year in most places.

5. Who it suits, and who it does not

It suits you if you are in England or Wales, out of work with a health condition or disability, and the barrier is not that you cannot find job adverts but that something between you and work needs unpicking. The model is a named adviser over time, in a place you can actually get to. That is a real thing and it is not widely available elsewhere.

It particularly suits you if you are not claiming. This is the rare piece of employment support that does not require a live benefit claim as the ticket in.

It probably does not suit you if you are job-ready and just want vacancies. Intensive one-to-one support is designed for complex barriers, and if that is not your situation you are the wrong fit for the resource.

It cannot suit you at all if you are in Scotland or Northern Ireland. England and Wales only.

And if you are 18 to 24, note that over a quarter of participants are in the 16 to 24 range, but there is also now a separate set of youth-specific routes. Look at both rather than assuming Connect to Work is the only door.

The honest verdict

The numbers published on 10 September do not tell you whether Connect to Work works, and it is worth resisting the pull to make them. A programme that finished rolling out nationally in July 2026 cannot be judged on data that stops in June 2026, and 28 of 46 areas having six months of data is the fact that settles it. Starts nearly doubled and outcomes more than doubled, which is the honest summary and is genuinely all this release supports.

The reason to read about it anyway is the eligibility line. Voluntary, and you do not need to be receiving benefits. For someone with a health condition who has been out of work and is not claiming, that combination is rare enough to be worth a phone call, and there is no cost to the call.

If you are in Scotland or Northern Ireland, none of this applies to you, and no article that skips that line is doing you a service.

Frequently asked

Do I have to be claiming Universal Credit to join? No. DWP's release states plainly that participants do not need to be receiving benefits. That is unusual for government employment support and it is the main reason the programme is worth knowing about.

Can I be sanctioned for refusing it or leaving it? The programme is described as voluntary throughout the release, so Connect to Work is not itself a condition of any benefit. If you are on a benefit with claimant commitments, those are a separate system with their own rules, and how any individual conversation with a work coach goes is not something a press release can answer.

Is it available where I live? It runs in England and Wales, through 48 delivery areas. Greater Manchester is a special case because it is using its funding for a different pilot. Scotland and Northern Ireland are not covered at all.

How long does it last? The release does not give a fixed duration. It describes intensive one-to-one support with a specialist adviser, including continued support after someone starts a job, which suggests it is shaped around the individual rather than run to a set programme length.
