1. How were these Chrome extensions for writers checked?

Every Chrome Web Store listing carries the same block of facts under the heading Details. It shows the version number, the date of the last update, the size, and the developer's name and address.

Above that, next to the rating, sits the user count. Below it, under Privacy, the developer must declare which kinds of data the extension handles. The list is fixed: personally identifiable information, personal communications, location, web history, user activity and website content, among others.

We first opened the four listings on 5 September 2026. We opened them again on 11 November 2026 and copied the same fields. Prices come from each vendor's own pricing page, read the same day. Nothing in this guide about the extensions comes from anywhere else.

The user count matters less than people think. A big number tells you an extension is popular, not that it is safe. The last update date matters more.

An extension that has not been updated in a year or two is either finished, which is rare, or abandoned. An abandoned extension with broad permissions is a standing risk, because whoever controls the developer account can push a new version to every installed copy. Section 7 explains how to use that date.

Permissions work the same way when an agent drives the whole browser rather than one tab. That is what Claude in Chrome asks for before it can act on a page.

2. What changed since September?

Two months is a long time on the Web Store. Every one of the four has shipped at least one new version since September, and the ratings moved a lot.

The biggest change is LanguageTool. In September its listing promised a free tier with no account needed. That line is still on the Web Store listing today. But LanguageTool's own Premium page now puts "Add-on for your browser" in the list headed "Only with Premium".

In plain terms, LanguageTool no longer belongs in a free stack. Free checking still exists on the LanguageTool website, with a limit of 2,000 characters per text field. Premium raises that to 150,000. Its rating fell from 4.7 to 3.3 over the same two months.

Grammarly: the free grammar checker that is left

Grammarly is the default grammar and tone checker. Its listing says it works in Gmail, Google Docs, LinkedIn and more than 500,000 other sites. That makes it the most common answer to the search for Google Docs extensions for writers.

The free tier corrects grammar, spelling and punctuation and suggests clearer, better toned wording. Grammarly's plans page says Free includes 100 AI prompts a month.

Pro adds sentence rewrites, tone adjustment, plagiarism and AI text detection, and 2,000 AI prompts a month. The listing also mentions Superhuman Go, an opt in beta assistant inside Chrome. You can leave it switched off.

LanguageTool: strong on British English, now a paid add-on

LanguageTool supports more than 25 languages and tells British from American English. Its listing says text is checked by sending it to languagetool.org over an encrypted connection.

In September it was the stronger free choice for a writer who works in British English. With the browser add-on now listed as Premium only, it is a paid tool for anyone who wants underlines inside Gmail or Google Docs. At £4.99 a month on the annual plan it is still half the price of Grammarly Pro.

Claude in Chrome: an assistant, not a checker

Claude in Chrome is not a grammar checker. Anthropic's listing says it reads the page you are on, then clicks, types, navigates and fills forms while you watch.

For a writer that means research across a dozen tabs, pulling quotes and figures into a document, and filling a client's content management system. The listing also offers scheduled tasks for recurring browser work.

It needs a paid Claude plan. Anthropic's help centre lists Pro, Max, Ultra, Team and Enterprise. Its 3.5 rating from 2,000 reviews is still the second lowest here, so read the recent reviews first. The Claude plans guide explains what the paid tiers include.

Toggl Track: the timer that pays for the rest

Toggl Track puts a timer into web tools such as Notion, Trello and Asana, so you can start tracking without opening a new tab. It adds a Pomodoro timer, idle detection and tracking reminders.

For a freelancer it is the tool that tells you what an article actually took. That is the number every rate decision depends on, as the freelance rates guide argues at length. Its listing declares only user activity, and the extension is open source.

5. What do the data declarations mean for client work?

Three of the four declare that they handle personal communications and website content. A grammar checker has to read what you type to check it. A browser agent has to read the page to act on it.

That is not sinister, but it matters for freelance work. If a client's contract says their material must not be shared with third parties, sending it to a server for checking may breach that clause.

Some clients care, most have never thought about it. The safe course is to ask before you paste a draft under NDA into anything. If something did go wrong, professional indemnity insurance for freelancers is the cover that deals with client claims.

Claude in Chrome goes further and declares web history, because it can open and navigate tabs. Anthropic's help centre lists fifteen Chrome permissions, including debugger, which lets it click and type, and tabs.

The listing warns that websites can hide instructions that try to redirect Claude. The help centre says the side panel starts in automatically approve mode, where a separate safety check reviews each consequential action. You can switch to asking before it acts.

For a writer, that means keeping it away from your banking tab and client portals until you trust it. Use the ask first setting on sites you do not know. It is the same advice you would give anyone about a new assistant.

Toggl Track declares only user activity, which is the timer data you deliberately create. If you want a productivity extension with the narrowest possible view of your work, that is the one.

6. A worked example in Carlisle

Hannah in Carlisle writes product copy and case studies for three B2B clients. She bills about 20 hours a week at £30 an hour.

In September she ran LanguageTool, Grammarly, Claude and Toggl on free tiers for a month, with Toggl on every job. LanguageTool caught things she would have caught on a second read anyway, saving perhaps ten minutes a week. Claude Free saved her more, mainly on case study outlines and client call summaries, but she hit its limit twice on busy days.

She upgraded one thing: Claude Pro at about £17.92 a month, which also unlocks the Chrome extension for client portal work. Break even at her rate is 36 billed minutes a month.

Her Toggl data for the next month showed the drafting stage of a 1,200 word case study fell from about 2 hours 10 minutes to about 1 hour 25 minutes, across four pieces. That is three hours saved, or £90 at her rate, against £17.92 spent.

In November, LanguageTool's add-on moved behind Premium. Hannah does not pay £4.99 a month for checks she barely needed. She removes it and keeps Grammarly Free, which already sat in the same text boxes. Her monthly bill stays at £17.92.

Her subscription is an allowable expense against self employed profit. The £1,000 trading allowance on GOV.UK is irrelevant at her income, and she is already registered for Self Assessment. The side hustle tax rules guide covers the thresholds for anyone starting out.

7. How do you spot an abandoned Chrome extension?

Open the listing and scroll to Details. If the Updated date is more than twelve months ago, treat the extension as abandoned unless you have a specific reason to trust it.

All four here were updated within three weeks of 11 November 2026. That is what you want to see.

Then read the Privacy section and ask whether the declared categories make sense for the job. A timer does not need your web history. A grammar checker does not obviously need your location, yet both Grammarly and LanguageTool declare it and neither explains why on the listing.

Next, compare the listing with the vendor's own pricing page. LanguageTool shows why. The store listing still says no registration is needed, while the vendor's page now puts the add-on behind Premium. When the two disagree, trust the vendor's page and expect a paywall.

Finally, look at the developer block. Grammarly, LanguageTool and Toggl identify themselves as traders under EU rules, with a business address and a D-U-N-S number.

Anthropic's listing is still marked non-trader, which the store notes means EU consumer rights do not apply to that contract. That is worth knowing even though the company is well known. A listing with no developer address, no website and no recent update is one to leave alone, whatever its user count.

The honest verdict

Install Grammarly, Claude and Toggl Track on their free tiers, run Toggl on every job for a month, and let the timer tell you which one deserves money. For most UK writers the first and often only upgrade worth about £17.92 is Claude Pro, because it turns the extension into something that does research and portal work rather than just checking commas. LanguageTool is now a paid add-on: worth £4.99 a month only if you write in more than one language. Skip paying for two grammar checkers, keep NDA material out of anything that sends text to a server unless the client has agreed, and re-check the Updated date on every extension you keep at least twice a year.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Grammarly Chrome extension free?

Yes, the free tier is. The Chrome Web Store listing and grammarly.com/plans both describe a free plan with grammar, spelling, punctuation and tone help, plus 100 AI prompts a month. Pro was shown at £10 on the plans page on 11 November 2026, with a free trial. Check the billing period and trial length at checkout.

Is the LanguageTool extension still free?

Not in full. On 11 November 2026 LanguageTool's own Premium page listed "Add-on for your browser" under "Only with Premium", even though the Web Store listing still says no account is needed. Free checking remains on the LanguageTool website, limited to 2,000 characters per text field. Premium costs £4.99 a month on the annual plan.

Does Claude in Chrome work on the free Claude plan?

No. The Web Store listing says it is available on all paid plans, and Anthropic's help centre names Pro, Max, Ultra, Team and Enterprise. Claude Pro is USD 20 a month before tax. With 20% VAT at HMRC's November 2026 exchange rate, that is about £17.92 a month, or about £14.93 a month if billed annually.

What are the best Chrome extensions for writers who work for UK clients?

Start with a grammar checker, an assistant and a timer. On our November 2026 check, Grammarly Free, Claude and Toggl Track Free cover that for nothing. Add LanguageTool Premium if you write in several languages. Whatever you install, check the Updated date and the Privacy section on the listing, and ask clients before using any of them on confidential work.

Are these extensions safe with client work under NDA?

Three of the four declare that they handle personal communications and website content, so the text you write or read passes through their servers. That may breach a confidentiality clause. Ask the client before using them on confidential material. Toggl Track declares only user activity, so it is the safest of the four for sensitive projects.
