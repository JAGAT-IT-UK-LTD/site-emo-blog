Old storage heaters are the most expensive way to heat a British home short of running a plug-in fan heater all winter. Replacing them is one of the few heating upgrades where the arithmetic actually works, and it works because of losses, not efficiency.

The short version

  • Every electric heater is 100 per cent efficient. A new storage heater does not turn electricity into heat any better than a 1980s one. It simply loses less of it before you want it.
  • Old brick heaters leak heat all day into an empty house, so by evening you top up on the expensive peak rate. That top-up is where the money goes.
  • High heat retention heaters are quoted at around a 30 per cent reduction in heating cost against old units, because the insulated core holds the charge until you call for it.
  • On a house spending about £1,600 a year on old storage heating, 30 per cent is about £480. A four-heater replacement at about £2,400 fitted is therefore a five year payback.
  • Do the two free checks first. If your loft is under 100mm of insulation, or you are not actually on an Economy 7 style tariff, fix that before you spend a penny on heaters.

1. Why old storage heaters cost so much, and it is not efficiency

Start with the thing that trips everyone up. Every electric heater on the market is 100 per cent efficient. A bar fire, a fan heater, an oil filled radiator, an infrared panel and a storage heater all turn one kilowatt hour of electricity into exactly one kilowatt hour of heat. There is nowhere else for the energy to go.

So a new storage heater cannot be more efficient than an old one. What it can be is better at holding the heat until you want it, and that is a completely different property.

A 1980s storage heater is a stack of dense ceramic bricks with a modest amount of insulation and simple vents. It charges overnight on the cheap rate, then begins leaking heat into the room from about six in the morning whether anyone is there or not. By early evening, when the house is full and cold, most of the charge has already been spent warming an empty room.

What happens next is the expensive part. You turn on a plug-in heater, or the storage heater's own boost element, and that heat comes off the peak rate, which is roughly two to three times the off-peak rate. A household can easily spend a quarter of its heating bill on top-ups that exist only because the morning heat was wasted.

A high heat retention heater attacks exactly that. Heavier insulation, a sealed core, a thermostat and a fan that only pushes heat out when the room calls for it. The charge is still bought at the cheap rate, but far more of it survives until evening, so the peak-rate top-up shrinks or disappears.

2. The rate on your bill is the whole calculation

This is the one place where a page like this has to be careful. Ofgem's price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 sets the single rate electricity unit at 26.32p per kWh with a daily standing charge of 54.83p. That figure is published and checkable.

Economy 7 and similar two-rate tariffs are set differently by each supplier and each region, with a cheaper night rate and a dearer day rate. There is no single national number, so this page will not invent one. Go and read the two unit rates off your own bill or your supplier's app. They will be printed as a day rate and a night rate in pence per kWh.

Once you have them, the arithmetic is simple, and here is the sum in words: the annual saving equals the kilowatt hours you currently top up at the day rate, multiplied by the gap between your day rate and your night rate. That is the money a better heater recovers, because it moves that consumption back overnight.

Two warnings while you are looking at the bill. First, check you are actually on a two-rate tariff at all. A surprising number of homes with storage heaters were moved onto a single rate at some point and are now charging bricks overnight at the full day price, which is the most expensive heating arrangement it is possible to have. Second, check the night hours. They are typically seven hours somewhere between midnight and 8am, they vary by supplier and by region, and running a washing machine outside them costs you the day rate.

The fourth row is the honest headline. Published figures for high heat retention units put the saving at around 30 per cent of the heating cost, not merely the peak top-up, because a well-insulated core also wastes less overall. On a house spending about £1,600 a year, 30 per cent is £480, and £2,400 divided by £480 is five years.

The last two rows matter more than the rest. If you are on a single rate, or the loft is bare, spending £2,400 on heaters is spending in the wrong order.

4. The three things to fix before you buy heaters

One: check the tariff. Storage heaters only make sense on a tariff with a genuinely cheap night rate. If you are on a single rate, moving to a two-rate tariff costs nothing but a phone call and can cut the same bill by a third with no hardware at all. Do that first and then re-run the sum, because the payback on new heaters will look different once the tariff is right.

Two: insulate the loft. Our page on loft insulation payback works through the numbers. Topping a bare or thin loft up to 270mm is a few hundred pounds and it reduces the heat you need to store in the first place, which makes every heater in the house smaller and cheaper to run. It is the wrong order to buy expensive heaters that then push heat straight through the ceiling.

Three: check the hot water. A lot of storage heater houses also have an immersion heater on the same off-peak circuit, and a cylinder with thin factory insulation. A £30 cylinder jacket pays back in about eight months, which is the best return anywhere on this site and about ten times faster than new heaters.

Only when those three are done does replacing the heaters become the best remaining pound you can spend.

5. Worked example: Steffan in Merthyr Tydfil

Steffan has four brick storage heaters fitted in about 1987, a two-rate tariff at 11p night and 32p day, and an all-electric two-bedroom terrace. His annual electricity is 5,200 kWh, of which he reckons about 4,000 is heating.

He tracks a week in January on his smart meter app and finds he is drawing roughly 25 kWh a night on the cheap rate and another 5 kWh most evenings on the day rate from a plug-in oil filled radiator in the sitting room.

Over a 150 day heating season that evening top-up is 750 kWh at 32p, which is £240 a year. His night charging is about 3,750 kWh at 11p, or £412.50. Total £652.50 for heating.

He is quoted £2,300 for three high heat retention heaters plus an electrician's day. If the new heaters remove the evening top-up entirely and cut the stored requirement by 30 per cent, his night charge falls to about 2,625 kWh, so £288.75, and the top-up goes to zero. New total £288.75, saving £363.75 a year. £2,300 divided by £363.75 is 6.3 years.

Then he checks the loft and finds 75mm of 1980s mineral wool. Topping it to 270mm costs him £520 and, on Energy Saving Trust figures for a terraced house, saves considerably more per pound spent than the heaters do. He does that first, re-measures the following winter, and discovers his heating demand has dropped enough that three heaters, not four, will do the job.

That is the correct order, and it saved him the price of a heater.

6. The honest verdict

If your storage heaters are pre-2000 bricks, you are on a two-rate tariff, and your loft is properly insulated, replace them. A payback of five to seven years on hardware that lasts twenty is a good deal, and the comfort difference is larger than the money difference.

If you are on a single rate tariff, do not buy anything. Change the tariff. That is free and it is worth more than the heaters.

If the loft is thin, insulate it first. Every pound of insulation makes every subsequent pound of heating cheaper, and it never works the other way round.

Frequently asked

Are storage heaters cheaper than a heat pump? Almost never on running cost. A heat pump delivers roughly three units of heat per unit of electricity, so it beats any resistive heater including a storage heater by a wide margin. It also costs several times more to install. Our page on the heat pump grant works through that comparison.

How do I tell a high heat retention heater from an ordinary modern one? Look for the specification, not the styling. A high heat retention unit will state heavy insulation, a fan-assisted output and programmable controls, and it will feel cool to the touch when it is holding a charge. A slimline heater that is warm all over all day is not one.

Can I fit them myself? The heaters themselves are usually hard-wired on a dedicated off-peak circuit, which is notifiable electrical work. A competent person scheme electrician is the sensible route, and the certificate matters when you sell.

What if I only replace one heater? That is a reasonable way to spread the cost, and it works. Start with the room you spend most evenings in, because that is where the peak-rate top-up is happening. The payback on that single heater is usually the best of the set.
