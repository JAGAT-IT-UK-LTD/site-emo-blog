Frozen tax thresholds after the Budget: the £12,570 allowance stays to 2031, and here is the cost

The Budget on 28 October 2026 kept the frozen tax thresholds exactly where they were. The personal allowance stays £12,570 and the higher rate still starts above £50,270 in 2027 to 2028, with the freeze running to April 2031. Here is what that does to a real payslip, with the arithmetic shown.

The short version

  • The Budget on 28 October 2026 kept the freeze. The personal allowance stays £12,570 in 2027 to 2028, the same as it has been since April 2022.
  • The higher rate threshold stays £50,270, which is the personal allowance plus the £37,700 basic rate limit. The additional rate still starts above £125,140.
  • The freeze runs to 5 April 2031. That was set at the 2025 Budget and the 2026 Budget did not change it.
  • No headline rate went up on wages. The Budget document says there is no rise to the headline rates of income tax, National Insurance or VAT.
  • One new relief: a Pension Tax Allowance from 6 April 2027, so pensioners whose income is at or under the full new State Pension are not pulled into paying small amounts of tax.
  • With CPI at 3.6% in October 2026, one year of the freeze costs a basic rate taxpayer about £90 and someone just over £50,270 about £362, compared with thresholds that rose with prices.

1. What did the Budget on 28 October do to frozen tax thresholds?

It kept them frozen. The Chancellor, John Healey, presented Budget 2026 on Wednesday 28 October 2026.

The rates and allowances annex published that day shows the personal allowance at £12,570 for both 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028. The basic rate band is £1 to £37,700 of taxable income in both years.

So the higher rate threshold stays £50,270. The additional rate still starts above £125,140. The £100,000 point where the personal allowance starts to taper away is unchanged too.

The weekly National Insurance Primary Threshold stays £242 and the Upper Earnings Limit stays £967 in 2027 to 2028. Those are the National Insurance versions of the same freeze.

Before the day there was talk that the freeze might be eased or extended again. Neither happened. The end date is still 5 April 2031, the date set at the 2025 Budget.

What the Budget did say, in its own words, is that there is no increase to the headline rates of income tax, National Insurance contributions or VAT. That is true for wages and pensions. It is not true for savings interest and property income, which are taxed at 24%, 44% and 49% from 6 April 2027. Our Autumn Budget 2026 summary covers the rest of the day.

2. What are the tax thresholds for 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028?

For England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the figures on wages and pensions are the same in both tax years.

  • Personal allowance: £12,570. Unchanged since April 2022.
  • Basic rate limit: £37,700, taxed at 20%.
  • Higher rate threshold: £50,270, which is the personal allowance plus the basic rate limit. Income above it is taxed at 40%.
  • Additional rate threshold: £125,140, above which the rate is 45%.

The freeze was first legislated to run to April 2028. At the 2025 Budget the Chancellor extended it by a further three years. The personal allowance, the basic rate limit and the matching National Insurance thresholds are now held until 5 April 2031.

That is a nine year freeze on a figure that, in a normal year, would rise with inflation. Our longer guide to frozen tax thresholds has the full history.

3. How does a freeze take money without a rate changing?

The idea is simple. It is worth spelling out, because "fiscal drag" is a phrase that explains nothing to anybody.

Income tax is charged on what you earn above the personal allowance. If your pay rises 4% and the allowance rises 4% too, the share of your income that is taxed stays roughly the same.

If your pay rises 4% and the allowance does not move, the whole 4% sits in taxable territory. A larger share of everything you earn is taxed.

Here is the same idea as a number. CPI inflation was 3.6% in the 12 months to October 2026, the latest ONS figure. If every threshold had risen by that instead of being held, this is where each one would sit, and what the gap costs someone whose income lands just above it.

Two things in that table are worth saying out loud.

The heaviest single hit is not at the top of the scale. It is at £100,000, where the personal allowance is withdrawn at £1 for every £2 of income. The effective rate on the next slice is 60%.

And every figure above is one year of drift only. The freeze runs to 5 April 2031, so each year stacks another gap on the one before. A freeze that looks small in year one is not small by year five.

No rate changed. No announcement was needed. The bill went up anyway.

The honest other side: this barely touches some people. Anyone whose total income stays under £12,570 for the whole year pays no income tax either way.

Anyone whose pay is not rising is not being dragged across anything either. They have the worse problem of a falling real wage.

The freeze bites hardest on people who are getting rises and sit just under a line. That is the group least likely to think of themselves as well off.

Two effects follow. People on low incomes who were below the allowance are pulled into paying income tax at all. People nearing £50,270 are pulled into the 40% band on part of their income for the first time.

4. A worked example: Aisha's payslip, with the arithmetic

Aisha is a senior nurse in Nottingham. Her salary this tax year is £48,000.

Her taxable income is £48,000 minus the personal allowance of £12,570. That is £35,430.

That sits entirely within the basic rate limit of £37,700, so all of it is taxed at 20%. £35,430 at 20% is £7,086 of income tax for the year.

Now suppose she gets a 5% pay rise in April 2027, taking her to £50,400. The Budget kept the thresholds the same for 2027 to 2028, so the sums use the same lines.

Her taxable income becomes £50,400 minus £12,570, which is £37,830. The first £37,700 is taxed at 20%, giving £7,540. The remaining £130 sits above the higher rate threshold and is taxed at 40%, giving £52.

Total income tax: £7,540 plus £52, which is £7,592.

So a pay rise of £2,400 increased her income tax by £506. It also made her a higher rate taxpayer for the first time.

The 40% is only charged on £130 of her income, not on all of it. This is the single most misunderstood point in British personal tax. She is not "taxed 40% on her salary". She is taxed 40% on the last £130 of it.

Had the thresholds risen with October's 3.6% inflation, her higher rate threshold would be about £52,080. Her whole £50,400 would then sit in the basic rate band, and the £52 at 40% would not exist.

Note what we have not calculated here. National Insurance and any student loan repayment come on top, and her pension contributions would reduce the taxable figure.

We have not put numbers on those because we would need her exact National Insurance category, loan plan and contribution rate. Inventing them would make the example look more precise while making it less true.

5. Who do frozen thresholds hit hardest?

The freeze bites hardest on two groups.

First, people just below the personal allowance. Any pay rise now creates an income tax bill that did not exist before.

Second, people in the low £40,000s. They are on a path to crossing £50,270 within a few normal pay rises, like Aisha.

It barely touches people whose income is well above £125,140. The additional rate is where they already sit, and the marginal change is small next to their bill.

Being blunt: if you are on a fixed income below £12,570 and it is not rising, this freeze does nothing to you at all. That is not good news. It just means this article is not about your problem.

Two important qualifications.

First, Scotland sets its own income tax rates and bands on non savings, non dividend income. A Scottish taxpayer's bands differ from the figures above, although the personal allowance itself is set UK wide.

Second, the freeze applies to the matching National Insurance thresholds as well as to income tax. That is why the effect on a payslip is larger than an income tax sum alone suggests.

There is also a sharper edge above £100,000, where the personal allowance is withdrawn by £1 for every £2 of income. That creates the band between £100,000 and £125,140 where the effective marginal rate is far higher than the headline 40%. A frozen £100,000 starting point drags more people into it each year.

Parents in that range can lose more than tax. Our guide to the High Income Child Benefit Charge shows how the two edges stack.

6. What about pensioners and the State Pension?

This is the one place the Budget softened the freeze.

The full new State Pension is close to the £12,570 personal allowance. As it rises each April, more pensioners with almost no other income would have started to owe small amounts of tax.

The Budget's answer is a new Pension Tax Allowance. It applies from 6 April 2027 to 5 April 2030 and will be legislated in Finance Bill 2026 to 27.

Its value is worked out by applying the 20% basic rate to the gap between the full new State Pension and the personal allowance. People over State Pension age whose total income is at or below the full new State Pension get it automatically.

For 2027 to 2028 the annex sets the point where the full allowance applies at £13,037 of income. It is withdrawn at £1 for every £3 above that, and it has gone completely at £13,319.

So a pensioner whose only income is the full new State Pension should not pay income tax in 2027 to 2028. A pensioner with a small private pension on top still will. Our guide to the State Pension rise in April 2027 covers the payment itself.

7. What can you do about it before April 2027?

Threshold changes normally take effect from the start of a tax year. With nothing changed for 2027 to 2028, there is no new date to wait for. The useful move is to know where you sit against each line.

  • Just over £50,270? Pension contributions reduce your taxable income. A higher rate taxpayer in a relief at source scheme has to claim the extra relief. See how to claim higher rate pension tax relief.
  • On a salary sacrifice scheme at work? It can also save National Insurance. Our salary sacrifice guide explains the cap due from 2029.
  • Married, and one of you earns under £12,570? The Marriage Allowance moves £1,260 of allowance across. See who can claim the Marriage Allowance.
  • A second job? Check the tax code. Our guide to the BR tax code on a second job shows what a wrong code costs.

The honest verdict: the Budget changed nothing on wages. The freeze is legislated to 2031, and every pay rise between now and then is taxed a little harder than it would have been. Knowing which line you are near is worth more than any rumour.

Frequently asked

What are frozen tax thresholds? They are tax bands that stay at the same cash level instead of rising with prices. The personal allowance has been £12,570 since April 2022. As pay rises and the bands do not, more of your income is taxed, and some of it at a higher rate. Nothing on your payslip says the rate went up, but the bill does.

How long are the tax thresholds frozen? The personal allowance, the basic rate limit and the matching National Insurance thresholds are held until 5 April 2031. That end date was set at the 2025 Budget. The 2026 Budget on 28 October left it in place, and its rates annex shows the same figures for 2027 to 2028 as for this year.

Did the 2026 Budget raise the personal allowance? No. The personal allowance stays £12,570 in 2027 to 2028 and the higher rate still starts above £50,270. The one new relief is a Pension Tax Allowance from 6 April 2027, which stops pensioners whose income is at or under the full new State Pension from paying small amounts of tax.

If I cross £50,270, is all my income taxed at 40%? No. Only the income above £50,270 is taxed at 40%. Everything below it is taxed at the rates that apply to those bands. In our example, a nurse on £50,400 pays 40% on just £130 of her pay. This is the most common misunderstanding in UK income tax.

Why is the higher rate threshold £50,270? It is the personal allowance of £12,570 plus the basic rate limit of £37,700. Because both parts are frozen, the threshold is frozen too. In Scotland the bands are set by the Scottish Parliament and differ, although the personal allowance is the same UK wide.
