New subscription rules are due on 1 January 2027: what changes when you cancel or a plan renews

Ruhul Amin · Published 16 November 2026 · 10 min read · Figures checked November 2026

A new UK law is due to start on 1 January 2027 that makes subscriptions easier to leave. Firms must remind you before renewals, let you cancel online if you signed up online, and give you 14 days to change your mind after an auto renewal. The detail is still in draft, so here is what is fixed, what is not, and what to do before the date.

The short version

  • The new subscription regulations say they come into force on 1 January 2027. They are still a draft on legislation.gov.uk and the page says they have not yet been made. Source: legislation.gov.uk, read 16 November 2026.
  • You get a 14 day cooling off period after a subscription renews. That covers a free trial that turns into a paid plan and a contract of 12 months or more that renews by itself. Source: GOV.UK consultation response.
  • If you signed up online, you must be able to leave online. The government says firms must make exit straightforward, and offers or feedback requests must not delay you.
  • Firms must send renewal reminders. The Act ties them to six month periods and to the first payment after a free trial. Source: DMCC Act 2024, sections 258 and 259.
  • Refunds must be made without undue delay and within 14 days, to the original payment method unless you agree otherwise (draft regulations).
  • It only covers contracts you enter into after the start date. Your existing subscriptions will not gain these rights.

1. Is it actually law yet, and who does it cover?

Not quite, and it is worth being exact about that. The rules sit in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, Part 4, Chapter 2. On legislation.gov.uk those sections are still marked as prospective, which means not yet in force.

The detail comes from a second piece of law. The Consumer Subscription Contracts (Cancellation and Information) Regulations 2026 are published as a draft. The page says the instrument has not yet been made. If it is made, regulation 1 says it comes into force on 1 January 2027.

That is a change of date. The government's own consultation response had said it expected the regime to start in spring 2027. The Competition and Markets Authority then wrote in October 2026 that the new regime "will come into force in January".

So treat 1 January 2027 as the planned date, not a guarantee. If Parliament does not approve the draft in time, the date can move again.

Who is covered? A subscription contract, in the Act, is one with automatic recurring supply and payment where you have a right to end it. A deal with a free or discounted first period that then rolls onto a higher price counts too. The Act calls that first stage a concessionary period.

The government response says charitable cultural and heritage memberships, such as museum, gallery and heritage site access, are excluded. Our reading of the draft regulations is that they only apply to contracts entered into once the new rules start. Your current gym, streaming or software plan is not rewritten on New Year's Day.

2. What changes for you on 1 January 2027?

Five things matter in practice.

Clearer information before you commit. The draft regulations list what a firm must tell you before you sign up. That includes the minimum total amount you will pay, the steps to cancel and the contact details for doing it, and a summary of your cancellation rights. If the firm leaves it out, you can get a right to cancel.

A clear payment step online. For online contracts the Act requires an express acknowledgement of the payment obligation at the final step. If that was missed, section 257 says you are not bound by the contract.

Reminders. Under sections 258 and 259, a firm must send a reminder for each renewal payment tied to a six month period. For a plan with a free or discounted start, the first reminder comes before the first full price payment. The notice must arrive a reasonable time before the last date you can cancel. If the next payment is more than 12 months away, an extra earlier reminder is needed. The government also says reminders must be in writing on a durable medium, such as an email, with their purpose obvious.

Leaving must be straightforward. Section 260 says ending a subscription must be straightforward. The government response is more concrete: if you signed up online, you must be able to exit online. You must be able to switch off auto renewal at any time. Terms that make cancelling disproportionately difficult are to be banned.

A firm may still make you a retention offer or ask why you are leaving. It just cannot use that to frustrate or unreasonably delay you.

A second chance after renewal. This is the headline. A new 14 day cooling off period starts at each relevant renewal, after a free trial ends or after a contract of 12 months or more auto renews. Section 264 says you can cancel in any circumstances, with no penalty or charge. The government says you should not be "out of pocket if they miss the auto renewal date".

The refund will be full or proportionate, depending on whether the supply has begun. The draft regulations add that refunds must be made without undue delay and within 14 days, using the same payment method unless you agree otherwise.

4. What happens to free trials?

This is where many people lose money. You hand over card details for a seven day trial, forget it, and a full price month is taken.

The Act treats a free trial that turns into a paid plan as a subscription contract with a concessionary period. That brings in the reminder before the first full payment and the 14 day cooling off after it starts.

The rules do not ban free trials. They do not stop a firm asking for a card. They make it harder for the trial to turn into a bill you did not see coming, and they give you a safety net if it does.

Worked example: Hannah Doyle in Bristol

These figures are made up for illustration. The arithmetic is ours, not an official calculation.

On 10 January 2027 Hannah buys a one year photo editing plan for £84, which works out at £7 a month. It auto renews on 10 January 2028. Under the new rules the firm must send her a reminder before that date, and she must be able to cancel online because she signed up online.

Suppose the reminder goes to a spam folder. The plan renews and takes £84 on 10 January 2028. She spots it on 20 January, 10 days later.

That is inside the 14 day renewal cooling off period. If the refund is proportionate to use, our arithmetic is 10 days out of 365, so about £2.30 of use (£84 × 10 ÷ 365). The refund would then be about £81.70. If the firm treats it as a full refund, she gets the full £84.

The lesson is the date, not the app. Whether the new rules apply depends on when she entered the contract. A plan signed on 31 December 2026 is outside them, and one signed on 1 January 2027 is inside, if the draft is made as planned.

5. What to do before 1 January

Because the rules only look forward, the date you sign matters more than usual.

List what you already pay. Check your bank statements for the last three months and write down each recurring payment, its price and its renewal date. You will not get the new rights on these, so put the cancel by date in your diary.

Think twice before an annual plan in December. A 12 month plan bought before the rules start has no renewal cooling off. The same plan bought after may have. If the price difference is small, waiting a few days for the new protections can be worth it.

Do not rely on the law before it exists. Until the regulations are made, a firm is not bound by them. Keep the sign up email and the cancellation steps in case you need to argue your case.

Know your other routes. If a firm will not refund you, paying by credit card can give you Section 75 protection on items over £100. Our guide to Section 75 versus chargeback sets out when each works. Our 30 day refund guide covers faulty goods.

6. The honest verdict

This is a useful change for people who sign up to things and forget. A reminder, an online exit and 14 days after a renewal remove the worst of the subscription trap.

But it is not a rule you can lean on yet. The regulations are a draft, the date has moved before, and the final guidance is still to come. The rights also only apply to contracts you enter into after the start, so none of your current plans improve.

The practical advice is dull and it works. Diary every renewal date today, cancel what you do not use, and keep the sign up emails. After 1 January 2027, treat the new rules as a backstop, not a substitute for checking.

Frequently asked

Do the new subscription rules apply to my existing subscriptions? Our reading of the draft regulations is no. They apply to contracts entered into once the new regime starts, planned for 1 January 2027. A plan you already have keeps its current terms. If you want the new protections, they would only come with a new or replacement contract signed after that date.

Can a company still make me phone to cancel? Not if you signed up online. The government response says people who signed up online must be able to exit online, and that firms must make leaving straightforward. Retention offers are allowed, but they must not frustrate or unreasonably delay you. Until the rules start, no such general duty exists.

Will I get a reminder before my free trial ends? If the new rules apply, yes. The Act requires a reminder before the first full price payment for a plan with a free or discounted start. The reminder must be in writing on a durable medium and arrive a reasonable time before your last day to cancel. The trader sets that notice period.

What if I miss the renewal and get charged? You get a 14 day cooling off period after an auto renewal, under the new rules. You can cancel without penalty and the refund will be full or proportionate to use. The firm must refund within 14 days, to the original payment method. Act quickly, because the 14 days run from renewal.
