Bank Rate rose to 4% on 5 November 2026. Here is what it means for your money

1. What the Bank decided on 5 November

At its meeting ending on 4 November 2026, the Monetary Policy Committee voted by 8 to 1 to raise Bank Rate by 0.25 percentage points, to 4%. The decision was published on Thursday 5 November.

The eight who voted for 4% were Andrew Bailey, Sarah Breeden, Swati Dhingra, Megan Greene, Clare Lombardelli, Huw Pill, Dave Ramsden and Alan Taylor. Catherine L Mann voted for a rise of 0.5 points, to 4.25%.

The votes had been building towards this. Bank Rate was held at 3.75% at the meetings ending 17 June, 29 July and 16 September 2026. The June vote was 7 to 2 to hold. July and September were both 6 to 3, with Greene, Mann and Pill wanting 4% in September. We covered that meeting in what the 17 September 2026 decision meant.

In November nobody voted to hold. That is the real news. The question has moved from whether the Bank raises to how far it goes.

2. Why it raised, and what the Report says next

The Bank's summary is plain about the cause. The conflict in the Middle East is in its ninth month, and global energy prices have kept rising from an already high level.

UK CPI inflation rose to 3.5% in September, from 3.1% in August, on ONS figures. The target is 2%.

The Committee said there is still limited evidence of knock-on effects on other prices and wages. But it judged that the pressure already building up was now enough to justify a rise. Activity has also been stronger than expected, which added to the case.

The November Monetary Policy Report, published with the decision, sets out the forecast. Its central projection has CPI inflation at around 5% in the second quarter of 2027, then falling back. In the final quarter of each year it shows 3.8% for 2026, 4.0% for 2027 and 2.0% for 2028.

The Report also shows where markets expect rates to go. Over the 15 working days to 27 October, the market curve rose to around 4.9% at the end of 2027, before easing to around 4.7% by late 2029. That is a market price, not a promise. The Committee says policy is not on a pre-set path.

The remaining dates are Thursday 17 December 2026, with no Report, then Thursday 4 February 2027, with one. Watch the December vote for how many members want to go further.

3. Savers: the rise only helps if your bank passes it on

A higher Bank Rate does not raise your savings rate by itself. Your provider decides whether to pass the 0.25 points on, how much, and when.

The gap is already wide. Moneyfacts said on 5 November that a typical easy access account paid 2.56% at the start of the month. A selection of easy access accounts from the big banks paid just 1.16% on average. The most competitive accounts paid more than 4%.

The Bank's own Report shows the same thing from its data. Rates on instant access deposits stayed largely flat between the July and November Reports, even while mortgage rates rose.

So the useful job this month is simple. Log in, find the actual rate on each savings account, and compare it with Bank Rate at 4% and inflation at 3.5%. Any account paying under 3.5% is losing buying power while it appears to earn.

Fixed rate bonds work the other way. A bond you already hold keeps its rate to the end of the term. If you are choosing a new one, Moneyfacts says many market leading bonds pay over 5%. Interest above your allowance is taxable, so read how tax on savings interest works before you move a large sum.

4. Borrowers: trackers, fixes and the standard variable rate

On a tracker mortgage, the rise feeds straight through, usually from the next payment or within a month or two. On a £200,000 repayment mortgage over 25 years, a move from 4.50% to 4.75% takes the payment from £1,111.66 to £1,140.23. That is about £29 a month, on our arithmetic.

On a fixed rate, nothing changes until your deal ends. New fixes are priced off swap rates, which follow where markets expect Bank Rate to be over two or five years. Lenders priced this rise in early. Moneyfacts records the average two year fix rising from 5.59% at the start of September to 5.97% on 1 November, and the five year from 5.63% to 6.00%.

The Bank's Report sees the same move. Quoted rates on two year fixes at 75% and 90% loan to value rose by around 60 basis points on average between the July and November Reports. By the end of 2029 it projects average annual mortgage payments around £1,500 higher than before the conflict began.

On a standard variable rate, your lender decides whether to pass the rise on. Moneyfacts put the average SVR at 7.13% on 1 November. If your fix ends soon, our guide to mortgage rates after the rise and whether to fix now runs the numbers.

On credit cards and overdrafts the link is weak. Those rates follow the lender's own risk pricing far more than Bank Rate.

5. A worked example: what checking one rate is worth now

Yvonne is in Bristol and has £12,000 sitting in an easy access account she opened in 2024.

She checks the account and finds it is paying 1.90%. Bank Rate is now 4%. She is not comparing her account to a best buy table. She is comparing it to Bank Rate, which is the fairer test of whether her provider passes anything on.

At 1.90%, £12,000 earns £228 in a year before tax.

Moneyfacts says the most competitive easy access accounts now pay more than 4%. At 4.00%, £12,000 would earn £480 in a year before tax.

The difference is £252 a year, for one afternoon of admin. If her bank passes on the whole 0.25 points, she moves to 2.15% and earns £258. The gap barely shrinks. The rise on 5 November is worth £30 to her. Switching is worth more than eight times that.

The gap is only half the story. Inflation is 3.5%, so the real test is whether an account pays more than 3.5%. The table below runs Yvonne's two rates across a range of balances and adds the real return, which is roughly what the money is worth after a year once prices have risen.

Yvonne is the £12,000 row, and the last two columns say the part most articles leave out. She is not earning £228 a year. After 3.5% inflation she is losing about £192 of buying power. Moving to 4.00% takes her from losing £192 to gaining about £60. That is the honest size of the prize, and it is still worth an afternoon.

The opposite case is just as real. Priya is in Leicester with £6,000 in easy access at 3.55%. Over a year that pays £213.00. An account at 4.00% would pay £240.00, so moving gains her £27.00. Her real return today is only 0.05 points above inflation, about £3 on the year.

For Priya the honest answer has changed since September. The Bank expects inflation to rise towards 5% by the middle of 2027. If her rate does not move, her real return turns negative without her doing anything wrong. A £27 gain is small, but it is now worth a short check, starting with whether her own bank passes the rise on.

Fixing is a closer call than it was. A one year fixed bond locks your rate, which protects you if easy access rates drift down, but leaves you behind if Bank Rate keeps rising. Keep it in proportion: on £10,000 a quarter of a percentage point is £25 a year. It is a reason to think for five minutes, not a reason to panic.

That is the honest point of this article. The 5 November rise was real, but the money most households leave on the table is not lost to the Bank of England. It is lost to a savings rate nobody has looked at in two years.

One caveat worth stating plainly. If your savings are already in a competitive account paying near Bank Rate, there is nothing here for you to do. Moving for a few basis points is not worth the afternoon. This article does not help everyone.
