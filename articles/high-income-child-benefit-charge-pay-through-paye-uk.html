The 31 January 2027 Fork: Pay the Child Benefit Charge Through Your Tax Code, or Get Dragged Into Self Assessment for Another Year

Ruhul Amin · Published 5 September 2026 · 6 min read · Figures checked September 2026

For a decade the High Income Child Benefit Charge forced hundreds of thousands of employees who had never filled in a tax return to start. Since the 2025/26 tax year there is a second door: pay the charge through your PAYE tax code and never file. But the door closes on 31 January 2027 for last year's charge, and it is only open to people with no other reason to file. Here is the rule as GOV.UK states it on 5 September 2026, the arithmetic at two typical salaries, the pension contribution that shrinks the bill, and what the PAYE route does not do.

The short version

  • The charge applies when the higher earner's adjusted net income is over £60,000 and takes 1% of the Child Benefit received for every £200 over, so 100% at £80,000. Source: GOV.UK, High Income Child Benefit Charge, read 5 September 2026.
  • Child Benefit in 2026/27 is £27.05 a week for the eldest child and £17.90 for each other child, so £1,406.60 a year for one child and £2,337.40 for two. Those are the sums the percentage bites into.
  • The PAYE route: GOV.UK says you can pay the charge through PAYE if you do not need to send a tax return for another reason, you are paying for the current or previous tax year, and it is on or before 31 January after the tax year. Its own example: for 2025/26, on or before 31 January 2027.
  • Miss 31 January 2027 and the 2025/26 charge must go through Self Assessment, with the registration, the return and the late-filing penalties that come with it.
  • What the PAYE route does NOT do: it does not reduce the charge by a penny, it does not work if you are self-employed, a landlord or have savings interest that needs a return, and it does not remove the charge for a partner who earns more than you.
  • Our verdict: if you are PAYE-only and between £60,000 and £80,000, register for the PAYE route now, and look at whether a pension contribution takes your adjusted net income under £60,000, because that removes the charge rather than just moving it.

1. The charge in numbers

The High Income Child Benefit Charge is a tax charge on the higher earner in a household where someone gets Child Benefit and one partner's adjusted net income is over £60,000. GOV.UK's rule since the 2024/25 tax year: "you'll pay back 1% of your Child Benefit for every £200 you earn over the threshold", and at £80,000 or more you pay back all of it. GOV.UK's own example is an adjusted net income of £67,600, which is £7,600 over, 7,600 divided by 200 is 38, so 38% of the Child Benefit comes back as tax.

Adjusted net income is total taxable income including savings interest and dividends, before the personal allowance, less certain reliefs, chiefly pension contributions and Gift Aid. It is measured on the individual, not the household, so two parents on £55,000 each pay nothing while one on £65,000 with a non-earning partner pays 25%.

2. The PAYE route and the 31 January 2027 deadline

GOV.UK now lists two ways to pay: through PAYE or through Self Assessment. The PAYE route is a registration service; once registered, HMRC adjusts your tax code and, in its words, "you will not have to do anything else unless your circumstances change". You can only use it if all three conditions hold: you do not need to send a tax return for another reason; you are paying the charge for the current or previous tax year; and it is on or before 31 January after the tax year you are paying for. GOV.UK's example is exact: "You need to pay the tax charge for the 2025 to 2026 tax year. If it's on or before 31 January 2027, you can pay through PAYE."

That makes 31 January 2027 a fork for anyone who first went over £60,000 in 2025/26. Register before it and the 2025/26 charge is collected through your tax code, no return required. Miss it and GOV.UK's other example applies: "If it's after 31 January 2027, you must make the payment through Self Assessment", which means registering for Self Assessment, a return, and the late-filing penalties if the return itself is late. People who already file a return only because of the charge can switch: GOV.UK says to phone HMRC to leave Self Assessment and register for PAYE collection.

3. The pension lever

Because the test is adjusted net income, and pension contributions come off before it is measured, a pension contribution reduces the charge and, if it takes you under £60,000, removes it. At £65,000 with two children, a £5,000 gross pension contribution (£4,000 from your pay, £1,000 basic-rate relief added by the scheme) takes adjusted net income to £60,000, saves the £584.35 charge, and attracts higher-rate relief on the contribution as well, because £60,000 to £65,000 is taxed at 40%. Add the £1,000 of higher-rate relief claimed back and the £584.35 charge avoided, and £5,000 in the pension has cost about £2,416, which is why accountants describe the £60,000 to £80,000 band as one of the highest marginal rates in the system. Salary sacrifice does the same job through the payroll and cuts National Insurance too. The same income test decides other family support too, which is why it is worth reading Tax-Free Childcare set against the 30 free hours.

4. What the PAYE route does not do

It does not reduce the charge. The same percentage is collected, just through the tax code rather than a January bill. It does not work if you have any other reason to file: self-employment income over £1,000, rental income, untaxed savings interest above the allowance, or a capital gain to report all push you into a return, and the charge goes in it. It does not shift the charge to the partner who claims Child Benefit; it stays with the higher earner even if that person never sees the money. And it is not the same as opting out. Opting out of the payments removes the charge entirely, but GOV.UK is explicit that the claim should stay registered so the claimant keeps National Insurance credits towards their State Pension, which is the trap that caught thousands of stay-at-home parents a decade ago. If you are near £80,000, opting out of payments while keeping the claim is usually the clean answer; between £60,000 and £80,000, keeping the money and paying the percentage leaves you better off. If the household has since separated, this charge and maintenance are two different bills, and the fees the Child Maintenance Service takes off both parents come out before anyone sees the money.

5. A worked example in Huddersfield

Nadeem in Huddersfield earns £67,600 as a project manager, PAYE only, and his partner Farah claims Child Benefit for their two children. In 2025/26 his salary went over £60,000 for the first time. On GOV.UK's own example figure the charge is 38% of the family's Child Benefit. At the 2026/27 rates used here for illustration that is £888.21 a year; his actual 2025/26 charge is 38% of the 2025/26 Child Benefit they received.

He has two doors. Register for the PAYE route before 31 January 2027, and HMRC collects the 2025/26 charge through his tax code, about £74 a month spread over a year, with the current year's charge following the same way. Or do nothing until February 2027, at which point he must register for Self Assessment, file a 2025/26 return, and pay the charge with it. The first door is 20 minutes online; the second is a permanent annual job. Separately, if he raised his pension contribution by £7,600 gross, about £6,080 from his pay, his adjusted net income would drop to £60,000 and the charge would vanish. With £3,040 of tax relief in total and £888 of charge avoided, £7,600 in his pension costs him about £3,672 net.

6. The honest verdict

The High Income Child Benefit Charge takes 1% of Child Benefit for every £200 of adjusted net income over £60,000, and since 2025/26 an employee with no other reason to file can pay it through their tax code. The catch is the date: for the 2025/26 charge, register on or before 31 January 2027 or you are in Self Assessment whether you like it or not.

The PAYE route moves the bill; it does not shrink it. A pension contribution that takes you under £60,000 shrinks it to nothing, and picks up 40% tax relief on the way. Keep the Child Benefit claim registered whatever you do, for the National Insurance credits.

Frequently asked

At what income does the Child Benefit charge start? When the higher earner's adjusted net income is over £60,000. It takes 1% of Child Benefit for every £200 over, and 100% at £80,000 or more. Source: GOV.UK, read 5 September 2026.

Can I pay it without a tax return? Yes, through PAYE, if you have no other reason to file, you are paying for the current or previous tax year, and it is on or before 31 January after that tax year. For 2025/26 that is 31 January 2027.

Does paying through PAYE make it cheaper? No. The same charge is collected through your tax code instead of a January bill. Only reducing adjusted net income, for example with pension contributions, reduces the charge.

Should we just stop claiming Child Benefit? Opt out of the payments if you are at £80,000 or more, but keep the claim registered so the claimant gets National Insurance credits. Between £60,000 and £80,000 you keep some of the money and are better off claiming.
