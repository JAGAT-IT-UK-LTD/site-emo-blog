1. How UK cashback cards actually work

A cashback credit card pays you back a small percentage of what you spend on it. You buy your shopping, your fuel, your train tickets, the last of which is often cheaper still through an employer season ticket loan than on any card, and at some point the card gives you a slice of that money back, either as a statement credit, an annual lump sum, or points you convert. Points are the fiddliest of the three, because a point is only worth what the scheme lets you redeem it for, and what a Clubcard or Nectar point is actually worth in pence is rarely the number the adverts imply.

The money does not come from nowhere. When a purchase goes wrong rather than right, the protection attached to the card matters far more than its rate, and Section 75 and chargeback cover different things. Every time you pay by card, the shop pays a fee to the card network. The card issuer keeps some of it and hands a little back to you to keep you using their card rather than someone else's. That is the entire business model, and understanding it tells you something important: the issuer will never pay you more than they make from you. Worth knowing before you choose on rate alone: paying by card also carries statutory protection, and it sits alongside your 30-day right to reject a faulty item, which applies whatever you paid with.

So the game is not finding a card that is generous. It is finding the card whose particular rules happen to fit the way you already spend, and then paying it off in full every single month. The downside case is worth knowing before you start: a small unpaid card debt can end up secured against your home, which costs far more than any cashback rate pays.

A cashback card is not a way to make money. It is a small discount on money you were going to spend anyway.

2. The four things that decide your real rate

Two people with the same card can end up with wildly different returns. These four factors are why.

Caps

Most UK cards advertise a high introductory rate that applies only for the first few months, or only up to a spending limit. After that you drop to the standard rate, which is usually far lower. A card advertising 5% may pay that on your first few hundred pounds and then settle to well under 1%. None of it counts for anything if the balance is not cleared in full each month, because card interest swallows the cashback several times over, and where a balance has stopped being clearable at all the formal routes are worth understanding early: how a debt relief order compares with bankruptcy. A cap also changes how you should think about one big planned purchase: the cashback on a £900 phone is often capped at a couple of pounds, so what buying a refurbished handset instead of a new one actually saves is worth far more than the card rate.

Excluded spending

This is the one that catches people out. Many cards pay nothing on the categories where you spend the most: rent, mortgage, council tax, utility direct debits, savings transfers, and anything treated as a cash-equivalent transaction. If half your monthly outgoings fall into those buckets, half your spending earns nothing. Subscriptions are the category worth looking at twice, because where you sign up decides whether the charge reaches your card at all. The CMA has been forcing Apple and Google to let apps point customers to cheaper direct billing, and the new steering rules on app store subscriptions mean the same service can now cost less and earn cashback if you buy it on the web rather than in the app. The same gap costs people more away from the card: a lot of everyday admin is cheap or free direct, and which driving licence fees you should and should not be paying is a short list worth knowing before a copycat site charges you for it. If you are weighing a balance transfer alongside the cashback, read what the Bank Rate decision on 5 November could do to card and loan pricing before you apply.

Annual fee

Premium cashback cards charge a yearly fee and pay a higher rate. That maths only works above a certain level of spending. Below it you are paying for the privilege of earning less than a free card would have given you.

Your actual monthly spend

The single biggest factor, and the one the adverts assume is far higher than it usually is. A card paying 1% on £500 a month returns £60 a year. The same card on £1,500 a month returns £180. Nothing about the card changed. Putting everything on one card raises a second question, which is who repays you if a payment is pushed to a fraudster, and the APP fraud reimbursement rules and what you are owed covers that.

3. Work out your own number in 5 minutes

Open your banking app and follow these four steps. This is more useful than any best-buy table, because it uses your life rather than an average.

  1. Take one normal month of spending, not a holiday month or Christmas.
  2. Strike out everything a card will not pay on: rent or mortgage, council tax, any direct debit the card excludes, cash withdrawals, and money moved between your own accounts. What is left is your eligible spend.
  3. Multiply eligible spend by the card's standard rate, not the intro rate. Then multiply by twelve.
  4. Subtract the annual fee. The number you are left with is what that card is genuinely worth to you in year one.

Do this for two or three cards and the winner is usually obvious, and often it is not the one with the loudest headline rate.

Worked example: Sarah from Leeds

Sarah spends around £2,400 a month in total. That sounds like a lot of cashback until she strikes out the ineligible parts: £950 rent, £180 council tax, £140 of utility direct debits her card excludes, and £200 she moves into savings. That removes £1,470.

Her eligible spend is roughly £930 a month, or about £11,160 a year. On a fee-free card paying a standard 0.5%, that is about £56 a year. On a premium card paying 1% with a £30 annual fee, it is about £82. The premium card wins, but by £26, not by the hundreds the advert implied. If the alternative at the checkout is paying in three instalments, the FCA rules that took over buy now pay later in July 2026 changed what protection comes with it.

Sarah's real lesson was not which card to pick. It was that her cashback for the entire year was worth less than one month of the interest she would pay if she ever let the balance roll over. The other way a small percentage gets wiped out is a subscription you no longer need. A printer ink plan is the classic, charged monthly whether you print or not, and working out whether an Instant Ink plan beats buying cartridges will settle more money in one evening than a year of chasing a better cashback rate.

4. When an annual fee is worth paying

There is a simple break-even test. Divide the annual fee by the extra percentage the paid card gives you over the best free card. That tells you how much you need to spend before the fee pays for itself.

0.5%£25£5,000 a year
0.5%£50£10,000 a year
1.0%£50£5,000 a year
1.0%£120£12,000 a year

Remember these thresholds are for eligible spending only. If a fee card needs £10,000 of qualifying spend a year and your eligible spend is £8,000, the free card is the better card for you, no matter what the comparison sites rank first. If you want a one-off win rather than a percentage, the cash bonuses banks pay for switching your current account will usually beat a year of cashback.

5. The traps that quietly erase your cashback

  1. Carrying a balance. Credit card interest is measured in tens of percent. Cashback is measured in fractions of one percent. Carry a balance for a single month and the interest can eat a year of rewards.
  2. The intro rate expiry. Many people apply during a high introductory rate, forget the date it ends, and spend the next two years on a rate they would never have signed up for.
  3. Cash withdrawals. Taking cash on a credit card usually earns no cashback, attracts a fee, and starts charging interest immediately with no grace period.
  4. Minimum payment autopilot. Setting a direct debit for the minimum feels responsible and is the most expensive habit in personal finance. Set it to clear the full balance.
  5. Spending more because it is rewarded. Earning 1% back on something you did not need is a 99% loss.
  6. Annual fee on autopilot. Fees renew silently. Check once a year whether your spending still justifies it.

6. Who should skip cashback cards entirely

Being honest about this matters more than any recommendation.

If you sometimes carry a balance, stop reading about cashback and look at 0% or low-rate cards instead. The saving there will be many times larger than any reward. If your borrowing already sits elsewhere, deal with that first: for anyone with a car on finance, where the paused motor finance redress scheme now stands is the more valuable half hour.

If your credit file is thin or recently damaged, applications for premium reward cards are likely to be declined, and each rejected application leaves a mark. Build the file first. You can see your credit file for free and check what a lender will see before you apply. If the damage there came from an unpaid debt rather than a late payment, look at what the creditor can do next, because a small debt can end up secured against your home long after you stopped hearing about it.

If most of your outgoings are rent, bills and direct debits, your eligible spend may be so small that the whole exercise returns a few pounds a month. Your energy is better spent on switching your bills, where the savings are in the hundreds. One thing worth knowing before you apply: the score these cards are advertised against is not the number a lender ever looks at, which our guide to free credit score services explains.

If you find it hard to stop spending when a card is available, a debit card and a plan will beat any cashback rate. That is not a judgement, it is just an honest cost-benefit call.

7. How to stack cashback on top of cashback

The people who genuinely get good returns are not using one clever card. They are layering.

The first layer is the card itself, and applying for one leaves a hard search on your file, so it is worth checking your credit file free before you apply rather than after. The second is a cashback site: you click through to the retailer from the cashback site, then pay with your card. That way one purchase earns twice, once from the retailer's referral and once from your card, and neither knows or cares about the other. A supermarket scheme is only worth joining once you know what a Clubcard point and a Nectar point are actually worth. Supermarket loyalty schemes are a fourth layer, and they are not equal: a Tesco Clubcard point is worth 1p and a Nectar point 0.5p, as our Clubcard versus Nectar comparison works through. The point-by-point comparison of the two schemes shows where that gap actually costs you.

The third layer is timing, buying in a sale period or when the cashback site is running a boosted rate. Stacked together, a £400 purchase can return meaningfully more than the card alone would ever have paid. None of that stacking changes your position if the thing arrives faulty: the 30 day right to reject under the Consumer Rights Act applies to sale items too, and it is separate from anything the card gives you.

8. The honest verdict

A cashback credit card is worth having if, and only if, three things are true: you clear the balance in full every month, a decent share of your spending is eligible, and you have checked the standard rate rather than the headline one.

Get those three right and you can expect somewhere between £30 and £150 a year for almost no effort. That is a real, if modest, win. Get any one of them wrong and the card will cost you more than it pays, quietly, over years.

Run the five-minute calculation above before you apply. It is the difference between choosing a card and being chosen by one.

Frequently asked

Does applying hurt my credit score? A full application leaves a mark on your file. Use eligibility checkers, which use a soft search, before applying, and avoid several applications in a short window. That window matters most when a mortgage decision is close, which is the whole question behind fixing now or waiting.

Is cashback taxable? Cashback earned on your own spending is generally treated as a discount rather than income, so it is not usually taxed. Referral rewards are a different matter, as covered in our side hustle tax guide. Interest on the savings you park the cashback in is different again: the Personal Savings Allowance has been frozen at £1,000 since 2016, and HMRC collects the tax through your PAYE code.

Can I use a cashback card abroad? You can, but many charge a foreign transaction fee that is larger than the cashback you earn. For travel, a dedicated fee-free travel card usually wins.
