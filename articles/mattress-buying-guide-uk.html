1. What does a mattress really cost per night?

This mattress buying guide for the UK starts with one sum. Most things you buy are used for an hour a day. A mattress is used for seven or eight hours, every night, for the best part of a decade.

Eight hours a night is 2,920 hours a year. That is close to 3,000 hours, more than almost anything else in your home.

Run the cost that way and the numbers change. A £500 mattress that lasts eight years costs about 17 pence a night.

A £250 one that sags after three years costs about 23 pence a night. You also spent three years sleeping badly to save the difference.

That is the whole case for spending properly once. It is not about luxury. It is about not paying twice.

A cheap mattress is rarely cheaper. It just moves the cost from this year to the year you replace it, and charges you interest in bad sleep.

2. How much should you spend on a mattress in the UK?

For most adults, a double or king in the £400 to £700 band is the sweet spot. Here is what each price band tends to buy.

Below about £300 you are gambling on how quickly it sags. Above about £900 the gain per pound falls fast, unless you have a clear reason to pay more.

Whatever you pay, aim for a mattress you will keep for years. Which? suggests replacing one every eight to 10 years, and its durability tests are built to mimic that span of use.

3. Why does the trial period beat every spec?

You cannot tell whether a mattress suits you by lying on it in a shop for four minutes with your coat on. Nobody can.

That is why the sleep trial is the most valuable line in the contract. Treat 90 nights as the minimum.

When we checked on 23 November 2026, Simba advertised a 200 night trial, Emma "up to 200 nights" and Nectar a 365 night home trial. That is the difference between a decision and a gamble.

What does the law give you anyway?

Buy online and you usually have 14 days from delivery to cancel, then another 14 days to send it back. GOV.UK sets this out for sellers.

There is a hygiene exception for sealed items that cannot be returned once opened. That is why a brand's own trial matters so much for a mattress.

A faulty mattress is different again. Your rights under the Consumer Rights Act are explained in our guide to the 30 day right to reject.

Three things to check in the trial small print

  1. Is collection free? Some trials look generous until you try to send the mattress back and find a collection fee.
  2. Is there a minimum period first? Many brands ask you to sleep on it for a set number of nights before a return. That is fair, but know the number.
  3. Do you need a protector? Some trials and warranties require one. A stained mattress can void both.

Note the warranty too, but do not confuse it with the trial. A long warranty usually covers manufacturing faults and serious sagging, not "I do not like it".

4. Which firmness do you need?

People choose firmness by what felt nice in a showroom. The better way is to choose by how you sleep.

  • Side sleepers generally need a softer surface. Shoulders and hips must sink in for the spine to stay straight.
  • Back sleepers usually want medium to medium firm. That keeps the lower back from dipping.
  • Front sleepers generally need firmer. A soft mattress lets the hips drop and arches the lower back.
  • Couples who sleep differently should look at a medium firm hybrid, or a zoned mattress, rather than one extreme.

Body weight matters too. A heavier sleeper sinks further, so one person's "medium" feels soft to another.

If you and your partner differ a lot in weight, edge support and zoning are worth paying for.

The 4 to 6 week rule

A new mattress often feels wrong for the first fortnight. That is especially true if your old one had a dip you had moulded to.

Give any new mattress at least four weeks, ideally six, before you judge it. This is exactly why a 90 night trial is the minimum worth accepting.

5. Foam, pocket sprung or hybrid: which is best?

All foam. Memory foam or similar. Very good at absorbing movement, so it suits you if your partner turns a lot. It can sleep warm, and cheaper foams soften faster.

Pocket sprung. Individual springs in fabric pockets. Breathable and supportive, and often better for heavier sleepers.

Hybrid. Pocket springs with foam or latex layers on top. This is where most of the sensible money goes, because it mixes airflow with pressure relief.

Open coil. One connected spring unit. Cheap, and the type most likely to sag in the middle. Fine for a guest bed, not for nightly use for years.

Bed in a box. Not a type, just a way of delivering foam or hybrid mattresses rolled up. Allow a day or more for it to expand fully before you judge it.

6. What is pure marketing?

Enormous spring counts. Past a point, more springs simply means thinner springs. It is a number for the advert, not for your back.

Permanent discounts. Online mattress prices are nearly always "reduced". If the sale never ends, the sale price is the real price.

Orthopaedic and medical sounding names. These are not the protected terms people assume. Often "orthopaedic" just means firm. If you have a real back condition, ask a GP or physiotherapist.

Cooling gel claims. A little help at the surface, rarely the change described. Airflow from springs and a breathable cover do more.

7. Should you buy a mattress on Black Friday?

Black Friday falls on 27 November 2026. Mattress brands push hard around it. On 23 November, Simba's own site was already running a Black Friday sale of "up to 33% off".

A Black Friday price can be a fair deal. It is only a good deal if it beats what the same mattress cost over the past few months.

So check the price history, compare the same size and model, and judge only what you pay. Our guide on how to spot fake Black Friday discounts walks through the checks.

The trial and collection terms should be the same in a sale. If they are worse, the discount is costing you more than it saves.

Worked example: Dan and Aisha replace a 12 year old bed

Their old mattress has a visible dip and Dan wakes with lower back ache. They have £600 and are tempted by a £250 special offer that would leave money for a new frame.

They run the per night sum instead. The £250 mattress, realistically lasting 4 years, is £250 divided by 1,460 nights, about 17p a night.

A £550 hybrid lasting 9 years is £550 divided by 3,285 nights, also about 17p a night, for a much better bed. The cheap option saves nothing over time. It only delays the spending.

They choose the £550 hybrid. It is medium firm, because Aisha sleeps on her side and Dan on his back. The retailer gives a 200 night trial with free collection.

Week two is uncomfortable and they nearly send it back. They stick to the six week rule, and the trial still has well over 150 nights left when they decide to keep it.

The frame waits until next year, paid for partly with the £50 left over. The mattress affects 3,000 hours a year. The frame mostly affects how the room looks.

8. When should you not buy a new mattress?

If the mattress is young and the problem is the base. A sagging divan or broken slats will ruin a good mattress. Check the base first, it is far cheaper to fix.

If you have not tried rotating it. Many mattresses need turning end to end every few months, especially in the first year. Some "worn out" mattresses are just settled unevenly.

If money is tight this month. A topper can buy a better year while you save. It is a delay, not a fix, but an honest one.

If the bedroom is cold rather than the bed uncomfortable. Warmth matters for sleep too. Our guide to an electric blanket versus heating the bedroom shows the cheaper way to stay warm.

If you would put it on a credit card you cannot clear. Interest will cost more than the upgrade, as covered in our cashback card guide.

9. The honest verdict

Spend £400 to £700 on a hybrid. Choose firmness by how you sleep, not how it feels in a shop. Insist on a trial of at least 90 nights with free collection.

That mix gets most UK buyers a bed they can keep for the eight to 10 years Which? suggests.

Ignore spring counts, ignore permanent sales, and ignore anything called orthopaedic unless a clinician said the word first.

And whatever you buy, give it six weeks. The most common mistake is not buying the wrong mattress. It is returning the right one too early.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on a mattress in the UK?

For a double or king used every night, £400 to £700 is the sensible range for most adults. That usually buys a decent hybrid that lasts. Spend less and it may sag early. Spend more only for a clear reason, such as a heavier build, and compare the cost per night, not the headline price.

How often should you replace a mattress?

Which? recommends replacing a mattress every eight to 10 years. Condition matters more than age, though. A visible dip, waking stiff, or sleeping better in a hotel bed are the real signs. A mattress that still supports you evenly at nine years does not need replacing just because of its birthday.

Can I return a mattress I simply do not like?

Usually, if you are inside the brand's sleep trial. Online orders also carry a 14 day legal right to cancel from delivery. GOV.UK notes an exception for sealed items that cannot be returned for hygiene reasons once opened, which is why the trial's own terms matter so much. Read them before you buy.

Is Black Friday a good time to buy a mattress?

It can be, but only if the price beats the recent past. Black Friday is 27 November 2026, and many mattress brands run sales for weeks around it. Compare the same model and size over several months. If the trial or collection terms are worse in the sale, walk away.

Does a topper fix a sagging mattress?

No. A topper changes how the surface feels. It cannot restore support that has collapsed underneath, so the dip will still pull your back out of line. A topper is a sensible stopgap for a few months while you save, but it is not a repair and will not add years to the mattress.
