The short version

  • The Flat Rate Scheme is for turnover up to £150,000 excluding VAT, not the £90,000 registration threshold. The two numbers are constantly confused.
  • You apply your flat rate to VAT-inclusive turnover, not to your net sales. That is why a 14.5% flat rate is not the same as keeping 5.5% of your VAT.
  • If you spend little on goods you are a “limited cost business” and pay 16.5%, which on the arithmetic below leaves almost nothing on the table. For most consultants and one-person service firms, that is the answer.
  • There is a 1% discount in your first year of VAT registration, worth £1,200 on a £120,000 VAT-inclusive turnover.
  • You give up input VAT to get it, except on capital assets over £2,000. So the scheme pays until your VATable purchases cross a break-even point, and every break-even is in the table below.

1. What the scheme actually does

Under normal VAT accounting you charge VAT on sales, reclaim VAT on purchases, and pay HMRC the difference. Under the Flat Rate Scheme you still charge your customers 20%, but you pay HMRC a fixed percentage of your VAT-inclusive turnover and keep the rest. In exchange, you give up the right to reclaim VAT on purchases, other than capital assets costing more than £2,000.

The eligibility limit is turnover of £150,000 or less excluding VAT. This is a different number from the £90,000 compulsory registration threshold, and mixing them up is the single most common error in write-ups of this scheme.

GOV.UK gives the calculation directly. Bill a customer £1,000 plus 20% VAT to make £1,200. A photographer’s flat rate is 11%, so the payment to HMRC is 11% of £1,200, which is £132. Not 11% of £1,000.

2. The limited cost trap

You are classed as a limited cost business if your spending on goods is less than either 2% of turnover, or £1,000 a year where your costs are more than 2%. If you are, your flat rate is 16.5% regardless of what you do for a living.

Note the word goods. Services do not count. Neither do most of the things a one-person consultancy actually buys: software subscriptions, accountancy, rent, training, travel, telephone. A consultant working from a laptop is very likely to be a limited cost business, which is exactly who the rule was introduced to catch.

At 16.5% of VAT-inclusive turnover, you hand over 19.8% of your net sales. The 20% you charged is 20%. There is almost nothing left.

If your flat rate is 16.5%, the scheme is not a saving with extra admin. It is extra admin.

3. The comparison, on £100,000 of net sales

Here is the whole decision in one table. Net turnover £100,000, so VAT-inclusive turnover is £120,000 and output VAT is £20,000. The break-even column is the level of VATable purchases at which standard accounting starts to beat the flat rate.

Read the last two columns together, because on their own each one misleads.

The builder on 9.5% appears to gain £8,600, the largest figure in the table after retail. But a general builder buying £43,000 of materials a year is entirely normal, so the break-even is realistic rather than theoretical, and many builders will be better off on standard accounting. The retailer on 7.5% is in the same position only more so, because stock is the whole business.

The consultant on 14.5% appears to gain only £2,600. But a consultant with £13,000 of VATable purchases a year is unusual, so that £2,600 is much more likely to be real money. The catch for that consultant is the row above: low spending on goods is exactly what makes you a limited cost business, and then the rate is 16.5% and the gain collapses to £200.

4. What it saves besides money

The flat rate scheme removes the need to track and evidence input VAT on every purchase. For a one-person business that is real time, and for some people it is the actual reason to join rather than the margin.

Be honest about the size of that saving though. If you already use bookkeeping software that categorises purchases automatically, the admin difference is small. The scheme is most valuable to someone doing their own books by hand.

Worked example: Nadia in Coventry

Nadia is an IT consultant. Net sales £100,000, so VAT-inclusive turnover is £120,000. Her VATable purchases last year came to £4,800, mostly software and her accountant.

Standard accounting. Output VAT £20,000, input VAT reclaimed £800, so she pays HMRC £19,200.

Flat rate at 14.5%. 14.5% of £120,000 is £17,400. On the face of it she is £1,800 a year better off.

The check that changes the answer. Almost none of that £4,800 is spent on goods. Software licences and accountancy are services. Her goods spend is under £1,000 and under 2% of turnover, so she is a limited cost business and her rate is 16.5%, not 14.5%.

Recalculated. 16.5% of £120,000 is £19,800, against £19,200 under standard accounting. The flat rate scheme would cost her £600 a year.

Her decision. Stay on standard accounting. The one exception worth noting: in her first year of registration the 1% discount would take her to 15.5%, or £18,600, which beats standard by £600 for that year only. A twelve month gain of £600 followed by a £600 annual loss is not a scheme to build a business around.

5. Frequently asked

Is the limit £90,000 or £150,000? Both, for different things. You must register for VAT once taxable turnover passes £90,000 in any rolling 12 months. You may join the Flat Rate Scheme if your VAT turnover is £150,000 or less excluding VAT.

Can I still reclaim VAT on a laptop? Only if it is a capital asset costing more than £2,000 including VAT. A £1,400 laptop is not reclaimable under the scheme. A £2,400 one is.

Do I still charge my customers 20%? Yes. The flat rate changes what you pay HMRC, not what you charge. Your invoices look identical.

What if my business type is not on the list? There is a catch-all, “any other activity not listed elsewhere”, at 12%. Choosing your category honestly matters, because HMRC can challenge a category that does not reflect what you actually do.

6. The honest verdict

Work out whether you are a limited cost business first, before looking at anything else. If you are, the rate is 16.5% and the scheme is worth about £200 a year on £100,000 of sales, which is not worth the risk of getting the category wrong.

If you are not a limited cost business, compare your flat rate against your actual VATable purchases using the break-even column above. The scheme pays when your purchases are below the break-even and costs you when they are above it. Most service businesses sit comfortably below. Most builders and retailers sit above.

One thing everyone should do: if you are newly VAT registered, check the 1% first-year discount. It is the single best year the scheme will ever have, and plenty of people never claim it.
