Waiting for the Bank did not get anyone a better fixed rate. Here is what happened, and what to do now

1. What the September and November decisions actually moved

When this guide was first published, Bank Rate was 3.75% and the next decision was due on Thursday 17 September 2026. The vote in July had been 6 to 3 to hold, with three members wanting a rise to 4%.

On 17 September the Committee held again, by the same 6 to 3. August inflation had come in at 3.1% the day before. Then on 5 November it raised Bank Rate by 0.25 points to 4%, by 8 to 1, after inflation reached 3.5% in September.

Here is what each decision did to you, depending on your mortgage:

  • If you are on a tracker, your rate is contractually linked to Bank Rate. The September hold changed nothing. The November rise feeds through to your payment, usually within a month or two depending on your lender's terms.
  • If you are on a standard variable rate, your lender decides whether to pass a change on, and by how much. It is not automatic, though in practice most do pass on rises.
  • If you are on a fix, nothing happened on either date. Your rate is your rate until your deal ends.
  • If you were shopping for a new fix, rates rose through the autumn, before and after the September hold.

That last point is the one that cost people money, so it deserves its own section.

2. Why fixed rates were already priced

A lender offering you a five year fix has to fund that promise for five years. It does that in the money markets, at a price driven by what those markets collectively expect Bank Rate to average over the period. That expectation is a live, continuously updating number.

This autumn showed it working. Bank Rate did not move in September, but fixed rates kept climbing because markets expected a rise. Moneyfacts says many lenders pre-empted the November decision when setting their fixed prices. The Bank's November Monetary Policy Report found quoted rates on two year fixes at 75% and 90% loan to value up by around 60 basis points on average since July.

So anyone waiting for the September announcement in the hope of a better deal got the opposite. By the time a decision is announced, the outcome the market expects is already in the price. Fixes move when the Committee, or its language about what comes next, surprises the market. Nobody at a kitchen table can forecast a surprise.

The same applies now. The Bank's Report shows the market curve rising to around 4.9% at the end of 2027. That expectation is already in the fixes you are quoted today.

3. The free option most borrowers do not use

Most UK lenders will let you reserve a new fixed rate somewhere around six months before your current deal ends. The important part is what happens next: with most lenders, if rates fall between the day you reserve and the day the new deal starts, you can switch to the cheaper product without penalty.

Read that again, because it converts an agonising forecast into a free option. You lock in a ceiling today and keep the upside if rates improve. The cost of using it is an application and some paperwork. The cost of not using it is that you find out what rates are on the day your deal expires, with no room to move.

Two things to check before you rely on it, because terms vary by lender: whether the switch to a lower rate is allowed and up to what point in the process, and whether any product fee is refundable if you do not complete. Ask those two questions in writing.

4. Worked example: Owen and Marta in Chesterfield

Owen and Marta have £180,000 left on their mortgage with 22 years to run. Their two year fix ends this November. In mid September they were deciding whether to reserve something or wait for the decision. Here is what the choice turned out to be worth, using Moneyfacts averages and a repayment basis over 22 years:

RateMonthly payment
4.68%, average two year tracker, 1 November£1,093.23
5.59%, average two year fix, start of September£1,186.31
5.97%, average two year fix, 1 November£1,226.34
7.13%, average standard variable rate, 1 November£1,352.61

The gap between the two fixes is £40.03 a month. Over a year that is about £480, and over a two year fix about £961. On a five year fix the averages went from 5.63% to 6.00%, which is £39.04 a month, about £2,342 across the five years. Those are our calculations on market averages, not quotes from any lender.

If Owen and Marta reserved a rate in September and kept the right to switch, they kept the lower rate and lost nothing. If they waited for the decision, they shopped in a market that kept rising: the average two year fix was 0.38 points higher on 1 November than at the start of September. The Bank held, and they still paid more.

If they did nothing at all, their deal rolls onto the lender's SVR. At the 7.13% average that is £126.26 a month more than an average fix today. Every month of drift costs more than three months of the rise they were trying to dodge.

A tracker at the 4.68% average would cost £133.12 a month less than a fix today. That is a real option, but it moves with Bank Rate. Another quarter point rise would add about £25 a month on their loan.

There is one exception worth naming. If a deal does not end for another eight or nine months, it is outside most lenders' reservation windows anyway, and there is nothing to decide yet. That is waiting because the door is not open, which is different from waiting for news.

5. How to actually decide

Work through these in order.

  1. Find your deal end date. It is on your mortgage offer or your annual statement. Everything else follows from it.
  2. If it is within six months, start now. Reserve a rate, confirm in writing whether you can switch down, and diarise the date the reservation expires.
  3. Work out what you would fall onto. Your lender's standard variable rate is the default if you do nothing, and it is almost always the most expensive option on the table. Knowing that number makes the decision less abstract.
  4. Decide the term on your own circumstances, not on a forecast. A longer fix buys certainty and costs flexibility. If you might move, or repay a lump sum, early repayment charges over a long fix are a real cost.
  5. Check the fee against the rate. A lower rate with a 1,499 pound fee can be worse than a higher rate with no fee on a smaller balance. Compare total cost over the fixed period, not the headline rate.

If the payment itself is what is worrying you, the broader squeeze is not only about the mortgage rate. Income tax thresholds have been frozen for years, which quietly raises the tax on every pay rise, and we have set out what frozen tax thresholds are doing to take home pay separately.

The honest verdict

This autumn settled the argument. The Bank held in September, and fixed rates rose anyway. It raised in November, and fixes had already moved. Anyone who waited for either announcement to choose a fix paid more for waiting.

If you are on a tracker or a standard variable rate, the 17 December decision matters to you and it is worth watching. If you are on a fix, nothing happens to you on that day. If your fix ends in the next six months, reserve a rate now, keep the right to switch to something cheaper, and get quotes from more than one lender. That is worth hundreds or thousands on a typical balance. The Monetary Policy Committee is not.

Frequently asked

Did fixed mortgage rates fall after the Bank of England held rates on 17 September? No. Moneyfacts records the average two year fix rising from 5.59% at the start of September to 5.97% on 1 November, as markets priced the rise that came on 5 November. Fixed rates follow expectations for the whole fixed period, not the decision on the day.

How early can I lock in a new fixed rate? Most UK lenders allow you to reserve a new rate roughly six months before your existing deal ends, though the exact window varies. Ask your lender for their figure rather than assuming it, and diarise the date the reservation offer expires so it does not lapse.

Can I switch to a cheaper rate if one appears after I have reserved? With most lenders, yes, up to a point in the process. It is not universal and it is not always up to the day of completion, so get the answer in writing from your specific lender before you rely on it.

Is it worth paying a product fee to get a lower rate? It depends entirely on your balance. On a large mortgage the interest saving usually outweighs the fee. On a small remaining balance it often does not. Compare the total cost over the whole fixed term, including the fee, rather than comparing headline rates.
