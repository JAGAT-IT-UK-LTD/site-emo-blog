Bills Are Up 4% From October. The VAT Cut Is Hiding a 5.8% Rise in Electricity

Ruhul Amin · Published 26 August 2026 · 7 min read · Figures checked August 2026

Ofgem confirmed the October price cap this morning at £1,723 a year, up £60. That headline is accurate, and it is also the least useful number in the announcement. The unit rates underneath it tell a different story, and so does the fact that Ofgem quietly changed the size of the household it measures.

The short version

  • Confirmed by Ofgem on 26 August 2026: the cap rises 4% to £1,723 a year for a typical direct debit household, from 1 October to 31 December 2026. That is £60 a year, or £5 a month, on typical use.
  • The electricity unit rate rose 5.8% before tax. It looks like it barely moved, going from 26.11p to 26.32p per kWh, but the old figure included 5% VAT and the new one includes none. Strip the VAT out and July's rate was 24.87p.
  • Gas is doing the damage. The gas unit rate is up 8.7%, from 7.33p to 7.97p per kWh. Gas is now roughly half of a typical dual fuel bill.
  • £1,723 is not comparable to the numbers you remember. Ofgem shrank its typical household in July 2026. On the old 2023 yardstick, the very same rates come to £1,935.
  • Payment method is worth £138 a year. Direct debit £1,723, prepayment £1,678, paying on receipt of a bill £1,861.
  • Electricity-only homes are roughly flat. Below about 4,100 kWh a year you will pay slightly less than you did in July, above it slightly more. At 26.32p a unit the big electricity items are the ones worth re-checking — a hot tub on its own runs to several hundred pounds a year.

1. What Ofgem actually confirmed this morning

At 7am on Wednesday 26 August 2026, Ofgem confirmed the price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026. For a typical household paying by direct debit for both gas and electricity, the cap goes from £1,663 to £1,723 a year, a rise of 4%, or £5 a month. If the real problem is what is already owed rather than the next bill, the Ofgem 2026 debt relief scheme sets out whose arrears can be written off.

The other two payment methods, which get far less coverage:

  • Prepayment meter: £1,678 a year. Still the cheapest capped rate, about £45 a year below direct debit.
  • Paying on receipt of a bill: £1,861 a year. That is £138 more than direct debit for exactly the same energy.

Here are the direct debit rates that actually govern your bill, taken from Ofgem's published tables. These are the England, Scotland and Wales averages, and your region will differ:

Electricity. 26.11p per kWh and 57.19p a day, rising to 26.32p per kWh and 54.83p a day.

Gas. 7.33p per kWh and 29.04p a day, rising to 7.97p per kWh and 29.68p a day.

Electricity prices from 1 October include no VAT. Gas still carries 5%.

Ofgem attributes the rise to wholesale gas prices, which it says are up 11% over the past three months, driven by the ongoing conflict in the Middle East. Around 11 million households on fixed tariffs are unaffected. Standing charges and repairs are the two costs a fix does not touch, which is why boiler cover against paying for repairs yourself is worth pricing separately.

2. The VAT cut is hiding a 5.8% electricity rise

Look at the electricity unit rate again. 26.11p to 26.32p. That is a rise of eight tenths of one percent, and most coverage today will report the electricity side as broadly stable, because that is what Ofgem's press release says. That same unit rate is the number that decides whether charging a car at home beats charging it in public, so what an EV home charger costs to install only makes sense read next to it.

It is stable for you, this quarter. It is not stable underneath.

The 26.11p you paid in July included 5% VAT. The 26.32p you will pay in October includes none, because the government zero rated domestic electricity from 1 October 2026. So the like for like comparison is not 26.11 against 26.32. It is 24.87p against 26.32p. That same unit rate is what decides whether to swap the last halogen bulbs now or wait for each one to fail. Unit rates are what turn an appliance rating into money, and a C on the rescaled label is not the old C.

The pre tax price of electricity went up 5.8%. The VAT cut absorbed all of it and a little more.

The same thing happened to the standing charge. It appears to fall from 57.19p to 54.83p a day, which sounds like a genuine cut. Strip the VAT out of July's figure and it was 54.47p. The underlying standing charge went up, by 0.7%.

This matters for one specific reason. The electricity zero rate is scheduled to end on 31 March 2027. If 5% VAT comes back and sits on top of the new base rate, that 26.32p becomes 27.64p, and the standing charge becomes 57.57p. Nothing in the wholesale market has to change for your April bill to jump. The tax break simply stops covering a price rise that already happened. Cornwall Insight has already forecast the cap that follows this one, and we have set out what the January 2027 cap is expected to do and why that VAT date sits underneath it.

None of this is hidden or improper. Ofgem's own note says the cap would have been about £45 higher without the VAT change. But "electricity bills will remain broadly stable" and "the price of electricity did not rise" are two different statements, and only the first one is true.

3. Why £1,723 is not the number you remember

In July 2026, Ofgem changed the Typical Domestic Consumption Values, the assumed usage it multiplies the rates by to produce the headline figure. Households now use around 7% less electricity and 17% less gas than at the last review, so the typical household was reduced to roughly 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year.

Run the identical October rates against the old 2023 yardstick of 2,700 kWh and 11,500 kWh, and the cap comes out at £1,935, rising from £1,862. Ofgem publishes both figures in the notes to its press release, and our arithmetic against the published unit rates matches them to the pound.

So the percentage is honest. Four percent is four percent on either measure. What has changed is the absolute number people anchor to. If your house has not become 17% more gas efficient since 2023, £1,723 was never going to be your bill, and the gap between the headline and your reality just got wider by about £200. Some households get help without applying for it at all, and the rules on who receives the 150 pound Warm Home Discount automatically are worth reading before you ring anyone.

There is a second, more technical effect that Ofgem is upfront about. Lowering the assumed consumption pushes the unit rate up, because suppliers still have to recover certain fixed costs over fewer units. The yardstick is presentational. Its consequences are not.

4. Worked example: the Begum household in Watford

A three bedroom semi with gas central heating, two working adults and a teenager. Their smart meter says they used 3,100 kWh of electricity and 12,000 kWh of gas over the last twelve months. That is above typical, which is true of most family homes of that size.

At July rates: electricity 3,100 × 26.11p = £809.41, plus 365 days × 57.19p = £208.74. Gas 12,000 × 7.33p = £879.60, plus 365 × 29.04p = £106.00.
Annual total: £2,003.75

At October rates: electricity 3,100 × 26.32p = £815.92, plus 365 × 54.83p = £200.13. Gas 12,000 × 7.97p = £956.40, plus 365 × 29.68p = £108.33.
Annual total: £2,080.78

The increase is £77 a year, not £60. And their bill is £358 above the headline £1,723.

Two things to take from that. Their electricity cost barely moved, falling slightly, because the standing charge saving offset the unit rate rise. All £83 of their gas increase came from gas. And because roughly a third of a year's gas gets burned between October and December, more of that increase arrives in this quarter than in any other.

The crossover point is worth knowing if you have no gas at all. Because the electricity standing charge fell in cash terms while the unit rate rose, the two cancel out at about 4,100 kWh a year. Below that, your electricity costs slightly less than in July. Above it, slightly more. The quickest way for an all electric home to get below that line is hot water, where a cylinder on a timer beats an immersion left on all day. Either way the change is small, which is genuinely good news for heat pump and all electric homes after several bad years. The same unit rate is what makes summer cooling expensive: a portable air conditioner run through a hot spell costs far more than most people assume before they meter it. The headline figure is worked through in what the 4 percent rise to a 1,723 pound cap adds to a typical annual bill.

5. What is actually worth doing this week

Four things, in order of how much they are worth.

Submit a meter reading on or close to 30 September. Free, takes two minutes, and it stops your supplier estimating September usage at October rates. On the Begums' usage that is worth a few pounds, but it also prevents the far more annoying problem of a wrong opening balance you then have to argue about. If you have a smart meter the reading goes in on its own, which is one of the few differences a smart meter makes to what you are actually billed.

Check whether you are paying on receipt of a bill. That £138 a year gap is the single largest free saving in this announcement, and it is entirely within your control. Prepayment is cheaper still on the capped rates, though switching to it deliberately is rarely the right move for other reasons.

Look at fixed deals, with your own numbers. Ofgem says fixes are available at £100 or more below the October cap. That is a real saving if your usage resembles the typical household. If you use much more gas than typical, a fix priced against the cap is worth proportionally more to you. Compare unit rates and standing charges, not headline annual figures, and check exit fees. Compare it unit rate by unit rate rather than on the headline, which is the whole method in working out whether your fix is beating the cap or quietly losing to it.

Do the cheap draught proofing now, not in December. Gas is now about half of a typical dual fuel bill and it is the part that rose. Sealing a draughty door and putting foil behind radiators on an external wall costs very little and works on the largest line on your bill. If you rent, do the version that unclips when you leave, which is what belongs in an energy saving kit you can take with you to the next tenancy.

The honest verdict

A £5 a month rise is not a crisis, and for households with no gas it is close to nothing. That is the accurate headline and it deserves saying plainly rather than dramatising.

The two things worth carrying away are less comfortable. Electricity did not stay flat, it rose 5.8% before tax and a temporary VAT cut paid for it, which means 1 April 2027 is a date to watch. And £1,723 is measured against a smaller household than the one in your head, so if your usage has not fallen since 2023, budget against your own kWh rather than the headline.

There is nothing to buy here and no urgent decision to make today. Submit a reading before 30 September, get off billing on receipt if you are on it, and spend twenty pounds on draught proofing. That is the whole list.

Frequently asked

Is my bill capped at £1,723? No. The cap limits unit rates and standing charges, not your total. If you use more than 2,500 kWh of electricity and 9,500 kWh of gas a year, you will pay more than £1,723, and most family homes do.

Should I fix now? Only against your own consumption. Fixes at £100 or more below the cap are genuinely available, but "below the cap" is measured against a typical household. Take your annual kWh from your last bill, price it at both the capped rates above and the fix's rates, and compare the two totals. If the fix runs past 31 March 2027, remember electricity VAT may return during it.

Two dates decide whether waiting is worth it. Ofgem announces the January cap on 25 November, and what that announcement does and does not settle before you commit to a fix is the part worth reading first. The VAT position is split as well: gas stayed at 5% while electricity went to zero, so a gas-heavy home sees far less of the cut than the headline suggests.

Can I still get the £150 Warm Home Discount this winter? The qualifying date for winter 2026/27 was 23 August 2026, which has now passed. Eligibility is judged on whether you were receiving a qualifying benefit on that date, so if you were not, applying now will not help for this winter. The payment itself lands between November 2026 and 31 March 2027. The full qualifying rules are in our guide to the Warm Home Discount for winter 2026.

Does any of this apply in Northern Ireland? No. Ofgem's cap covers England, Scotland and Wales only. Northern Ireland has a separate regulator and market.
